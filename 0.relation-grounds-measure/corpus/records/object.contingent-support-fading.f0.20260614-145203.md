**statement (plain)** — for scaffolding to actually teach, two things must happen. First, support has to be *contingent*: the helper gives more control when the learner struggles and less when the learner succeeds, always tuned to the learner's current grasp. Second, support has to *fade*: as the learner gains competence, the helper withdraws, transferring responsibility for the task back to the learner. Fading only works if it is done contingently — pulled back in step with rising competence, not on a fixed schedule.

**statement** — the contemporary synthesis (van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen, 2010) characterizes scaffolding by three features: (a) **contingency** — adaptive support responsive to the learner's current level; (b) **fading** — the gradual decrease of support over time; and (c) **transfer of responsibility** — handing control of the task/learning to the learner, achievable through contingent fading. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — contingency is operationalized as the *degree of control* the helper exercises: increase control after a learner failure, decrease it after a success (the "contingent shift" rule). This calibration keeps support inside the [[object.zone-of-proximal-development]]. Fading and transfer of responsibility are the temporal counterpart: support is only temporary scaffolding (per [[object.instructional-scaffolding]]) if it is removed as competence grows, with effective transfer depending on the withdrawal being contingent rather than arbitrary.

**lineage** — the principle of **contingency** was introduced by Wood, Wood & Middleton, "An experimental evaluation of four face-to-face teaching strategies," *International Journal of Behavioral Development* 1(2) (1978):131–147 [[source.wood-wood-middleton-1978]], where the "contingent" tutoring approach produced the most active and efficient learners. The integration of **contingency + fading + transfer of responsibility** as the defining triad of scaffolding is the synthesis of van de Pol, Volman & Beishuizen, "Scaffolding in Teacher–Student Interaction: A Decade of Research," *Educational Psychology Review* 22(3) (2010):271–296 [[source.van-de-pol-2010]]. The fading/temporary-support intuition traces back to the original scaffolding metaphor of Wood, Bruner & Ross (1976) [[source.wood-bruner-ross-1976]]. **[confirmed]**
