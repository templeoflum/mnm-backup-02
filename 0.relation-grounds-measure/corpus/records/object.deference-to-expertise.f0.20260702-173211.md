**statement (plain)** — when a situation is fast, novel, or dangerous, the person who should make the call is the one who knows the most about *this problem, right now* — often someone junior and close to the work — not the person highest in the hierarchy. Authority is expected to move to expertise and move back afterward; the mark of the practice is that a low-ranking operator can halt a hazardous operation without first asking permission and without penalty for a good-faith wrong call.

**statement (framework)** — the fifth of the five HRO principles of collective mindfulness [[object.high-reliability-organization]] (Weick & Sutcliffe 2001). Its empirical anchor is the carrier flight deck (Rochlin, La Porte & Roberts 1987): "Even the lowest rating on the deck has not only the authority but the obligation to suspend flight operations immediately… he will not be penalized for being wrong." The subtlety stressed in the literature: "expert" means knowledge at the point of the problem, not rank — and this is how HROs are simultaneously centralized (in goals and the disciplined permission to proceed) and decentralized (in the authority to detect and stop).

**basis** — empirical, from the Berkeley HRO field studies [[source.rochlin-1987]], codified as a principle in [[source.weick-sutcliffe-2001]]. *Support* — adopted across aviation, healthcare, and incident command. *Limits* — depends on genuine psychological safety and management commitment; bolted onto a blame culture, the authority-migration does not happen.
