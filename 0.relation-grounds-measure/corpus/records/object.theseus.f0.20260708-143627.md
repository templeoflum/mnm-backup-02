**statement (plain)** — **Theseus** (Greek *Θησεύς*) is the national hero of Athens: son of **Aegeus**, king of Athens (or, in a parallel account, of the god **Poseidon**), and of Aethra of Troezen. Raised in Troezen, he recovers his father's tokens — a sword and sandals hidden beneath a rock — and travels to Athens by land, clearing the road of brigands (Periphetes, Sinis, Procrustes, and others). His central deed answers Athens's grim tribute to **Crete**: he volunteers among the youths and maidens sent to be devoured by the **Minotaur**, the bull-headed monster kept in the **Labyrinth** of Daedalus. **Ariadne**, daughter of King **Minos**, falls in love with him and gives him a **ball of thread** by which he retraces his way after slaying the Minotaur. He abandons Ariadne on **Naxos** on the voyage home, and — having forgotten to change his ship's **black sail** for a white one as a signal of success — causes his father Aegeus, seeing the black sail, to cast himself into the sea (thereafter the Aegean). As king he is credited with the **synoikismos**, the political unification of Attica under Athens.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: heroic quest, monster-slaying, kingship, civic founding. Parentage: Aegeus of Athens (mortal line) or Poseidon (divine line) — the double paternity is an attested feature, not an error. Key episodes: the tokens beneath the rock, the road-clearing labours, the **Minotaur** and the **thread of Ariadne**, the abandonment on Naxos, the fatal black sail, and the **synoikismos** of Attica. Later Athenian tradition attaches further exploits (the Amazons, the friendship with Peirithous, the descent to the underworld). The connected biography is fullest in **Plutarch's *Life of Theseus*** (not among the four cited sources — see report); the deeds are attested across the tragedians and Apollodorus. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested in Homer's epics [[source.homer]] and the tragedians, and systematized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]]; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The double paternity and the sequence of exploits are attested tradition, variable across sources, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
