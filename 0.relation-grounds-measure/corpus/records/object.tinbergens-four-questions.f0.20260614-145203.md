**statement (plain)** — to fully explain why an animal does something, you have to answer four separate questions, and they don't substitute for each other. Two are "proximate" (how it works in the individual right now): what mechanism causes it, and how it develops over a lifetime. Two are "ultimate" (why evolution produced it): what it's good for (survival value), and how it arose across evolutionary history. A complete account answers all four.

**statement** — Tinbergen's four questions / levels of analysis:
- **causation (mechanism)** — proximate: what stimuli and physiological machinery trigger and control the behavior.
- **ontogeny (development)** — proximate: how the behavior arises and changes over the individual's lifetime (maturation, learning).
- **survival value (function / adaptation)** — ultimate: how the behavior contributes to fitness in the current environment.
- **evolution (phylogeny)** — ultimate: the evolutionary history by which the behavior came to be.
Proximate = causation + ontogeny; ultimate = survival value + evolution. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — not a theorem but an organizing framework: it asserts that the four questions are logically distinct and mutually irreducible, so confusing them (e.g. answering "why" with a mechanism) is a category error. Its standing rests on six decades of use across ethology, behavioral ecology, and evolutionary psychology as the canonical checklist for a complete behavioral explanation. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — named and codified by [[source.tinbergen-1963]] (N. Tinbergen, "On Aims and Methods of Ethology," *Zeitschrift für Tierpsychologie* 20:410–433). Tinbergen built on Aristotle's modes of explanation and, more directly, on Julian Huxley's "three major problems of biology" (causation, survival value, evolution); Tinbergen's distinct contribution was adding **ontogeny** as a fourth, separating development from mechanism. The framework is the methodological backbone of the discipline founded with [[source.tinbergen-1951]] (*The Study of Instinct*) and the Lorenz–Tinbergen program. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — [[source.tinbergen-1963]] (the four questions, primary); [[source.tinbergen-1951]] (the discipline's foundational textbook). Huxley-priority on three of the four carried as lineage, not as a competing attribution.
