**statement (plain)** — The Pseudoscope Effect refers to the perceptual consequences of viewing the world through a pseudoscope, an optical device invented by Charles Wheatstone in 1852 that reverses binocular disparity by swapping the images presented to the left and right eyes. This reversal inverts depth relationships, causing near objects to appear far and far objects to appear near, effectively turning the three-dimensional world inside out. When viewing familiar objects through a pseudoscope, observers experience profound depth cue conflicts, as the reversed stereoscopic information contradicts monocular cues such as perspective, occlusion, shading, and prior knowledge about object structure.
