**statement (plain)** — **Thor** (Old Norse *Þórr*) is the thunder-god of the Aesir, son of **Odin**: the protector of gods and men, and the chief adversary of the giants (*jötnar*), whom he battles with his hammer **Mjollnir**. He drives a chariot drawn by goats across the sky, his hammer-strikes the thunder; his strength is the guard of Asgard and Midgard against the giant powers. He is a constant hunter and slayer of giants — fishing for the world-serpent **Jormungandr** in *Hymiskviða*, recovering his stolen hammer disguised as a bride in *Þrymskviða*. At **Ragnarök** he faces Jormungandr a final time: he slays the serpent but falls dead after nine steps, killed by its venom.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: thunder, storm, strength, protection, the warding of the cosmos against the giants. Key attributes: the hammer **Mjollnir**, the strength-belt *Megingjörð*, the iron gloves, the goat-drawn chariot. Germanic cognates: **Thunor** (Anglo-Saxon), **Donar** (continental Germanic) — from Proto-Germanic *\*Þun(a)raz* "thunder"; the Roman-era *interpretatio* identified him with **Jupiter** (hence Thursday / *Þunresdæg* ← *dies Iovis*). Loose cross-pantheon comparisons (Indra, Hercules) are made by analogy of function, not descent. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Norse mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested across the Poetic Edda [[source.poetic-edda]] and systematized in Snorri's Prose Edda [[source.prose-edda]]; the standard reference treatment is Lindow [[source.lindow-2001]]. The etymology (*\*Þunraz*) and the Jupiter *interpretatio* are philologically established; the connected biography owes much to Snorri's 13th-c. Christian framing and should be read as attested tradition, not neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
