**statement (plain)** — **Achilles** (Greek *Ἀχιλλεύς*) is the foremost of the Greek warriors at **Troy**: son of the mortal **Peleus** and the sea-nymph **Thetis**, and leader of the Myrmidons. His **wrath** (*mēnis*) — the opening word and announced theme of Homer's **Iliad** — is kindled when **Agamemnon** takes from him the captive Briseis; he withdraws from the fighting, and the Greeks suffer grievously in his absence. He returns to battle only after his beloved companion **Patroclus**, fighting in Achilles' own armour, is killed by the Trojan prince **Hector**; grief and rage drive Achilles back, and he slays Hector and drags his body before the walls, relenting at last to return it to old King Priam. The tradition of his single vulnerable point — the **heel** by which Thetis held him when dipping him for invulnerability — and his death from an **arrow** shot by **Paris** and guided by **Apollo** belong to the later, post-Homeric tradition; they are not narrated in the Iliad itself, which ends before his death.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: martial prowess, heroic wrath, honour (*timē*), mortality. Parentage: Peleus and the Nereid Thetis; his son is Neoptolemus. Key attributes: swiftness ("swift-footed," *podas ōkys*), the divine armour forged by **Hephaestus**, the immortal horses. The **Iliad** treats his wrath, withdrawal, and return, and closes with Hector's funeral; his own death, the **vulnerable heel**, and the arrow of Paris/Apollo are **later tradition** (the *Aethiopis* and subsequent sources, with Apollodorus' Bibliotheca among the summarizers) — not Homeric, a point to flag. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested pre-eminently in Homer's Iliad [[source.homer]], with the death-and-vulnerability material systematized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]] and the wider epic cycle; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The wrath-narrative is Homeric; the invulnerability and the fatal heel are attested later tradition, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
