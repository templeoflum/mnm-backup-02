**statement (plain)** — feedback is when a system's output is fed back to its own input, so the system's later behavior depends on its earlier behavior. In *negative* feedback the loop opposes change — it senses how far the output is from a target and acts to close that gap (a thermostat, a steersman correcting course). In *positive* feedback the loop reinforces change — output amplifies the very signal that produced it (a microphone howl, a population boom).

**statement** — given a system with output y fed back to influence input, the loop is **negative** when the fed-back signal acts to reduce the deviation of y from a reference (180° out of phase with the input), and **positive** when it acts to increase that deviation (in phase). Negative feedback drives a system toward a stable equilibrium / setpoint; positive feedback drives it away (toward growth, saturation, or instability). **[confirmed]**

**basis** — feedback is the structural primitive of cybernetics: control is achieved not by open-loop command but by continuously measuring the gap between actual and desired state and acting to reduce it. James Clerk Maxwell's "On Governors" (1868) gave the first mathematical analysis of a feedback regulator (the steam-engine governor) [[source.maxwell-1868]]; Harold S. Black's negative-feedback amplifier (conceived 1927, published 1934) showed feedback could trade gain for stability and linearity in electronics; Wiener's *Cybernetics* (1948) raised feedback to the unifying principle across animal and machine [[source.wiener-1948]]. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the noun "feed-back" was first used by Karl Ferdinand Braun (1909) for unwanted electronic coupling, standard by ~1920 **[confirmed]**. The control-engineering and cybernetic readings are the load-bearing ones here.
