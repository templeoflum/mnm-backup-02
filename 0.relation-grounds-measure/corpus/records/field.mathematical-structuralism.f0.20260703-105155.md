**origin** — Paul Benacerraf, "What Numbers Could Not Be," *Philosophical Review* 74 (1965): 47–73 — the argument that since infinitely many set-theoretic progressions serve equally well as "the" natural numbers, numbers cannot *be* any particular objects; they are positions in a structure. The catalyst for structuralism in the philosophy of mathematics. [[source.benacerraf-1965]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Stewart Shapiro, *Philosophy of Mathematics: Structure and Ontology* (Oxford University Press, 1997) — the principal statement of **ante rem** (non-eliminative) structuralism: structures exist *sui generis*, prior to the systems instantiating them; mathematical objects are "offices, not officeholders." Michael Resnik, *Mathematics as a Science of Patterns* (OUP, 1997) develops the pattern-based version in parallel. [[source.shapiro-1997]] [[source.resnik-1997]] **[confirmed]**

**door** — Shapiro (1997) is the standard entry text; the debate's map is the SEP/IEP "Structuralism in the Philosophy of Mathematics" entries. Eliminative and modal-structural alternatives are set out in Geoffrey Hellman, *Mathematics Without Numbers* (OUP, 1989). [[source.hellman-1989]] **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — distinct from [[field.structuralism]] (the linguistic/cultural structuralism of Saussure & Lévi-Strauss, mind canon) and from [[field.ontic-structural-realism]] (the *physics/metaphysics* thesis that relational structure is fundamental): this is the philosophy-of-mathematics branch. Closely tied to [[field.category-theory]] as an alternative structural foundation (Lawvere's ETCS). Its live problems — the identity of indiscernibles in non-rigid structures (Keränen, Ladyman) and what makes a property "structural" (Korbmacher & Schiemer) — sit at the seam with OSR.

*grade s — origin (Benacerraf), codification (Shapiro/Resnik), and door (Shapiro + the eliminative/modal alternatives) all present.*
