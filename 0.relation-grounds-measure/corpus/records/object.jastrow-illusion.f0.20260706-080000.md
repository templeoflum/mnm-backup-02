**statement (plain)** — The Jastrow Illusion is a robust size illusion in which two identical curvilinear shapes—typically annular sectors or fan-shaped figures with one curved edge longer than the other—appear markedly different in size when arranged such that the longer edge of the upper shape is adjacent to the shorter edge of the lower shape. Discovered by Polish-American psychologist Joseph Jastrow in 1889, the illusion demonstrates that perceived size depends critically on local spatial relationships and edge comparisons rather than global shape metrics. When the two identical shapes are presented in this configuration, the lower shape consistently appears substantially larger than the upper one, even though they are physically identical.

**statement (framework)** — category: geometric size length; perceptual mechanism(s): spatial-filtering, contour-integration.
