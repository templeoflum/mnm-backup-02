**origin** — William James, *The Principles of Psychology* (Henry Holt, 1890), chapter IV "Habit" — the first systematic psychological treatment: habit as acquired automaticity that economizes attention and stabilizes conduct ("habit is the enormous fly-wheel of society"). [[source.james-1890]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Wendy Wood & David Neal, "A new look at habits and the habit–goal interface," *Psychological Review* 114(4):843–863 (2007) — the modern account: habits as context–response associations acquired through repetition, cued directly by environment and running in parallel with (often winning over) goal-directed control. [[source.wood-neal-2007]] **[confirmed as attribution]**

**door** — Wendy Wood, *Good Habits, Bad Habits: The Science of Making Positive Changes That Stick* (Farrar, Straus & Giroux, 2019) — the leading researcher's popular synthesis: willpower is overrated, context (friction and cues) is decisive, and durable change comes from engineering the environment so the desired behavior repeats. [[source.wood-2019]] **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — descends from associationism and from operant conditioning ([[field.operant-conditioning]], stimulus–response reinforcement) but is distinct: it centers on *automaticity and context-cueing* rather than reinforcement schedules, and on the habit–goal interface. Meets choice architecture ([[field.choice-architecture-behavioral-economics]]) at the shared premise that arranging the environment beats exhorting the will. Popular treatments — Duhigg's *The Power of Habit* (2012, cue–routine–reward) and Clear's *Atomic Habits* (2018) — carry the ideas to a general audience; the scholarly anchors are James, Wood & Neal, and Lally et al. (2010, formation-timing).

*grade s — origin, codification, and door all present.*
