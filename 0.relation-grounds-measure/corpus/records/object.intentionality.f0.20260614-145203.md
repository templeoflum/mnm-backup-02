**statement (plain)** — every act of consciousness is *of* or *about* something. To perceive is to perceive an object, to believe is to believe something, to desire is to desire something. This directedness — "aboutness" — is the defining mark of the mental, and the structure phenomenology sets out to describe.

**statement** — consciousness is intentional: it is essentially directed toward an object (the *intentional object*). Brentano makes "intentional in-existence" (*intentionale Inexistenz*) the criterion distinguishing mental phenomena from physical ones — every mental phenomenon contains an object within itself, has content, is directed at an object. Husserl rebuilds the notion, distinguishing the act (*noesis*) from the object-as-meant (*noema*), and makes the act–object correlation the field of phenomenological analysis. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — descriptive, not a formal theorem: it is a structural claim about the nature of conscious experience, established by reflective description rather than proof.

**lineage** — the concept derives from Aristotle's theory of perception and thought as read through the Scholastic / Thomistic tradition (whence "intentional"). Revived in its modern form by Franz Brentano, *Psychology from an Empirical Standpoint* (1874), as the mark of the mental [[source.brentano-1874]]. Husserl, who attended Brentano's Vienna lectures (1884–1886), reinterpreted Brentano's "descriptive psychology" as phenomenology and made intentionality central — *Logical Investigations* (1900–01) [[source.husserl-1900]], *Ideas I* (1913) [[source.husserl-1913]] — while criticizing and reworking Brentano's account. **[confirmed]**
