**statement (plain)** — to ask a horse for something, you apply a small steady pressure (a cue) and then take it away the instant the horse responds — or even just leans toward the right answer (a "try"). The horse doesn't learn from the pressure; it learns from the relief when the pressure stops. So timing the release, and feeling the horse's smallest try, is the entire skill. Done well it's gentle and conversational ("feel"); the goal is the lightest possible cue.

**statement** — **pressure-and-release** (release-based training): a cue is applied as pressure and removed (released) the moment the animal performs, or initiates, the requested response. Operant analysis: the response is strengthened by **negative reinforcement** — removal of an aversive (the pressure) contingent on the behavior. The craft variables are **feel** (reading the horse's state and offering pressure as communication, "the language of horses"), **timing** (when the release lands — on the try, not after), and **balance**. Reward-by-release, not punishment-by-force, is the defining commitment of natural horsemanship. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — a craft method, validated by practice rather than proof, but with a clean theoretical mapping onto [[field.operant-conditioning]]: the release is the negative-reinforcement contingency and "rewarding the try" is shaping by successive approximation. The principal claim — that learning is driven by the *release*, so timing is decisive — is consistent across the natural-horsemanship literature and its behavior-science readings. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — ancient roots in [[source.xenophon-horsemanship]] (*On Horsemanship*, c. 4th c. BCE: gentle, reward-and-calm handling, earliest surviving treatise). The modern "feel/timing" method was codified by **Tom Dorrance** ([[source.dorrance-1987]], *True Unity*, 1987), his brother **Bill Dorrance** (*True Horsemanship Through Feel*, where feel is called "the language of horses"), and their foremost student **Ray Hunt** (*Think Harmony with Horses*; "feel, timing, and balance"). Carried as a craft/method strand of the dual ethology–horsemanship lineage. **[confirmed]**
