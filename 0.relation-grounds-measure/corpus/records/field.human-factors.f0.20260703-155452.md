**origin** — the discipline of **human factors** (a.k.a. human factors and ergonomics) — fitting the machine and task to the human — took its modern supervisory-control form in Thomas Sheridan's program on **human supervisory control** (from the 1970s; *Telerobotics, Automation, and Human Supervisory Control*, MIT Press, 1992) and Lisanne Bainbridge's founding critique "Ironies of Automation" (*Automatica*, 1983). [[source.sheridan-1992]] [[source.bainbridge-1983]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Jens Rasmussen's **skill–rule–knowledge** framework (*IEEE SMC*, 1983) gave the cognitive model of operator performance and error, growing into "cognitive systems engineering" (Rasmussen, Hollnagel & Woods). Mica Endsley's **situation awareness** theory (*Human Factors*, 1995) and Vicente & Rasmussen's **ecological interface design** (*IEEE SMC*, 1992) turned it into a design program. [[source.rasmussen-1983]] [[source.endsley-1995]]

**door** — the applied standards make it concrete: alarm-management practice (EEMUA 191; ANSI/ISA-18.2) and the "dark cockpit / quiet-dark" philosophy of flight-deck design. The journal *Human Factors* and Endsley's SA model are the standard entries.

**lineage** — sits between [[field.human-computer-interaction]] (the interface as an augmentation surface) and [[field.high-reliability-organizations]] (how high-hazard *organizations* stay safe): human factors is the *cognitive-ergonomic* dimension — the individual operator's perception, workload, error, and interface. The current keystone objects cluster in supervisory control / control-room work; the field is broader (workload, anthropometrics, error).
