**statement (plain)** — **Orpheus** (Greek *Ὀρφεύς*) is the legendary poet and musician of Thracian origin: son of the Muse Calliope (and, in some accounts, of Apollo), whose **song and lyre** are so surpassing that they charm wild beasts, move trees and stones, and still rivers. When his wife **Eurydice** dies of a serpent's bite, he descends alive into the **underworld** and, by the power of his song, wins from Hades and Persephone her release — on the single condition that he **not look back** at her until both have reached the light. At the threshold he turns to look, and she is lost to him a second time and forever. Grieving, he withdraws from the company of women and is at last **torn apart by maenads** (frenzied women of Dionysus); his head, still singing, floats to Lesbos. He is also the founder-figure to whom the **Orphic mysteries** and their theogonic poetry were ascribed.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: music and poetry, mystic initiation, katabasis (descent to the dead). Parentage: the Muse Calliope, with Apollo or the Thracian king Oeagrus as father. Key elements: the **lyre**, the descent for **Eurydice** and the fatal backward glance, the *sparagmos* (dismemberment) by the **maenads**, and the **Orphic** tradition of mysteries and cosmogonic poems attributed to his name. The look-back-and-loss narrative in its familiar form is given fullest by the Roman poets **Virgil (*Georgics* 4)** and **Ovid (*Metamorphoses* 10)** (not among the four cited sources — see report); Greek attestation of the descent and death runs from the archaic and dramatic tradition, with Apollodorus summarizing. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested from the archaic lyric and dramatic tradition and summarized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]]; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The canonical narrative of the loss-by-looking-back is fullest in Virgil and Ovid, and the Orphic mysteries are a distinct religious tradition attached to his name; these are attested tradition, variable across sources, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
