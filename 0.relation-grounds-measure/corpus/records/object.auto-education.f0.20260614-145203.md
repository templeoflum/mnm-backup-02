**statement (plain)** — children teach themselves by working with specially designed ("didactic") materials that show them their own mistakes. Because each material has a built-in "control of error," the child gets immediate feedback from the object itself and corrects without an adult telling them they are wrong. The teacher prepares the materials and steps back; the material does the teaching.

**statement** — auto-education (self-education) is the principle that learning is self-directed and material-mediated: didactic materials isolate one quality or concept and embed a **control of error** so the child can "recognize, correct, and learn from an error without adult assistance" (American Montessori Society). The canonical example is the cylinder block — if a child misplaces cylinders, "one cylinder is left which is too large for the only remaining hole," signalling the error materially. The materials, made available in the [[object.prepared-environment]], support repetition, concentration, and independence.

**basis** — a pedagogical method grounded in Montessori's clinical observation that children correct themselves through structured sensorial feedback. The didactic apparatus and the three-period lesson are adapted from her predecessors in the education of children with disabilities; the self-correcting design operationalizes the [[object.prepared-environment]] and is a precondition for [[object.normalization]]. **[confirmed]**

lineage — Montessori drew directly on **Jean-Marc-Gaspard Itard** (1774–1838), whose observational techniques she studied, and **Édouard Séguin** (1812–1880), from whom she adapted didactic sensorial materials and the three-period lesson (studied in Paris around 1899). Set out in *Il Metodo* (1909) [[source.montessori-1909]] / *The Montessori Method* (1912) [[source.montessori-1912]]. The Itard/Séguin influence and the control-of-error mechanism are confirmed by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy and AMS/AMI sources. **[confirmed]**
