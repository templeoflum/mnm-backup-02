**statement (plain)** — If a mathematical object is *nothing but* its structural relations (ante rem structuralism), then any two positions related by a symmetry of the structure — a non-trivial automorphism — must be identical, since they share every structural property. But the complex numbers i and −i, or the two square roots of a generic element, are clearly *two*, not one. So either the structuralist smuggles in non-structural identity facts, or the view over-identifies. This is the identity (or "indiscernibility") problem for structuralism.

**the reply** — Ladyman (2005): such positions are **weakly discernible** — they stand in an irreflexive relation to *each other* (i is the additive inverse of −i, not of itself), which suffices to make them two without any absolutely-distinguishing property. Contested (Ketland 2006), with a rival move to take identity in structures as primitive (Leitgeb & Ladyman 2008). The debate has a direct physics twin in the discernibility of quantum particles (Saunders, Muller) [[source.keranen-2001]] [[source.ladyman-2005]] **[confirmed as attribution]**.
