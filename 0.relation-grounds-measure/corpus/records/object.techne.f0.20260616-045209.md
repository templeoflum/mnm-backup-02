**statement (plain)** — *techne* (τέχνη), usually translated "craft" or "art," is the knowledge that guides making — the reasoned skill of the carpenter, doctor, navigator, sculptor. For Aristotle it is one of the five states by which the soul grasps truth, and it is defined as "a state involving true reason concerned with production" (poiesis). It is distinguished on one side from *episteme* (scientific knowledge of what cannot be otherwise — the eternal and necessary) and on the other from *phronesis* (practical wisdom, which concerns action, praxis, not production).

**statement (formal)** — Aristotle, *NE* VI.4 (Bekker ~1140a): techne is ἕξις μετὰ λόγου ποιητική — "a productive state involving true reason" — concerned with things that come into being and whose origin (*arche*) is in the maker, not in the thing made. Because its objects could be otherwise, techne deals with the contingent, unlike episteme; because its end is an external product, it is governed differently from phronesis, whose end is acting well itself.

**basis** — this is a position in the history of philosophy, not an empirical finding; "established" marks it as a real, central reading in the literature, not a claim asserted as true. The Aristotelian definition and its placement among the intellectual virtues are documented in *NE* VI [[source.aristotle-ne6]] and surveyed, with the surrounding Greek debate (Socratic use of crafts as models of knowledge; Plato's *Gorgias*, *Republic*, *Philebus*), in the SEP article [[source.sep-episteme-techne]]. Support: techne anchors the entire craft-philosophy lineage and supplies the vocabulary later authors (Heidegger, Sennett) return to. Limits: the translation "craft/art" carries modern theory/practice assumptions the Greek term does not; whether techne yields genuine knowledge or only knack is itself contested from Plato onward; and the techne/episteme line is drawn differently across Aristotle's own works. **[confirmed]**
