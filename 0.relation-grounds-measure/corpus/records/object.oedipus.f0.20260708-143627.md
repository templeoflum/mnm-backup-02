**statement (plain)** — **Oedipus** (Greek *Οἰδίπους*, "swollen-foot") is the ill-fated king of **Thebes**: son of King **Laius** and Queen **Jocasta**. Warned by oracle that his son would kill him, Laius exposes the infant on Mount Cithaeron with his ankles pierced; the child is rescued and raised as a prince of Corinth. Grown and told by the Delphic oracle that he is fated to **kill his father and marry his mother**, Oedipus flees Corinth (which he believes his home) to escape the prophecy — and on the road, in a quarrel, unknowingly **kills Laius**, his true father. Coming to Thebes, he answers the riddle of the **Sphinx** that has been ravaging the city, and is rewarded with the kingship and the hand of the widowed queen — **Jocasta**, his own mother, by whom he has children. When a plague at last drives him to seek the killer of Laius, he uncovers the whole truth: Jocasta hangs herself, and Oedipus **blinds himself** with the pins from her robe.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: prophecy and fate, unwitting transgression (parricide and incest), kingship, blindness and insight. Parentage: Laius and Jocasta of Thebes. Key elements: the exposure and the pierced feet (the source of his name), the **oracle**, the killing of Laius at the crossroads, the **riddle of the Sphinx**, the marriage to Jocasta, and the self-blinding. The narrative in its canonical, tightly-plotted form is **Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex* (Oedipus Tyrannus)** (not among the four cited sources — see report); Homer already knows the outline (*Odyssey* 11), and Apollodorus' Bibliotheca summarizes it. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested from Homer [[source.homer]] onward and summarized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]]; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The canonical dramatic form is Sophocles' *Oedipus Rex*, and details (notably the manner of the discovery and the self-blinding) are attested tradition, variable across sources, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
