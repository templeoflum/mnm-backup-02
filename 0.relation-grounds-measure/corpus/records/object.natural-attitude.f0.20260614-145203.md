**statement (plain)** — most of the time we live inside the unquestioned assumption that the world is simply *there*, real and independent of us, waiting to be found. We accept our bodies, other people, language, culture, and the laws of nature without examining them. This default, taken-for-granted stance is the natural attitude — the everyday outlook that phenomenology must suspend to begin its work.

**statement** — the *natural attitude* (*natürliche Einstellung*) is the pre-philosophical mode of consciousness governed by the "general thesis": the standing, implicit positing of the world as factually existing. It is the stance of everyday life *and* of the positive sciences, which inherit its unexamined belief in a mind-independent reality. Phenomenology does not refute it but neutralizes it through the epoché, in order to study how that very world is constituted in and for consciousness. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — a descriptive concept naming a structural feature of ordinary experience; established by reflective description, the contrast term against which the phenomenological attitude is defined.

**lineage** — articulated explicitly in *Ideen I* (*Ideas I*, 1913) [[source.husserl-1913]] as the stance suspended by [[object.epoche-phenomenological-reduction]]. Its world-correlate is later thematized as the [[object.lifeworld]] in the *Crisis* (1936). The natural attitude is the necessary point of departure: phenomenology begins by recognizing, then bracketing, it. **[confirmed]**
