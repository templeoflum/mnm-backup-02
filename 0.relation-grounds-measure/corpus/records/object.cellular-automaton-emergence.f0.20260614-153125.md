**statement (plain)** — a cellular automaton is a grid of cells, each alive or dead, that all update together by the same simple rule looking only at their neighbours. From rules this small, complex and lifelike patterns appear that no one designed — gliders that move, structures that grow and die. This "complex behaviour from simple local rules" is emergence, and it is a core engine of generative art.

**statement** — Conway's Game of Life (devised 1970), the canonical example: on an infinite square grid each cell is live or dead with eight neighbours, and at each step — (1) a live cell with fewer than two live neighbours dies (underpopulation); (2) a live cell with two or three live neighbours survives; (3) a live cell with more than three live neighbours dies (overpopulation); (4) a dead cell with exactly three live neighbours becomes live (reproduction). The Game of Life is **Turing-complete**: anything computable can be computed within it. [[source.conway-1970]] [[source.gardner-1970]] **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the concept matters to the field for two reasons. (1) **Emergence**: a fixed, tiny rule set produces unpredictable global order, so the artist authors the rule and the system supplies the form — the [[object.process-as-artwork]] move made vivid, and a clean instance of [[object.generative-art]]'s "system set in motion with autonomy." (2) **Substrate**: cellular automata are a standard generative substrate in creative coding (Shiffman's *Nature of Code* devotes a chapter to them). Cellular automata originate in the 1940s work of Stanisław Ulam and John von Neumann on self-replicating machines [[source.von-neumann-1932]]; Conway simplified von Neumann's heavy 29-state construction into a two-state rule. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — Conway devised Life in 1970 but did not publish it himself; it reached the public through Martin Gardner's "Mathematical Games" column, *Scientific American*, October 1970 [[source.gardner-1970]] — the work-vs-publication split carried as lineage, not conflict. The deeper origin (Ulam, von Neumann, 1940s) predates Life by three decades; [[source.von-neumann-1932]] is used here as the von Neumann anchor, with the self-replicating-automata work itself being 1940s–50s (completed/edited posthumously by Arthur Burks, 1966) — flagged so the date is not over-pinned.
