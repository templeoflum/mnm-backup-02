**origin** — Thomas Sheridan's program on **human supervisory control** (from the 1970s, codified in *Telerobotics, Automation, and Human Supervisory Control*, MIT Press, 1992): the human sets goals and monitors while automation executes, intervening at the edges. The founding critique is Lisanne Bainbridge, "Ironies of Automation" (*Automatica*, 1983) — automating the routine leaves the operator the residue that could not be automated, degraded skills, and the burden of taking over exactly when things are worst. [[source.sheridan-1992]] [[source.bainbridge-1983]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Jens Rasmussen's **skill–rule–knowledge** framework (*IEEE SMC*, 1983) supplied the cognitive model of operator performance and error; the field became "cognitive systems engineering" (Rasmussen, Hollnagel & Woods). Mica Endsley's **situation awareness** theory (*Human Factors*, 1995) and Vicente & Rasmussen's **ecological interface design** (*IEEE SMC*, 1992) turned it into a design program. [[source.rasmussen-1983]] [[source.endsley-1995]]

**door** — the applied standards make it concrete: alarm-management practice (EEMUA 191; ANSI/ISA-18.2) and the "dark cockpit / quiet-dark" philosophy of flight-deck design. Endsley's SA model is the standard entry to the theory.

**lineage** — sits between [[field.human-computer-interaction]] (the interface as an augmentation surface) and [[field.high-reliability-orgs-safety-engineering]] (how high-hazard organizations stay safe): this is specifically the *cognitive* work of an operator supervising automation in a control room. Directly grounds control-room / dispatch practice.

*grade s — origin (Sheridan/Bainbridge), codification (Rasmussen SRK, Endsley SA, EID), and door (alarm-management standards, SA model) all present.*
