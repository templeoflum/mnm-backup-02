**statement (plain)** — David Pye splits all making into two. The **workmanship of risk** is "workmanship using any kind of technique or apparatus, in which the quality of the result is not predetermined, but depends on the judgment, dexterity and care which the maker exercises as he works" — every stroke can spoil it, as with a pen, a brush, a chisel. The **workmanship of certainty** is found in mass production and automated processes: the quality is fully predetermined before a single workpiece is made, fixed in the jig, mould, or program, so the outcome no longer rides on the operator's moment-to-moment skill. The distinction is about the locus of risk, not about hand vs machine — power tools can be either, and a printing press is high-certainty.

**basis** — a design-theory concept, not an empirical law; "established" marks it as a recognized and widely cited position in craft and design philosophy. Pye states and develops it across *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* (1968), alongside companion distinctions (free vs regulated workmanship; diversity and soundness) [[source.pye-1968]]. It is taken up and extended by Richard Sennett in *The Craftsman*, where the maker's exposure to risk and resistance is central to skill and to "the desire to do a job well for its own sake" [[source.sennett-2008]]. Support: the risk/certainty axis is a touchstone in craft, design, and material-culture writing. Limits: critics note the binary can become a slogan that romanticizes hand-risk and undervalues high-certainty work; the line blurs for digitally controlled and hybrid processes; "risk" conflates several things (variability, skill-dependence, reversibility). **[confirmed]**
