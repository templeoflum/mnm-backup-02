**statement (plain)** — The transient hypofrontality hypothesis holds that altered states of consciousness are produced by a temporary, reversible decrease in prefrontal-cortex activity. Because the prefrontal cortex supports higher-order executive functions (working memory, sustained and directed attention, abstract reasoning, temporal integration, self-reflective awareness), attenuating it yields the characteristic ASC phenomenology — distorted time sense, reduced self-monitoring, loosened analytic control. The phenomenological differences between states (dream vs. meditation vs. runner's high) are attributed to *which* frontal circuits are differentially downregulated.

**basis** — Set out by Dietrich, who marshals evidence across dreaming, endurance exercise, meditation, daydreaming, hypnosis, and drug states for selective prefrontal attenuation as the common denominator [[source.dietrich-2003]] [confirmed]. The broader survey by Vaitl et al. independently reviews frontal/prefrontal and metabolic changes across induction routes, situating hypofrontality among the neurobiological findings for several ASC [[source.vaitl-2005]] [confirmed].

**limits** — It is a unifying hypothesis, not an established law: "hypofrontality" is not observed uniformly (some states show frontal *increases* or mixed patterns), the prefrontal cortex is functionally heterogeneous so "downregulation" is coarse, and the account competes with network-level explanations (e.g. default-mode-network changes). Recorded here as an empirical hypothesis with partial support and active dispute. [candidate, partially confirmed]
