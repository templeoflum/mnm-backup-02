**statement (plain)** — to know what a sentence means, you need two things and only two: what its words mean, and how they are put together. Change either — a different word, or the same words combined differently ("dog bites man" vs "man bites dog") — and the meaning changes accordingly. Meaning is built up by rule from the bottom, not read off the whole.

**statement (formal)** — the meaning of a syntactically complex whole is a function only of the meanings of its syntactic parts together with the manner in which they are combined (Partee's formulation). "Strong" (immediate parts + top-level function) vs "weak" (parts + complete combination) variants. Montague formalizes it as a homomorphism from a syntactic to a semantic algebra. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — credited to Frege (1892), though never stated verbatim by him and in tension with his context principle (1884); prominence via Montague and Partee. Known limits: idioms ("kick the bucket"), context-dependence, irony, belief reports, quotation — where meaning is not a function of parts alone. [[source.frege-1892]] [[source.montague-1970]]
