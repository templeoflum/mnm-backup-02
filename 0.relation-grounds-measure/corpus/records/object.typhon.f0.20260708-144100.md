**statement (plain)** — **Typhon** (Greek *Τυφῶν*, also *Typhōeus*) is a monstrous, **serpentine storm-giant**, the youngest child of **Gaia** (Earth) and **Tartarus** (*Theogony* 820–822). Vast beyond the mountains, with a hundred snake-heads and eyes of fire, he is the **last and greatest challenger** to the newly won rule of **Zeus**. In a cosmos-shaking battle Zeus overcomes him with the **thunderbolt**, blasts him, and casts him down beneath the earth — pinned, in the common tradition, under **Mount Etna**, whose eruptions are his fury. By **Echidna** (the half-serpent monster) he fathers a brood of monsters, among them **Cerberus**, the **Hydra**, and the **Chimera**.

**statement (attestation)** — domain: destructive storm and whirlwind, monstrous chaos, and the final threat to the Olympian order. Genealogy: son of Gaia and Tartarus; consort of Echidna; father (through her) of Cerberus, the Hydra, the Chimera, Orthrus, and (in some accounts) further monsters. Etymology: Greek *Typhōn* / *Typhōeus*, connected with *typhōs*, the destructive whirlwind or hurricane (hence "typhoon" by later, disputed transmission). He has no Roman *interpretatio* proper; in some Greek accounts he is equated with the Egyptian **Set**. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested in Hesiod's Theogony [[source.hesiod-theogony]] and systematized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]]; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The parentage, the battle with Zeus, and the defeat by thunderbolt are attested; the burial under Etna and the catalogue of his monstrous offspring are attested tradition, variable across sources, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
