**statement (plain)** — The Reversing Depth Illusion encompasses a class of visual phenomena in which the perceived depth organization of a stimulus spontaneously alternates between two mutually exclusive three-dimensional interpretations, despite unchanging retinal input. Classic examples include hollow face masks that flip between appearing concave and convex, architectural drawings such as the Necker cube that reverse between different depth configurations, and ambiguous wire-frame figures that alternate between competing spatial arrangements. These reversals occur because the two-dimensional retinal projection is compatible with multiple three-dimensional structures, and the visual system lacks sufficient information to uniquely determine the correct depth interpretation.
