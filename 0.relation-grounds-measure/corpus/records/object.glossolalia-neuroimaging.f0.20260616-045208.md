**statement (plain)** — When experienced practitioners speak in tongues, functional neuroimaging shows a distinctive pattern of brain activity: regional cerebral blood flow shifts relative to a closely matched control of religious singing in ordinary language, with decreased activity in the frontal lobes (regions associated with volitional control of speech and self-direction), alongside changes in the parietal lobes and left caudate.

**statement (formal)** — SPECT (single-photon emission computed tomography) measures regional cerebral blood flow (CBF) as a correlate of neural activity. In Newberg et al. (2006), n = 5 practitioners imaged during glossolalia vs. an English religious-singing baseline showed the frontal lobes, parietal lobes, and left caudate as most affected, with relatively decreased frontal CBF during glossolalia.

**basis (empirical)** — The pattern is reported by the first functional neuroimaging study of glossolalia, which deliberately contrasts the glossolalic state with a singing state matched on movement and devotional context but differing in that singing uses actual language [[source.newberg-2006]] [confirmed]. The direction of the finding — reduced frontal/volitional engagement during glossolalia — is consistent with the older behavioral account that glossolalic articulation is produced in an altered, non-ordinary mental state rather than by ordinary language control [[source.goodman-1972]] [confirmed].

**limits** — The study is explicitly preliminary: n = 5, a single practiced and self-selected population, and SPECT's coarse spatial/temporal resolution. It establishes that measurable cerebral changes accompany glossolalia; it does not establish a mechanism, and the neurotheological interpretations attached to it (loss of self-control, relation to the divine) are framings beyond what the blood-flow data alone show. Recorded as a studied empirical result, with its religious interpretation bracketed.
