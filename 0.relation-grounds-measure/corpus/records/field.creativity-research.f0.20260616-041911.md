Creativity research is the branch of psychology that studies how minds produce ideas that are both novel and useful (or appropriate), and how that capacity is described, measured, and explained.

**origin** — the modern research program is conventionally dated to J. P. Guilford's 1950 presidential address to the American Psychological Association, which argued that creativity had been neglected and recast it as a set of measurable cognitive abilities, centered on *divergent production* [[source.guilford-1950]] [confirmed]. An earlier process model — Graham Wallas's four stages (preparation, incubation, illumination, verification) — predates the psychometric program and supplies its phenomenological vocabulary [[source.wallas-1926]] [confirmed].

**codification** — the field consolidated around (a) the associative account of creativity, in which creative response is the joining of remote elements into useful combinations [[source.mednick-1962]] [confirmed]; (b) divergent-thinking testing as the dominant operationalization of creative potential [[source.runco-acar-2012]] [confirmed]; and (c) a cognitive-neuroscience synthesis framing creative cognition as dynamic cooperation between the brain's default and executive-control networks [[source.beaty-2016]] [confirmed].

**door** — R. Keith Sawyer's *Explaining Creativity: The Science of Human Innovation* (2nd ed., Oxford University Press, 2012) is a standard survey covering the cognitive, social, and historical strands of the field [[source.sawyer-2012]] [confirmed].

**lineage** — the founding is dated to Guilford 1950 by convention, but the divergent/process distinction and the incubation construct descend from Wallas 1926; the two are complementary lenses (ability vs. process) rather than competing claims.

*grade [s] — origin (Guilford 1950 / Wallas 1926), codification (Mednick, Runco & Acar, Beaty), and door (Sawyer 2012) all present.*
