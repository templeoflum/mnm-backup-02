**statement (plain)** — a Zettelkasten ("slip-box") is a single growing collection of small note-cards, each holding one idea, each carrying a permanent address, and each linked to other cards by reference. You don't file notes under topics; you let them connect. Over time the web of links becomes a "second memory" you can interrogate — Luhmann described it as a communication partner that can surprise you.

**statement** — the method has three load-bearing parts working together: (1) the [[object.atomic-note]] — one idea per card, in your own words; (2) the [[object.fixed-branching-address]] — every card gets a permanent unique number it never loses, abstracted from any topical order; (3) [[object.linking-emergent-structure]] — cards reference each other and a keyword register points into the web, so structure emerges from connection rather than from a pre-planned hierarchy. Luhmann frames the result as "a kind of second memory, an alter ego with which you can always communicate," whose value is that "both partners can surprise each other." **[confirmed]**

**basis** — this is a practitioner method, not a formal system; its authority rests on documentation of an actual working archive rather than proof. Luhmann's own 1981 experience-report [[source.luhmann-1981]] states the design intent ("a system of notes that can act as a competent communication partner"); Schmidt's archival scholarship [[source.schmidt-2018]] verifies the realized structure of the ~90,000-card index Luhmann maintained from 1952 to 1997. Ahrens [[source.ahrens-2017]] codified it into a teachable workflow.

**lineage** — Luhmann is the source of the specific method. Deep precursors in the broader history of note-keeping are commonly cited — Conrad Gessner's 16th-c. slips [[source.gessner]], Locke's commonplace-book method (1686 Fr. / 1706 Eng.) [[source.locke-commonplace]], and Sertillanges, *La vie intellectuelle* (1921) [[source.sertillanges-1921]] — but these are antecedents of the genre, not of Luhmann's particular branching-address design. **[confirmed as attribution]** *(honesty flag: a large share of what circulates as "the Luhmann technique" is later interpretation by the note-taking community and by Ahrens; the documented core is the 1981 essay plus Schmidt's archival work — much workflow detail beyond that is candidate, not confirmed Luhmann practice.)*

**door** — Luhmann's essay in English ("Communicating with Slip Boxes" / "Communications with Zettelkastens," Kühn and later Sascha trans.) free at zettelkasten.de; Schmidt (2018) open access in *Sociologica*; Ahrens, *How to Take Smart Notes* (2017).

**sources** — [[source.luhmann-1981]]; [[source.schmidt-2018]]; [[source.ahrens-2017]]; zettelkasten.de translation and the Bielefeld Niklas-Luhmann-Archiv digital edition for the verified archive (~90,000 cards). **[confirmed]**
