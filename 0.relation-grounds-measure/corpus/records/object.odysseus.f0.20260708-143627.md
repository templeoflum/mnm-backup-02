**statement (plain)** — **Odysseus** (Greek *Ὀδυσσεύς*) is the king of **Ithaca** and the most cunning of the Greek heroes (*polymētis*, "of many devices"): son of Laertes, husband of **Penelope**, father of Telemachus. At **Troy** his craft as much as his courage serves the Greeks — the stratagem of the **Trojan Horse** is his. He is the hero of Homer's **Odyssey**, whose ten-year homeward voyage is a chain of trials: the blinding of the Cyclops **Polyphemus**, the enchantress **Circe**, the descent to consult the dead, the song of the **Sirens**, the passage between **Scylla and Charybdis**, the cattle of the Sun, the sojourn with the nymph Calypso, and the shipwreck that leaves him alone. Returning at last to Ithaca disguised as a beggar, he strings the great bow that no suitor can bend, slays the suitors who have besieged Penelope, and is reunited with her and with his father.

**statement (attestation)** — domains: cunning intelligence (*mētis*), endurance, homecoming (*nostos*), kingship. Parentage: Laertes and Anticleia of Ithaca. Key attributes: eloquence, guile, the great **bow**; epithets *polymētis* ("of many devices") and *polytlas* ("much-enduring"). Key episodes of the **Odyssey**: **Polyphemus**, **Circe**, the underworld, the **Sirens**, **Scylla and Charybdis**, Calypso, and the slaying of the suitors. The wanderings are the matter of Homer's Odyssey; Apollodorus' Bibliotheca (in its Epitome) summarizes the wider cycle. The Roman *interpretatio* names him **Ulysses** (Latin *Ulixes*). **[confirmed as attribution]**

**basis** — a figure of Greek mythic tradition, documented as *held* (`status: doctrinal`), not asserted. Attested pre-eminently in Homer's Odyssey and Iliad [[source.homer]], and summarized in Apollodorus' Bibliotheca [[source.apollodorus-bibliotheca]]; the standard reference treatment is Hard [[source.hard-2004]]. The sequence of the wanderings is attested tradition, variable in later sources, and should be read as such, not as neutral report. **[confirmed as attribution]**
