**statement (plain)** — perceived sensation grows as a *power* of physical intensity, with an exponent that depends on the sense. Some senses compress (brightness, loudness grow slower than the stimulus, exponent < 1); some expand (electric shock grows faster, exponent > 1). The exponent is the signature of the modality.

**statement (formula)** — for stimulus intensity I,

ψ(I) = k · Iᵃ,

where ψ is perceived magnitude, a the modality-specific exponent, and k a unit constant. Example exponents (magnitude-estimation data): brightness ≈ 0.33–0.5, loudness (sound pressure) ≈ 0.67, taste (salt) ≈ 1.4, electric shock ≈ 3.5. **[confirmed]**

**basis / empirical support & limits** — supported by **magnitude estimation** and **magnitude production**, in which observers assign numbers directly proportional to perceived intensity (Stevens' "direct scaling"). On log–log axes the power law is a straight line of slope a. It fits a wider range than the logarithmic law (extending toward zero intensity). Stevens advanced it as a *replacement* for [[object.fechners-law]], arguing Fechner's indirect JND-summation scaling did not reflect raw sensation. **Criticisms:** magnitude-estimation procedures carry response biases (e.g. difficulty thinking in true ratios), exponents vary with method and context, and averaging across observers can mask individual differences. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — S. S. Stevens, "On the psychophysical law," *Psychological Review* 64(3): 153–181 (1957) [[source.stevens-1957]] — the paper that revived the power function (anticipated by Plateau and others in the 19th c.) and the modern direct-scaling program. The polemic title "To honor Fechner and repeal his law" is a *separate* later Stevens paper (*Science* 133, 1961), not this one. Distinct from [[source.stevens-1946]] (scales of measurement). **[confirmed]**
