**statement (plain)** — getting good at something comes from a particular kind of practice: focused, effortful work aimed at fixing specific weaknesses, ideally guided by a teacher and met with immediate feedback. Just doing an activity a lot ("more experience") is not the same thing — what matters is practice deliberately designed to push past what you can already do.

**statement** — Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) defined deliberate practice as practice activities designed (typically by a teacher) for the sole purpose of improving performance, requiring effort and concentration, not inherently enjoyable, and not immediately rewarded. Their central claim: individual differences in attained performance — even among elite performers — are closely related to the accumulated amount of deliberate practice. In their study of violinists, the best had accumulated substantially more solitary practice hours than less accomplished peers. **[confirmed]**

**basis & limits** — the 1993 study is correlational and relies on retrospective practice estimates; it operationally measured "practice alone" rather than strictly teacher-designed activity. The strong "differences largely accounted for by practice" claim has been bounded by later evidence: the [[source.macnamara-2014]] meta-analysis found deliberate practice explains ~12% of performance variance overall (26% games, 21% music, 18% sports, 4% education, <1% professions), and the 2019 replication of the violinist study found 26% variance vs. the original's 48%, with no significant best-vs-good practice difference. The concept stands as a real, important factor in skill acquisition; its sufficiency-for-mastery framing does not. The popularized [[object.ten-thousand-hour-rule]] is a distortion of this concept, not part of it.
