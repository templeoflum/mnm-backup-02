**statement (plain)** — from birth to about age six the child's mind takes in the surrounding world — language, movement, order, social and cultural norms — quickly, effortlessly, and without conscious study, the way a sponge takes up water. Through this absorption the child literally self-constructs their own mind and personality.

**statement** — "a sensitivity in the child's mind that can absorb knowledge, experiences, relationships, and images quickly and effortlessly into its subconscious mind" (AMI). Montessori divided it into two phases: an **unconscious** absorbent mind (≈0–3 years), when impressions form and become "incarnated" before voluntary attention, memory, and reason develop; and a **conscious** absorbent mind (≈3–6 years), when the child actively seeks experience and deliberately repeats work to master it. After about age six, learning increasingly requires conscious effort.

**basis** — a developmental-psychological claim about the qualitatively distinct mode of learning in early childhood, grounded in Montessori's observation that very young children acquire language and adapt to their culture with a speed and ease unmatched in later life. It is the mental capacity that makes the [[object.prepared-environment]] effective and that operates through the [[object.sensitive-periods]]. **[confirmed]**

lineage — the term "absorbent mind" is Montessori's own coinage; the concept runs throughout her work and receives its fullest treatment in *The Absorbent Mind* (1949) [[source.montessori-1949]], with earlier development in *The Secret of Childhood* (1936) [[source.montessori-1936]]. Confirmed by the Association Montessori Internationale (AMI glossary) and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy. **[confirmed]**
