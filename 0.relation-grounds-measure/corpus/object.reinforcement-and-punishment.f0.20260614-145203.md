**statement (plain)** — a consequence either strengthens a behavior (reinforcement) or weakens it (punishment), and it does so either by adding a stimulus (positive) or by removing one (negative). Crossing these two distinctions gives four operations: positive reinforcement (add something wanted → behavior up), negative reinforcement (remove something aversive → behavior up), positive punishment (add something aversive → behavior down), negative punishment (remove something wanted → behavior down). "Positive/negative" mean add/remove, not good/bad.

**statement** — for an operant behavior B emitted in the presence of a discriminative stimulus, a contingent consequence changes the future probability of B. Reinforcement = consequence that increases the rate/probability of B; punishment = consequence that decreases it. Each is realized either by stimulus presentation (positive) or stimulus withdrawal (negative), yielding the 2×2: {positive, negative} × {reinforcement, punishment}. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — empirical and definitional: reinforcement and punishment are defined functionally by their effect on response rate (measured in the operant chamber), not by the presumed pleasantness of the stimulus. A consequence "is" a reinforcer only if it raises the behavior's probability. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — descends directly from the [[object.law-of-effect]]. Skinner substituted observable terms for Thorndike's "satisfaction/discomfort," introducing "reinforcement" in [[source.skinner-1938]] *The Behavior of Organisms* (1938) and developing the functional account in [[source.skinner-1953]] *Science and Human Behavior* (1953), where he argued positive reinforcement is more effective than punishment. The fully articulated four-cell table is standard in the secondary literature built on Skinner. **[confirmed]**
