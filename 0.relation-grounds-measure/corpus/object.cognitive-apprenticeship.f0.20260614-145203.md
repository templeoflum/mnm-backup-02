**statement (plain)** — a way of teaching complex mental skills (reading, writing, math, problem-solving) by borrowing how traditional craft apprenticeship works. In a craft, the master's actions are visible, so the apprentice can watch, imitate, and be corrected. Thinking is invisible — so the teacher must deliberately make it visible: show their reasoning out loud, then watch and coach the learner, then gradually remove the help.

**statement** — Collins, Brown & Newman (1989) proposed cognitive apprenticeship as a framework for designing learning environments that "make thinking visible." Its core teaching methods are six: **modeling** (expert performs a task while verbalizing the normally hidden reasoning), **coaching** (observing the learner and giving hints/feedback), **scaffolding** (temporary supports that are gradually withdrawn — "fading"), **articulation** (getting learners to verbalize their own reasoning), **reflection** (comparing one's process with an expert's or peer's), and **exploration** (learners take autonomy in posing and solving their own problems). The first three form the core. **[confirmed]**

**basis & limits** — a pedagogical design framework, not an empirical law; it synthesizes situated-cognition theory with apprenticeship practice and is validated through instructional studies rather than a single decisive experiment. It is the companion codification to the deliberate-practice tradition — where deliberate practice characterizes *what the learner does*, cognitive apprenticeship characterizes *how the master transmits*. Effectiveness depends on domain, implementation, and the expert's ability to externalize tacit reasoning.
