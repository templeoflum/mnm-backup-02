**statement (plain)** — Goodman analyzed recordings of glossolalia from congregations with different native languages (Mexico City Spanish, Yucatec Maya, English-speaking Indiana) and found striking cross-cultural agreement in their phonological and suprasegmental (rhythm, stress, intonation) form. Because the form holds across unrelated languages, she argues the utterance is not a language but an **artifact of a hyperaroused dissociative state (trance)**: in Chomskyan terms, the surface structure of a non-linguistic "deep structure," namely the altered state of consciousness itself. Speakers vocalize as they do *because* their speech is being modified by the trance they have entered.

**basis** — fieldwork, sound recordings, and phonetic analysis across four cultural settings; the cross-setting structural convergence is the central evidence, with Yucatán fieldwork reported as confirming the hypothesis [[source.goodman-1972]]. The phenomenon sits within the cross-cultural inventory of institutionalized trance behaviors mapped by the Cross-Cultural Study of Dissociational States [[source.bourguignon-1973]]. **[confirmed]**

limits — later linguists (e.g. William Samarin) disputed the strong "trance-driven" reading, arguing glossolalia can be produced without measurable trance and reflects learned phonological patterning of the speaker's own language; Goodman's trance-artifact account is influential but not uncontested. The religious significance attributed by practitioners is bracketed here — recorded as the practice studied, not endorsed.

**sources** — [[source.goodman-1972]]; [[source.bourguignon-1973]]; Goodman, "Phonetic Analysis of Glossolalia in Four Cultural Settings," *JSSR* 8(2), 1969 (Semantic Scholar). **[confirmed]**
