**statement (plain)** — the file-drawer problem is the worry that the studies you can read are not a fair sample of the studies that were done. Experiments that "work" (show a significant effect) get written up and published; experiments that find nothing get filed away and never seen. So the published literature over-represents positive results and the true picture is hidden in everyone's file drawers.

**statement** — the file-drawer problem is the canonical name for publication bias: the outcome of a study influences the probability that it is submitted and published, with statistically significant ("positive") results favored over null/non-significant ones. The result is a systematically biased published record and inflated apparent effect sizes; in the extreme framing, journals fill with the ~5% of false positives while the ~95% non-significant studies remain unpublished. **[confirmed]**

**basis/origin** — coined by Robert Rosenthal in "The file drawer problem and tolerance for null results," *Psychological Bulletin* 86(3) (1979):638–641, which also introduced the "fail-safe N" tolerance computation. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**lineage** — single clear coinage (Rosenthal 1979). "File-drawer problem" and "publication bias" are used near-synonymously; "file-drawer" is the metaphor, "publication bias" the general mechanism. It is a primary motivating diagnosis behind [[object.preregistration]] and [[object.registered-report]], and a key contributor to the [[object.replication-crisis]]. [[source.ioannidis-2005]] models its compounding effect on the proportion of false published findings. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — Rosenthal 1979 [[source.rosenthal-1979]]; Ioannidis 2005 [[source.ioannidis-2005]].
