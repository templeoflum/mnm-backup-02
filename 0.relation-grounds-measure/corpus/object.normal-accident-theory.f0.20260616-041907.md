**statement (plain)** — some technologies are built so that many parts interact in hidden, unexpected ways (interactive complexity) and so that trouble spreads fast with no slack to absorb it (tight coupling). In such systems a chain of small, individually minor failures will eventually combine into a catastrophe that no operator could foresee and no added safety device can fully prevent. Because this is a property of the system's structure, Perrow calls the result a "normal accident."

**statement (framework)** — Perrow places technologies on two axes: **interactive complexity** (linear ↔ complex) and **coupling** (loose ↔ tight). The complex-and-tightly-coupled quadrant (e.g. nuclear power, nuclear weapons, some chemical plants) has the highest "normal accident" potential. Interactive complexity is the *trigger* (failures interact unobservably); tight coupling is the *propagator* (no time or slack to intervene). Redundancy and added safety systems can raise complexity and thus, perversely, increase accident potential.

**basis** — grounded in Perrow's case analyses of Three Mile Island, petrochemical plants, aviation, marine transport, dams and mining, later extended to Bhopal, Chernobyl, and Challenger [[source.perrow-1984]]. *Support* — the complexity/coupling lens is now standard vocabulary across safety scholarship. *Limits* — Leveson, Dulac, Marais & Carroll note the theory's concepts (especially "complexity" and "coupling") are hard to operationalize and define narrowly, and that it tends to over-pessimism; the rival HRO program reports hazardous systems running for long periods without such accidents [[source.leveson-2009]]. **[confirmed]**
