**statement (plain)** — we know more than we can put into words. A person can recognize a face in a crowd, ride a bike, or judge that an experiment "feels right" without being able to state the rules they are following. That knowing is real and reliable but it lives in the doing, not in a set of articulable propositions. Polanyi's slogan: "we can know more than we can tell."

**statement** — *tacit knowing* is the act of knowing in which we rely on particulars we cannot fully specify in order to attend to a coherent whole. The known-but-untellable surplus is irreducible: it is not merely knowledge that has yet to be written down, but knowing whose structure (attending *from* unspecifiable clues *to* a focus) cannot be exhaustively converted into explicit statements without destroying it.

**basis** — the claim rests on cases, not proof. Recognition (a face among thousands), skilled performance (swimming, cycling, the pianist's touch), perception, and scientific discovery all show competent, checkable knowing that the knower cannot fully articulate. Polanyi argues all explicit knowledge is itself rooted in tacit knowing (rules must be tacitly applied), so the tacit is foundational rather than residual. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — coined by Michael Polanyi in *Personal Knowledge* (1958) [[source.polanyi-1958]] and given its canonical statement in *The Tacit Dimension* (1966), based on his 1962 Terry Lectures [[source.polanyi-1966]]. Polanyi builds on [[source.ryle-1949]] Ryle's *knowing-how* / *knowing-that* distinction. Later carried into management/organization theory by Nonaka and Takeuchi (SECI model, from 1990) and named "Polanyi's Paradox" by economist David Autor (2014). **[confirmed]**
