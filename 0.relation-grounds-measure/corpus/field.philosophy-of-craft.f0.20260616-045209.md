**origin** — Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* Book VI — *techne* (craft knowledge) named as one of the five intellectual virtues and distinguished from *episteme* (demonstrative science) and *phronesis* (practical wisdom); the poiesis (production) / praxis (action) distinction set out at NE VI.4–5. [[source.aristotle-ne6]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — twentieth-century revival: Heidegger's *The Question Concerning Technology* (1954) recasts *techne* as a mode of revealing and contrasts *poiesis* (bringing-forth) with modern technology's *Gestell* [[source.heidegger-1954]]; David Pye's *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* (1968) gives craft theory its sharpest modern axis — the workmanship of risk vs certainty [[source.pye-1968]]; Richard Sennett's *The Craftsman* (2008) synthesizes the social-philosophical case for craftsmanship as a way of knowing through the hand [[source.sennett-2008]]. **[confirmed]**

**door** — Richard Parry, "Episteme and Techne," *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy* — free, citable survey mapping the Greek theory/craft distinction from the Presocratics through Aristotle. [[source.sep-episteme-techne]] **[confirmed]**

lineage — the field is genuinely composite and contested: it is read variously as a branch of ancient ethics/epistemology (Aristotle), of philosophy of technology (Heidegger), of design and material culture (Pye), and of social theory / pragmatism (Sennett, drawing on Dewey). It overlaps but does not coincide with [[field.tacit-knowledge]] (Polanyi's "we can know more than we can tell" supplies the epistemology of the maker's hand) and with [[field.deliberate-practice-apprenticeship]] (the acquisition side of skill). No single founding text owns it; the Aristotelian vocabulary is the shared root the later authors return to.

*grade [s] — origin (Aristotle NE VI), codification (Heidegger / Pye / Sennett), and door (SEP) all present and web-verified; state float pending settling through use.*
