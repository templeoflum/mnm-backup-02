**statement (plain)** — normalization is the change Montessori observed when a child, given freedom in a prepared environment, becomes deeply absorbed in self-chosen work: scattered, restless, or withdrawn behaviours ("deviations") fade and the child settles into concentration, calm, self-discipline, independence, and a love of work. It is the emergence of the child's true, undistorted nature — not making children "normal" in the conformist sense.

**statement** — a technical term Montessori borrowed from anthropology, denoting the process by which a child reaches "concentration, inner discipline, and peace" through meaningful, freely chosen work, becoming "a contributing member of society." Behaviours seen as defects or as superficial virtues — both treated as **deviations** caused by obstacles to development — disappear once the child concentrates on freely chosen work. "If a child is placed in an optimum prepared environment where inner impulses match external opportunities, the undeviated self emerges." Montessori noted normalization "causes the disappearance of many childish traits, not only those which are considered to be defects but also others which are generally thought to be virtues."

**basis** — an empirical claim about a repeatable behavioural transformation, grounded in Montessori's classroom observation that concentration on self-chosen work reliably produced calm, ordered, self-disciplined children. It is the outcome that the [[object.prepared-environment]] and [[object.auto-education]] are arranged to make possible; deep concentration is its trigger. **[confirmed]**

lineage — set out in *The Secret of Childhood* (1936) [[source.montessori-1936]] and discussed extensively in *The Absorbent Mind* (1949) [[source.montessori-1949]]. Confirmed by the Association Montessori Internationale (AMI/NAMTA literature) and the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy ("a psychological recovery" in which "the child's true 'personality'" is "allowed to construct itself normally"). **[confirmed]**
