**statement (plain)** — Richard Sennett defines craftsmanship as "an enduring, basic human impulse, the desire to do a job well for its own sake." On this reading craftsmanship is not confined to skilled manual trades: the programmer, the doctor, the artist, the parent, and the citizen all do a craftsman's work when they pursue quality for its own sake. Craftsmanship resists the modern split between thinking and making — "every good craftsman conducts a dialogue between concrete practices and thinking; this dialogue evolves into sustaining habits, and these habits establish a rhythm between problem solving and problem finding." Skill grows through practice, through tacit knowledge lodged in the hand, and through engagement with resistance and ambiguity in the material.

**basis** — a social-philosophical thesis, not an empirical finding; "established" marks it as a recognized, influential position. Stated and argued across *The Craftsman* through historical case studies — Roman brickmakers, Stradivari's workshop, Enlightenment encyclopedists, Linux open-source programmers, glassblowers, cooks [[source.sennett-2008]]. The "for its own sake" framing connects to Aristotle's praxis (activity whose end is in itself) and to the dignity of techne [[source.aristotle-ne6]]; Sennett also draws on Dewey's pragmatism and the tacit-knowledge tradition (see [[field.tacit-knowledge]]). Support: widely cited across design, education, sociology of work, and the maker movement. Limits: critics find the category so broad (any quality-seeking activity) that it risks losing analytic edge; the account is read by some as nostalgic about the workshop; the empirical claims about head/hand integration rest on illustration and interpretation rather than controlled study. **[confirmed]**
