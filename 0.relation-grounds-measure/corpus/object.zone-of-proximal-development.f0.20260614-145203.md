**statement (plain)** — there is a gap between what a learner can already do on their own and what they can do with help from someone more capable. That gap is the zone of proximal development. Skills sitting in the zone are "ripening" — not yet independent, but reachable with support today, and tomorrow they become independent.

**statement** — Vygotsky defined the ZPD as "the distance between the actual developmental level as determined by independent problem solving and the level of potential development as determined through problem solving under adult guidance or in collaboration with more capable peers" (*Mind in Society*, 1978, p. 86). It marks functions "that have not yet matured but are in the process of maturation … which will mature tomorrow but are currently in an embryonic state." **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the construct rests on distinguishing two developmental levels: the *actual* (what the child does unaided) and the *potential* (what the child does with guidance). The ZPD is the contrast between aided and unaided performance, and Vygotsky's claim is that instruction is effective when it is aimed at this zone rather than at already-matured functions.

**lineage** — originated by Lev Vygotsky in the last years of his life (early/mid 1930s; he died 1934). The concept reached the contemporary English-speaking field chiefly through the 1978 edited collection *Mind in Society* [[source.vygotsky-1978]], which served as the catalyst for its uptake in psychology and education. The term "scaffolding," now strongly associated with the ZPD, was *not* Vygotsky's — see [[object.instructional-scaffolding]]. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — definition and the "actual vs. potential / ripening functions" framing confirmed across the Vygotsky primary text as quoted in [[source.vygotsky-1978]] and independent secondary references (Wikipedia "Zone of proximal development"; Simply Psychology). Death year 1934 and 1930s development window confirmed independently.
