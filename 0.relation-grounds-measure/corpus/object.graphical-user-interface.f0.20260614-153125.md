**statement (plain)** — the graphical user interface (GUI) is the interaction paradigm in which the user works in a visual, spatial environment rather than by typing commands. Its canonical form is **WIMP** — Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointer — arranged as a **desktop metaphor**: the screen is treated like the surface of a desk holding documents and folders that the user points at, opens, and rearranges. It made computing accessible beyond specialists by letting people recognize and act on visible objects instead of recalling command syntax.

**statement (formula)** — none; a design paradigm. Defined by its four WIMP elements (Windows, Icons, Menus, Pointing device) and by the desktop metaphor that organizes them; it is the standard vehicle for [[direct manipulation]]. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — a design paradigm that emerged from research practice, not a derived or measured law. Developed at Xerox PARC through the 1970s on the Xerox Alto (operational 1973, first machine built around a GUI and the first to combine all four WIMP elements) and commercialized in the Xerox Star (1981); popularized by the Apple Lisa/Macintosh (1983–84). Status established as the dominant interface paradigm; not formalized. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — antecedent: [[Ivan Sutherland]]'s Sketchpad (1963), the first interactive graphical system [[source.sutherland-1963]], and Engelbart's NLS (1968), which introduced windows and the mouse. The desktop metaphor is credited to [[Alan Kay]], [[David C. Smith]] and colleagues at [[Xerox PARC]] (early 1970s); the Alto and Star realized WIMP; Apple brought it to the mass market. Sits beside [[direct manipulation]] (Shneiderman 1983) in the HCI canon of [[Card, Moran & Newell]] (1983) [[source.card-moran-newell-1983]]. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — [[source.sutherland-1963]] (antecedent, Sketchpad); [[source.card-moran-newell-1983]] (canon).
