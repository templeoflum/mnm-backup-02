**statement (plain)** — an object-to-think-with is a thing you can hold, build, or steer that lets you think about something abstract by working with the concrete object instead. Papert's own childhood gears were one: by imagining his body turning like a gear he could feel his way into algebra. The Logo Turtle is the designed version — a little on-screen (or floor) creature a child commands with simple instructions, and through playing with it the child reasons about geometry, angle, and procedure with their whole body.

**statement** — Papert calls certain objects "objects-to-think-with": concrete or computational entities a learner makes their own and uses to construct, examine, and revise the links between old and new knowledge. The Turtle is the constructed paradigm — "an object-to-think-with" that carries [[turtle-graphics]] and lets a child use "body-syntonic" reasoning (steering the Turtle as if it were their own body) to enter mathematics (*Mindstorms*, 1980). The gears of Papert's childhood are his autobiographical first example. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the concept supplies the bridge between [[object.constructionism]] (build to learn) and the [[object.microworld]] (where you build): the object is what the learner appropriates and reasons through. Its force is that abstract structure (gear ratios, turning angles) becomes thinkable because it is embodied in something the learner can manipulate and identify with — "anchoring" formal ideas in body-knowledge and personal attachment. It is an established design concept, not a formal result. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — introduced by Papert in *Mindstorms* (1980), opening with "The Gears of My Childhood" and developed through turtle graphics [[source.papert-1980]]. The framing was extended by Papert's MIT colleague Sherry Turkle (evocative objects / objects-to-think-with in identity and computing), building on the same 1980 source. **[confirmed as attribution]**
