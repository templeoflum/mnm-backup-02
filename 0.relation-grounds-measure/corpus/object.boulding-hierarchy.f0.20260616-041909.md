**statement (plain)** — Boulding's "Skeleton of Science" arranges systems in a ladder of increasing complexity, each level adding capabilities the ones below lack:
1. **Frameworks** — static structure (a map, an anatomy).
2. **Clockworks** — simple dynamic systems with predetermined motion (the solar system, a machine).
3. **Control / thermostat** — cybernetic systems that hold a variable to a target via feedback.
4. **Open systems / cell** — self-maintaining structures with throughput; the threshold of life.
5. **Genetic-societal / plant** — division of labor among differentiated parts, a blueprint.
6. **Animal** — mobility, specialized receptors, a brain, awareness.
7. **Human** — self-consciousness, language, symbol use.
8. **Social organization** — systems of roles, communication, and shared value.
9. **Transcendental systems** — the ultimates and unknowables.

Boulding observed that adequate theoretical models existed only up to about the fourth level, thinning sharply above it — a deliberate map of where systems knowledge was strong and where it was missing. [[source.boulding-1956]] **[confirmed]**

**basis** — The hierarchy is Boulding's 1956 *Management Science* proposal, offered as one of two complementary routes to general systems theory (the other: looking for phenomena common across disciplines) [[source.boulding-1956]]; it is reproduced and discussed as a standard map of the field in survey treatments [[source.skyttner-2005]]. **Support**: it became a canonical teaching scaffold for systems thinking and a widely cited classification. **Limits**: the levels are a heuristic ordering, not a measured or formally derived scale; boundaries between levels (especially 4–6 and 8–9) are interpretive, and the ninth "transcendental" level is explicitly a placeholder for the unknown rather than a characterized class. **[confirmed]**
