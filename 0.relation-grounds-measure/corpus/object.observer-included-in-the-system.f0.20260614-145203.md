**statement (plain)** — there is no view from nowhere. Whoever describes a system is themselves a system, doing the describing — so the observer belongs inside the picture, not outside it. The old ideal of a detached, objective scientist looking in from beyond the glass is dropped: the observer is part of what they observe.

**statement** — second-order cybernetics is "the cybernetics of observing systems": any account of a system is produced by an observer who is themselves a cybernetic system, and that observer must be included within the domain of description rather than presumed to stand outside it. The role of the observer is acknowledged rather than disguised; the system under study and the observer form a coupled whole.

**basis** — von Foerster drew the distinction between first-order cybernetics (the cybernetics of *observed* systems — feedback, control, regulation studied from outside) and second-order cybernetics (the cybernetics of *observing* systems — including the observer who draws the distinctions). The principle is the load-bearing move of the whole field: self-reference is unavoidable because the theorist is one of the systems the theory must cover. It carries an ethical edge — the observer chooses the distinctions and the "desired states," and so bears responsibility for them.

**lineage** — the impulse and the phrase "cybernetics of cybernetics" came from Margaret Mead's 1967 address to the American Society for Cybernetics, published 1968 [[source.mead-1968]]. Heinz von Foerster codified the first/second-order distinction and the observer-inclusion principle, editing *Cybernetics of Cybernetics* (BCL Report 73.38, 1974) [[source.von-foerster-1974]] and gathering the mature statements in *Understanding Understanding* (Springer, 2003) [[source.von-foerster-2003]]. Credit is shared: Mead supplied title and impulse, von Foerster the formalization. Ranulph Glanville and Gordon Pask are associated later developers.
