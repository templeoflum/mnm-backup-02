**statement (plain)** — a nudge is any aspect of the choice architecture that alters people's behavior in a predictable way without forbidding any options or significantly changing their economic incentives. To count as a mere nudge, the intervention must be easy and cheap to avoid: putting fruit at eye level is a nudge; banning junk food is not. Canonical instances — automatic enrollment, default fund allocations, reminders, framing of feedback, simplification, and salience of information.

**basis** — methodological/empirical. Support: definition stated in [[source.thaler-sunstein-2008]] *Nudge*; the most-cited concrete nudge — switching retirement plans to automatic enrollment — is empirically demonstrated by [[source.madrian-shea-2001]], where participation rose sharply with no change to incentives, satisfying the definition exactly. Limits: the "easy to avoid / no significant incentive change" boundary is contested at the margins (when does a default become a mandate? are deceptive or attention-exploiting nudges legitimate?); effect sizes vary widely by domain and a 2022 meta-literature debate questions average effectiveness and publication bias. **[confirmed]**
