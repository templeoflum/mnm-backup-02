**statement (plain)** — an auditor should not simply trust what management says or what the records show. Professional skepticism is the working attitude of keeping a questioning mind, staying alert for signs that something might be misstated (whether by honest error or by fraud), and critically weighing the evidence rather than accepting it at face value — without assuming dishonesty either.

**statement** — professional skepticism is "an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions that may indicate possible misstatement due to fraud or error, and a critical assessment of audit evidence." It is one of the two pervasive principles underlying generally accepted auditing standards (the other being professional judgment), to be exercised throughout the engagement. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the wording is the AICPA definition in [[source.au-c-200]] (*Overall Objectives of the Independent Auditor and the Conduct of an Audit in Accordance With GAAS*); the IAASB gives a substantively identical definition in ISA 200. The IAASB Handbook phrasing — "an attitude that includes a questioning mind, being alert to conditions which may indicate possible misstatement due to error or fraud, and a critical assessment of evidence" — matches. The standards note skepticism guards against over-reliance on prior experience and against assuming management is necessarily honest or dishonest. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the principle is the ethical/epistemic spine of the tradition from Dicksee's verification ethic [[source.dicksee-1892]] forward; it was sharpened and made central by post-Enron and fraud-standard revisions, and is the attitude the [[object.audit-risk-model]] and [[object.audit-sampling]] operationalize into evidence-gathering.

**sources** — [[source.au-c-200]], [[source.arens-elder-beasley]], [[source.montgomerys-auditing]]; cross-checked against the ACCA / IAASB Handbook statement of the same definition.
