**statement (plain)** — The felt sense is a bodily, holistic awareness of a situation or problem — a single, often vague and pre-verbal bodily sensation (frequently in the chest or abdomen) that carries the meaning of a whole situation at once, distinct from a discrete emotion or a verbal thought. In Gendlin's Focusing method, the practitioner attends to the felt sense and lets it shift ("felt shift") toward a more articulate, resolved sense.

**basis** — Defined by Gendlin in *Focusing* (1978), grounded in his philosophy of experiencing and in psychotherapy-process research correlating clients' bodily-referent attention with therapeutic outcome [[source.gendlin-1978]] **[confirmed as attribution]**. The felt sense is a clinical/phenomenological account of attending to internal bodily state and thus a precursor concept within the experiential strand of interoception; Craig's homeostatic-afferent model (2002) supplies the neuroscientific framing of an integrated bodily feeling that such introspective attention plausibly engages [[source.craig-2002]] **[confirmed]**.

*Limits* — "felt sense" is a clinical/phenomenological construct, not an experimentally operationalized measure; its mapping onto neural interoceptive pathways or onto measured interoceptive accuracy is theorized and partial, not demonstrated. The link between Focusing practice and objective interoceptive accuracy is an open empirical question, not an established result.
