**origin** — I. M. Lewis, *Ecstatic Religion* (1971) — the classic comparative anthropology that frames spirit possession and shamanism by their social function, distinguishing central (establishment) from peripheral (protest) cults. [[source.lewis-1971]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Erika Bourguignon (ed.), *Religion, Altered States of Consciousness, and Social Change* (1973) — the large-sample report of the Cross-Cultural Study of Dissociational States; gives the field its quantitative spine (488 societies; ≈90% institutionalize ASC) and its core analytic split between possession and possession trance. [[source.bourguignon-1973]] **[confirmed]**

**door** — T. M. Luhrmann, *When God Talks Back* (2012) — an accessible modern ethnography that recasts ecstatic experience as learned, attention-trained perception (absorption), connecting the classic anthropology to cognitive science; Felicitas Goodman's *Speaking in Tongues* (1972) is the standard companion on glossolalia as a trance artifact. [[source.luhrmann-2012]] · [[source.goodman-1972]] **[confirmed]**

lineage — the field's interpretive frames are contested: Lewis's deprivation/marginality thesis was challenged on empirical (e.g. Brazilian) data and is now held as partial rather than complete; the "altered states" framing itself is debated against more performative and constructionist accounts of possession. The phenomena are recorded here as ethnographically studied, with the truth of insider claims bracketed.

*grade [s] — origin (Lewis) + codification (Bourguignon) + door (Luhrmann / Goodman).*
