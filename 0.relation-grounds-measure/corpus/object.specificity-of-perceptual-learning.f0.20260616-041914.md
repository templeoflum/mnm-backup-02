**statement (plain)** — gains from perceptual practice frequently fail to generalize: improvement on a trained discrimination is often specific to the trained retinal location, stimulus orientation, and sometimes the trained eye. Because such attributes are segregated only at early stages of the visual system, strong specificity has been read as evidence that perceptual learning can change processing in early sensory cortex (e.g. primary visual cortex), not only higher-level decision stages.

**basis** — Karni & Sagi (1991) found texture-discrimination learning that was retinotopically local, specific for background-element orientation, and strongly monocular (little interocular transfer), arguing for early visual cortex plasticity [[source.karni-sagi-1991]] **[confirmed]**. Goldstone (1998) reviews specificity as a signature of low-level perceptual change [[source.goldstone-1998]] and Kellman & Garrigan (2009) discuss specificity vs. transfer in expertise and instruction [[source.kellman-garrigan-2009]] **[confirmed]**. **Limits:** specificity is not universal — degree of transfer depends on task difficulty, training regime, attention, and stimulus precision; later "double-training" and reverse-hierarchy accounts show specificity can be reduced or eliminated, so it indexes conditions of learning rather than a fixed locus.

**sources** — [[source.karni-sagi-1991]]; [[source.goldstone-1998]]; [[source.kellman-garrigan-2009]].
