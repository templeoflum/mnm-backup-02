**statement (plain)** — General systems theory proposes that systems — whether physical, biological, psychological, or social — share structural and dynamic principles that can be stated formally and independently of the particular kind of thing the system is made of. Where two unlike systems obey laws of the same form, the laws are *isomorphic*, and the shared form is the proper object of study. The aim is a body of theory situated between pure mathematics (too general) and the specialized sciences (too particular). [[source.von-bertalanffy-1968]] [[source.boulding-1956]] **[confirmed]**

**basis** — Bertalanffy generalized from his organismic biology and the theory of open systems: a living organism is an open system in a steady state, exhibiting growth, regulation, and equifinality, and these features recur across domains [[source.von-bertalanffy-1950]]. Boulding's "Skeleton of Science" reframes the program as a nine-level hierarchy of system complexity and as a search for general statements true at each level [[source.boulding-1956]]. **Support**: the program seeded systems thinking across biology, management, ecology, engineering, and the social sciences, and institutionalized in 1954 as the Society for General Systems Research. **Limits**: the framework is a research program and conceptual vocabulary rather than a predictive theory; critics note that the "isomorphisms" are often analogies whose explanatory force varies by case, and that much rigorous content migrated into the more tractable special fields (cybernetics, control theory, dynamical systems, network theory). **[confirmed]**
