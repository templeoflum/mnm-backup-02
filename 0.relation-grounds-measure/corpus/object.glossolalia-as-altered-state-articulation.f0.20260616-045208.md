**statement (plain)** — Goodman proposed that the characteristic accent, intonation, and phonetic features of glossolalia — which recur with surprising agreement across unrelated cultures — are not the marks of some shared language but expressions of bodily and neurophysiological changes the speaker undergoes in an altered (trance) state, which may accompany or even facilitate the religious experience.

**basis (empirical)** — Goodman's cross-cultural fieldwork (apostolic congregations in Mexico City and Indiana, Maya in the Yucatán, comparison samples from non-Christian rituals) found that the phonological and suprasegmental structure of glossolalia is stereotyped within a group and shows cross-cultural regularity, which she ties to a trance state rather than to language transmission [[source.goodman-1972]] [confirmed].

**lineage (contested)** — Samarin disputes the strong altered-state account: from his corpus he argues glossolalia "is not simply a product of dissociative states, like trance," noting practitioners can produce it volitionally and outside trance [[source.samarin-1972]] [confirmed]. The position is therefore established as a real and influential reading of the evidence, with Samarin's volitional/learned-behavior account standing as the rival. The degree to which altered states are necessary, facilitating, or incidental remains open.

**limits** — "Trance"/"altered state" is itself an interpretive construct measured indirectly; the cross-cultural regularity is robust as description, but its causal attribution to an altered state is the contested step. Recorded as a studied position, not asserted as settled.
