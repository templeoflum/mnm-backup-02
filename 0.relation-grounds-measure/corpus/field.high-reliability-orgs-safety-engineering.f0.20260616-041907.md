The study of safety in complex high-hazard systems grew from two opposed founding claims and a parallel cognitive-error tradition; modern work synthesizes them through systems engineering.

**origin** — Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies* (Basic Books, 1984), written after the 1979 Three Mile Island accident, founds **Normal Accident Theory**: in systems that are both interactively complex and tightly coupled, catastrophic accidents are structurally inevitable. [[source.perrow-1984]] **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the field has a contested second founding. The UC Berkeley **high reliability organization** program (Rochlin, La Porte & Roberts, "The Self-Designing High-Reliability Organization," *Naval War College Review*, 1987; Roberts, *Organization Science*, 1990) inverted Perrow's question — asking how some hazardous organizations stay nearly error-free — and reached the opposite, optimistic conclusion. [[source.roberts-1990]] Running alongside both is the cognitive-error tradition of Jens Rasmussen's skill-/rule-/knowledge framework (1983) and James Reason's *Human Error* (1990). [[source.rasmussen-1983]] [[source.reason-1990]] **[confirmed]**

**codification** — Karl Weick and Kathleen Sutcliffe, *Managing the Unexpected* (Jossey-Bass, 2001), distilled the HRO program into the five principles of collective **mindfulness**, giving the optimistic side a portable, teachable framework. [[source.weick-sutcliffe-2001]] **[confirmed]**

**door** — Nancy Leveson, Nicolas Dulac, Karen Marais & John Carroll, "Moving Beyond Normal Accidents and High Reliability Organizations," *Organization Studies* 30 (2009) 227–249, surveys the NAT-vs-HRO debate, names its limits, and points to the systems-engineering turn — a single entry into the whole field. [[source.leveson-2009]] **[confirmed]**

*grade [s] — origin (Perrow 1984) + codification (Weick & Sutcliffe 2001) + door (Leveson et al. 2009) all present; founding contested, captured as lineage (Berkeley HRO program + Reason/Rasmussen cognitive tradition).*
