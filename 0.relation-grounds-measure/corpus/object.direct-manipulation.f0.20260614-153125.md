**statement (plain)** — direct manipulation is an interaction style in which the user works on the objects of interest themselves — files, shapes, text — which stay continuously visible on screen, by physical-feeling actions (pointing, dragging, clicking) rather than typed command syntax, and where every action is rapid, incremental, reversible, and its effect is immediately visible. The model: see the thing, act on the thing, watch it respond.

**statement (formula)** — none; this is a qualitative design concept. Shneiderman names three defining principles: (1) continuous representation of the object of interest; (2) physical actions or labeled-button presses in place of complex syntax; (3) rapid, incremental, reversible operations whose effect on the object is immediately visible. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — design principle distilled from practice, not a derived or measured law. Shneiderman (1983) abstracted the common virtues of then-novel interactive systems (spreadsheets, display editors, video games) into the three principles, arguing they make novices learn fast, experts work fast, and reduce user anxiety because actions are reversible and comprehensible. Status established as a guiding concept; not formalized. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the term was coined by [[Ben Shneiderman]]; he used it in talks and writing in 1981–82 and gave the canonical statement in the 1983 *IEEE Computer* article "Direct Manipulation: A Step Beyond Programming Languages" [[source.shneiderman-1983]]. The idea draws on prior interactive systems — Sutherland's Sketchpad and the Xerox systems — and sits in the HCI canon of [[Card, Moran & Newell]] (1983) [[source.card-moran-newell-1983]]. **[confirmed]**
