**statement (plain)** — a high reliability organization (HRO) is one that *could* cause great harm to itself and its surroundings — a nuclear carrier flight deck, an air traffic control center, a nuclear power plant, a wildland fire crew — yet manages to operate for very long stretches with almost no serious accidents. The HRO program studies how they do it, treating near-error-free operation in a dangerous setting as the thing to be explained, the mirror image of Perrow's "accidents are inevitable."

**statement (framework)** — Weick and Sutcliffe codify HRO performance as **collective mindfulness**, sustained through five principles: (1) **preoccupation with failure** — treat small anomalies as symptoms; (2) **reluctance to simplify** — resist convenient interpretations; (3) **sensitivity to operations** — keep an accurate live picture of the front line; (4) **commitment to resilience** — build capacity to absorb and recover from error; (5) **deference to expertise** — migrate decisions to whoever has the most relevant knowledge, regardless of rank.

**basis** — empirical, from the Berkeley field studies of three sites: U.S. Navy aircraft-carrier flight operations, FAA air traffic control, and the Pacific Gas & Electric grid [[source.roberts-1990]], later distilled into the five-principle framework [[source.weick-sutcliffe-2001]]. *Support* — widely applied in aviation, healthcare, and nuclear operations. *Limits* — Leveson et al. argue the program conflates reliability with safety and is hard to falsify, and the NAT camp disputes whether observed safe periods prove accidents avoidable rather than merely deferred [[source.leveson-2009]]. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — [[source.roberts-1990]] (founding empirical statement, carrier type-case); [[source.weick-sutcliffe-2001]] (the five principles / mindfulness). Critique in [[source.leveson-2009]]. **[confirmed]**
