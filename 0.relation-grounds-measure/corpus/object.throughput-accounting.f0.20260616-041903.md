statement (plain): a decision-support accounting that rejects per-unit cost allocation in favor of three global measures:
- Throughput (T) — the rate at which the system generates money through sales (sales revenue minus totally variable cost, chiefly direct materials).
- Inventory / Investment (I) — all the money tied up in things the system intends to sell, including raw material, work-in-process, finished goods, and the money invested in equipment and facilities.
- Operating Expense (OE) — all the money the system spends turning inventory into throughput (e.g., payroll, rent, utilities, maintenance).
Decisions are judged by their effect on T, I, and OE together — increasing throughput is favored over local cost-cutting.

basis: the three measures are introduced in *The Goal* (1984) as the operational way to judge whether a decision moves the plant toward its goal of making money [[source.goldratt-cox-1984]] [confirmed]; throughput accounting is treated as an established component of TOC, with these definitions, in the 2010 Handbook [[source.cox-schleier-2010]] [confirmed].

limits: throughput accounting is a managerial-accounting method, established by use rather than proved, and is not a substitute for statutory financial reporting (which requires cost allocation, e.g. inventory valuation). Its short-run treatment of operating expense as largely fixed can mislead over longer horizons where labor and capacity are variable. [confirmed]
