## TARGET 2A — The Eleusinian Mysteries & the Incommunicable (Burkert)

### 2A.1 — Ritual structure: dromena / legomena / deiknymena

**What the sources say.** The reconstructed tripartite scheme of the Eleusinian rite divides the secret content into three categories:
- **dromena** (δρώμενα) — "things done/enacted/performed"; the root of the word "drama" (verb *draō*). Widely reconstructed as a dramatic re-enactment of the Demeter/Persephone myth.
- **legomena** (λεγόμενα) — "things said/spoken"; short liturgical statements, formulae, explanations, perhaps invocations accompanying the dromena.
- **deiknymena** (δεικνύμενα) — "things shown/revealed"; the display of sacred objects, in which the hierophant ("he who shows the sacred") played the essential role.

This tripartite structure is reconstructed from indirect references in classical and patristic authors; the source frequently cited for the scheme is Clement of Alexandria (Protrepticus 2.12). Scholars stress that no ancient source described the exact content of each category.

**Historical span.** The Mysteries were observed regularly from c. 1600 BCE until 392 CE, when the Christian emperor Theodosius I closed the sanctuaries by decree (reported by Eunapius); the sanctuary was destroyed by Alaric and the Goths in 396 CE.

**Canonical scholarship named in sources:** George E. Mylonas, *Eleusis and the Eleusinian Mysteries* (Princeton UP, 1961); Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion* (Harvard UP, 1985; German orig. 1977); Walter Burkert, *Ancient Mystery Cults* (Harvard UP, 1987); Karl Kerényi, *Eleusis* (1967). On the kykeon (ritual drink of barley, water, pennyroyal), the search material notes Burkert argued that fasting followed by a communal sacrificial meal could produce "communal bliss" without chemical/psychedelic explanation, and that the simpler non-psychedelic explanation is more compatible with a mass public religion lasting two millennia. The ergot/psychoactive hypothesis (Wasson–Hofmann–Ruck, *The Road to Eleusis*, 1978; popularized by Brian C. Muraresku, *The Immortality Key: The Secret History of the Religion with No Name*, St. Martin's Press, 2020, which Muraresku characterizes as "the pagan continuity hypothesis with a psychedelic twist") is described in rigorous-adjacent sources as **contested/unproven**.

### 2A.2 — The arrheton / aporrheton: why the secret was kept, and whether there was doctrinal "content" at all

**Terminology (philological).** Two adjectives recur in ancient usage: **arrheton** (ἄρρητον, "unspeakable") and **aporrheton** (ἀπόρρητον, "forbidden"). Krzysztof Bielawski, "Arrheton — Unspeakable in the Greek Mystery Cults" (*Filozofia Chrześcijańska* 13, 2016, pp. 37–52) catalogues the evidence (Aristotle, Xenophon, Herodotus, Aristophanes, Euripides, Sophocles, Apuleius, the Derveni Papyrus, and early Christian writers) and treats the two terms within "Greek religion" framed as an adogmatic, performance- and ritual-centered system.

**Burkert's framing (verbatim, *Ancient Mystery Cults*, 1987, Introduction).** Burkert treats the two adjectives as nearly interchangeable and identifies the structural paradox of mystery secrecy. On p. 9 he writes that *aporrheta* ("forbidden") and *arrheta* ("unspeakable") "seem to be nearly interchangeable in this usage, hinting at a basic problem inherent in the 'secret' of mysteries: a mystery must not be betrayed, but it cannot really be betrayed because told in public it would appear insignificant; thus violations of the secrecy that did occur did no harm to the institutions, but protection of the secrecy greatly added to the prestige of the most sacred cults." In *Greek Religion* (1985, p. 207) he leaves open "whether in mysteries the sacred was forbidden, aporrheton, or unspeakable, arrheton in an absolute sense" — i.e., whether secrecy was legal/cultic prohibition or a deeper ineffability.

**The pathein/mathein debate (experience vs. doctrine).** The pivotal ancient testimony is **Aristotle, *On Philosophy*, fragment 15 (Rose)**, preserved/paraphrased by **Synesius of Cyrene, *Dio* 10**: initiates "did not learn anything (*ou mathein*) but experienced/suffered (*pathein*) and were put into a certain disposition/state of mind." Multiple scholarly and reference sources render this as *ou mathein ti dein alla pathein*. Nancy A. Evans ("Diotima and Demeter as Mystagogues in Plato's Symposium," *Hypatia* 21.2, 2006, p. 19) discusses the fragment, glossing the Mysteries as experiential rather than didactic. The older view that the Mysteries enshrined a secret doctrine (e.g., secret monotheism) was, per the sources, "destroyed" by C. A. Lobeck's *Aglaophamus* (1829); W. R. Inge and the *International Standard Bible Encyclopaedia* both note Plutarch (*De defectu oraculorum* 22) saying initiates could produce no demonstration or proof of their acquired beliefs. **Scholarly consensus reported:** there was likely no transmissible dogmatic "content"; the rites worked by inducing an experience/disposition, not by transferring propositions. NOTE: Burkert's own sentence discussing fr. 15 sits in Ch. IV ("The Extraordinary Experience," pp. 89 ff.); my dedicated quote-finder could not load that page from the oversized scan, so I do not quote Burkert verbatim on the fragment — flagged as a gap.

### 2A.3 — Initiation as transformation-by-passage (myesis / telete / epopteia; mystes / epoptes)

**Key terms (as scholars reconstruct them):**
- **myesis** (μύησις) — initiation; etymologically linked to *myein*, "to close" (the eyes/lips), connoting secrecy. Sometimes "initiation in general," the total experience of the mystes.
- **telete** (τελετή) — the rite/ceremony; from the root *telos* ("goal/completion"); "to make perfect/complete." Robert M. Simms ("Myesis, Telete, and Mysteria," *Greek, Roman, and Byzantine Studies*) cautions that *telete* does not denote merely "the first-degree ceremony" and that *teleō* does not simply mean "to initiate."
- **epopteia** (ἐποπτεία) — the highest grade, "viewing/beholding"; from *ops* ("eye"). The initiate becomes an **epoptes** (ἐπόπτης, "one who has seen"), as distinct from the first-time **mystes** (μύστης).

There is genuine scholarly debate about the number and sequence of stages. H. Pringsheim (1905) influentially argued that *myesis* was a distinct preliminary "pre-initiation" (Einweihung) conducted individually by the Eumolpidai and Kerykes gene; Simms scrutinizes and partly resists that separation. Plutarch (*Demetrius* 26) is the source for myesis preceding epopteia. (A Proclus-derived scheme listing telete/muesis/epopteia circulates, but the Theosophy World source carrying it is non-scholarly and is flagged, not relied upon.)

**What initiation DID (per scholars).** Burkert frames mystery initiation as a personal change of state rather than information transfer. In *Ancient Mystery Cults* (Introduction, p. 8) he writes that ancient mysteries form a "special category" — not puberty rites, not strong-tie secret societies (except Mithras), with "no visible change of outward status"; "the change of status affects his relation to a god or a goddess… a personal change, a new state of mind through experience of the sacred." His summary definition (p. 11): "Mysteries were initiation rituals of a voluntary, personal, and secret character that aimed at a change of mind through experience of the sacred." On the experience-vs-creed point (Ch. II, p. 43): "The unity of the group has been in action and in experience, not in faith. There is no Credo." And p. 46: "in Christianity there are articles of faith… whereas in the mysteries there is reference to a sequence of rituals that have taken place… But ritual does not need explicit theology to be effective." The promised benefit was a better hope for the afterlife, attested verbatim at *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 480–482 (Evelyn-White trans., Loeb/Harvard UP, 1914): "Happy is he among men upon earth who has seen these mysteries; but he who is uninitiate and who has no part in them, never has lot of like good things once he is dead, down in the darkness and gloom."

## TARGET 2B — Esotericism as "a Form of Thought" (Faivre; Hanegraaff)

### 2B.1 — Faivre's four intrinsic + two secondary characteristics

From Antoine Faivre, *Access to Western Esotericism* (SUNY Press, 1994; translating *Accès de l'ésotérisme occidental*), and restated in *Theosophy, Imagination, Tradition* (2000) and *Western Esotericism: A Concise History* (SUNY, 2010, trans. Christine Rhone). Faivre defines esotericism as "an ensemble of spiritual currents in modern and contemporary Western history which share a certain air de famille, as well as the form of thought which is its common denominator" (Faivre 1998, p. 2). He identifies six characteristics; **four "intrinsic"** (their simultaneous presence is "a necessary and sufficient condition for a discourse to be identified as esoteric") and **two "secondary"/non-intrinsic**:

**Intrinsic:**
1. **Correspondences** — "Non-'causal' correspondences operate between all the levels of reality of the universe," a "theater of mirrors" / "mosaic of hieroglyphs to be decoded"; everything in Nature is a sign. Includes macrocosm/microcosm (planets/body), and correspondences between Nature/history and revealed texts (*physica sacra*). Faivre: the principles of noncontradiction and excluded middle are "replaced by those of synchronicity and included middle."
2. **Living Nature** — the cosmos is "permeated with invisible but active forces"; Nature as a living organism/person with a history (relevant to magia, alchemy).
3. **Imagination and Mediations** — rituals, symbols, intermediary spirits/angels function as "mediations" providing "passages between different levels of reality"; the "active"/"creative"/"magical" imagination makes them "a tool of knowledge (gnosis)." "The eye of fire that pierces the bark of appearances and renders the invisible visible."
4. **Experience of Transmutation** — confers an "experiential" character; "the transformation of oneself, which can be a 'second birth'," with a corollary transmutation of part of Nature (e.g., alchemy). The Project MUSE review glosses this as "a kind of second birth through 'gnosis,' a personal form of knowing that erases the distinction between wisdom and faith."

**Secondary (non-intrinsic; "may be absent"):**
5. **Praxis of Concordance** — "positing a priori that common denominators can exist among several different traditions, indeed among all of them, and then… comparing them with a view to finding a higher truth that overhangs them."
6. **Transmission** — emphasis on "channels of transmission" (since the 18th c.); "transmission from master to disciple, from the initiator to the 'initiable' (self-initiation is not possible)"; validity tied to an "affiliation whose authenticity ('regularity')" is considered genuine. McCalla's overview restates this as: "Esoteric teaching passes from master to disciple through initiation. The mark of authenticity of esoteric knowledge is filiation to an established line of transmission."

### 2B.2 — Hanegraaff: "rejected knowledge," empirical-historical method, critique of religionism

Wouter J. Hanegraaff (Professor of History of Hermetic Philosophy, University of Amsterdam; President, ESSWE; member, Royal Dutch Academy), *Esotericism and the Academy: Rejected Knowledge in Western Culture* (Cambridge UP, 2012, 478 pp.).

**"Rejected knowledge" / "waste-basket."** Hanegraaff argues that, in the wake of the Enlightenment, Western esotericism was conceptualized as a "waste-basket category" for domains of knowledge rejected by disenchanted science (the phrase appears around p. 254; "waste-basket of history" also p. 375). The currents covered (alchemy, astrology, magic, divination, illumination, clairvoyance, symbolism, the occult) were defined in "sharp contrast" to what scholars constructed as biblical monotheism and Greek rationality. Per reviewers, Protestant theologians and Enlightenment scholars "joined hands to proclaim their own modern rationality while relegating esoteric currents to the 'waste-basket.'" These traditions became "the Other by which academics define their identity."

**Mnemohistory.** Hanegraaff adopts Jan Assmann's distinction between **history** ("what happened") and **mnemohistory** ("what a given culture imagines has happened"), arguing esotericism became "a casualty of mnemohistory."

**Method.** His approach is **empirical-historical**, grounded "in a meticulous study of primary and secondary sources," and explicitly **anti-religionist/anti-essentialist** (he professes "nominalism/anti-essentialism"). Critics (e.g., the *Religion* 43.2 [2013] critique "Deconstructing 'Western esotericism'") note tensions: the "radically historicist approach" leads to the "atomization" of esotericism into disparate currents with few shared elements, and a "vacillation between seeing 'esotericism' as merely a waste-basket category and attempting nonetheless to salvage a minimal substantive definition." A book-length study, *Esotericism, Gnosis, Imagination: An Analysis of Wouter J. Hanegraaff's Core Concepts, 1988–2020*, addresses these inconsistencies and his mixing of Lovejoy's history-of-ideas with discourse theory.

### 2B.3 — Gnosis as a distinct mode of knowing (vs. reason/logos and faith/pistis)

Hanegraaff, "Reason, Faith, and Gnosis: Potentials and Problematics of a Typological Construct" (2008, in *Clashes of Knowledge*, Knowledge and Space vol. 1, Springer). Against an older approach associated chiefly with **Gilles Quispel**, Hanegraaff argues the triad "reason—faith—gnosis" should NOT describe actual historical currents but may serve as an **analytical typology** of claimed knowledge:
- **Reason** (logos): knowledge that is "both communicable and verifiable/falsifiable."
- **Faith** (pistis): "communicable but not verifiable/falsifiable."
- **Gnosis**: "a superior type of knowledge that is neither communicable nor verifiable/falsifiable" — the structurally key claim for the transmission problem. Hanegraaff (citing Corpus Hermeticum IX, 10, in Copenhaver 1992, p. 29): "the actual gnosis is a gift from God and has a content that can no longer be communicated but only beheld directly by some faculty beyond the senses and reason."

**Wider historiography of Gnosticism.** Elaine Pagels (*The Gnostic Gospels*, pp. xviii–xix): gnosis "is not primarily rational knowledge… for gnosis involves an intuitive process of knowing oneself… to know oneself, at the deepest level, is to know God." On the pistis/gnosis distinction in Valentinianism: pistis = "intellectual and emotional acceptance of a proposition"; gnosis = experiential "insight." Reference work: the two-volume *Dictionary of Gnosis and Western Esotericism* (Hanegraaff, ed., in collaboration with Faivre, van den Broek & Brach, 2005). **Contested-figure flag:** April DeConick (*The Gnostic New Age*) argues that Gnosticism scholarship "is still entrenched in an Eliadian phenomenological paradigm which essentializes an ahistorical sui generis 'Gnosis'" — a critique aligned with the anti-religionist camp (links Target 2B to the Eliade critiques in 2C.3).

## TARGET 2C — Tacit/Experiential Knowing & the Private-Language Problem (the transmission gap)

### 2C.1 — Experiential knowledge resists propositional transfer (Polanyi; Russell; Ryle)

**Michael Polanyi**, *The Tacit Dimension* (1966): "I shall reconsider human knowledge by starting from the fact that we can know more than we can tell" (p. 4). The **proximal/distal (from–to) structure**: we attend *from* subsidiary particulars (proximal term, subsidiary awareness) *to* a focal whole (distal term, focal awareness); shifting attention onto the subsidiaries destroys the meaning (e.g., focusing on finger movements disrupts fluent typing/piano-playing). Polanyi: "While tacit knowledge can be possessed by itself, explicit knowledge must rely on being tacitly understood and applied. Hence all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable" (*The Tacit Dimension*). Also *Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy* (1958): "Personal participation is the universal principle of knowing." (MIT economist David Autor coined "Polanyi's Paradox" in 2014 for the AI/automation context — a secondary, non-philosophical extension, flagged as such.)

**Bertrand Russell**, "Knowledge by Acquaintance and Knowledge by Description" (paper read to the Aristotelian Society, 6 March 1911; published 1910–11 in *Proceedings of the Aristotelian Society*) and *The Problems of Philosophy* (1912, ch. 5). Russell: "We shall say that we have acquaintance with anything of which we are directly aware, without the intermediary of any process of inference or any knowledge of truths" (1912, p. 78). Knowledge by acquaintance = direct, unmediated, non-inferential (sense-data, memory, introspection, the self "to an extent," universals); knowledge by description = indirect, mediated, inferential, "secondhand." The acquaintance principle: "every proposition which we can understand must be composed wholly of constituents with which we are acquainted" (1912, p. 58). Predecessors noted in the literature: John Grote ("knowledge of acquaintance"), Hermann von Helmholtz (1868: *das Kennen* vs *das Wissen* — the *Kennen* kind "can not be expressed in words, even to ourselves"), and William James (1890). The SEP and IEP entries note the contemporary debate centers on whether there is "privileged unmediated knowledge by acquaintance" (classical foundationalism).

**Gilbert Ryle**, *The Concept of Mind* (1949): the **knowing-how vs. knowing-that** distinction — practical competence ("knowing how") is not reducible to, and does not require prior grasp of, propositional knowledge ("knowing that"). (Ryle's anti-intellectualist thesis; later contested by Jason Stanley & Timothy Williamson's intellectualism, "Knowing How," *Journal of Philosophy* 2001 — the major opposing position.)

### 2C.2 — Wittgenstein's private-language argument (PI §§243–315; beetle §293; Kripke 1982)

**Ludwig Wittgenstein**, *Philosophical Investigations* (1953, composed mainly 1936–45). Per the **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy** entry "Private Language" (Stewart Candlish & George Wrisley; first pub. 1996, substantive revision 30 July 2019):

- **Definition (§243, quoted in SEP):** "The words of this language are to refer to what only the speaker can know — to his immediate private sensations. So another person cannot understand the language." SEP stresses this is NOT a personal code (which "could in principle be deciphered") but "a language conceived as *necessarily* comprehensible only to its single originator because the things which define its vocabulary are necessarily inaccessible to others."
- **What's at stake (SEP):** "an unformulated reliance on the possibility of a private language is arguably essential to mainstream epistemology, philosophy of mind and metaphysics from Descartes to versions of the representational theory of mind."
- **The conclusion (SEP §1):** "a language in principle unintelligible to anyone but its originating user is impossible… such a so-called language would, necessarily, be unintelligible to its supposed originator too, for he would be unable to establish meanings for its putative signs."
- **The diarist / sensation-"S" (§258):** the attempt to give meaning to a sign "S" by private ostensive definition (concentrating attention on a recurring sensation and writing "S" in a diary) fails because no criterion of correctness can be established; Wittgenstein (§258): "whatever is going to seem correct to me is correct. And that only means that here we can't talk about 'correct'." (§265: "As if someone were to buy several copies of today's morning paper to assure himself that what it said was true.")
- **Beetle-in-a-box (§293):** "Suppose everyone had a box with something in it: we call it a 'beetle'. No one can look into anyone else's box, and everyone says he knows what a beetle is only by looking at his beetle." Conclusion: "the thing in the box doesn't belong to the language-game at all… one can 'divide through' by the thing in the box; it cancels out, whatever it is." Standard scholarly reading: public sensation-words do not get their meaning from the private inner object; the object "drops out of consideration." **Important caveat:** the current SEP "Private Language" entry does NOT expound §293 directly — it references the beetle only via Cora Diamond's title and a bibliography entry (David Stern, "The uses of Wittgenstein's beetle: *Philosophical Investigations* §293 and its interpreters"). The §293 text above is from Wittgenstein's *PI* directly (corroborated across multiple expository sources); attribute it to *PI* §293, not to the SEP private-language entry.
- **Kripke's interpretation:** Saul Kripke, *Wittgenstein on Rules and Private Language: An Elementary Exposition* (Harvard UP, 1982). Kripke reframes the core as a **skeptical paradox about rule-following/meaning** (PI §201: "no course of action could be determined by a rule, because every course of action can be brought into accord with the rule"). The "quus"/plus example: nothing in past use determines whether one meant *plus* or the deviant *quus*. Kripke calls this "the most radical and original skeptical problem that philosophy has seen to date" (p. 60); he offers a **"skeptical solution"** (modeled on Hume) grounded in **communal agreement / assertibility conditions**: "The impossibility of private language emerges as a corollary of [Wittgenstein's] sceptical solution of his own paradox" (p. 68). Kripke himself doubts the interpretation is the historical Wittgenstein's ("Kripkenstein"). SEP: "Kripke's Wittgenstein, real or fictional, has become a philosopher in his own right." Most commentators accept the rule-following paradox as Kripke presents it but "few have concurred in attributing Kripke's skeptical solution to Wittgenstein." SEP's critical verdict: Kripke's account is "vitiated by his unargued reliance on ideas which Wittgenstein argued against," though it "gave new life to the debate over the community view." (Recent guide: Ali Hossein Khani, *Kripke's Wittgenstein: Meaning, Rules, and Scepticism*.)

### 2C.3 — Eliade on initiation as ontological passage; and the major critiques (presented together)

**Eliade's claim.** Mircea Eliade, *Rites and Symbols of Initiation: The Mysteries of Birth and Rebirth* (Harper & Brothers, 1958; first titled *Birth and Rebirth*) and *The Sacred and the Profane: The Nature of Religion* (1957; Eng. trans. Willard R. Trask, 1959; this text dated "Saint-Cloud, April 1956"). Eliade frames initiation as a **change of existential/ontological status, not an information transfer**: initiation "result[s] in a change of existential status," transforming the novice "into another person" via "a crucial revelation of the world and life." Initiatory **death-and-rebirth**: "death" = a "temporary return to Chaos," "the paradigmatic expression of the end of a mode of being — the mode of ignorance and of the child's irresponsibility"; "initiatory death provides the clean slate on which will be written the successive revelations whose end is the formation of a new man." "It is only in initiation that death is given a positive value." Initiatory spaces (huts, caves, chambers) represent "both the grave and the womb." In *The Sacred and the Profane*, the key concepts are **the sacred**, **hierophany** (the manifestation of the sacred; "the manifestation of the sacred ontologically founds the world"), and **homo religiosus**. For religious man, space and time are non-homogeneous; the sacred is "an absolute reality, opposed to the nonreality of the vast surrounding expanse." Method: explicitly comparative and (by his own admission in the preface) NOT historicist — "the writer, in citing examples, has not undertaken to indicate their historico-cultural contexts."

**The major scholarly critiques (named):**
- **Jonathan Z. Smith** — *Map Is Not Territory: Studies in the History of Religions* (1978) and *To Take Place: Toward Theory in Ritual* (1987). In *To Take Place*, Smith's "close examination of Eliade's influential interpretation of the Tjilpa [Achilpa] tradition leads to a powerful critique of the approach to religion, myth, and ritual that begins with cosmology and the category of 'The Sacred.'" Smith stresses constructed/local place over universal cosmology, and caught factual/interpretive problems in Eliade's use of the Achilpa sacred-pole example. *Map Is Not Territory* "critically evaluates the notion of sacred space and time as it is represented in the works of Mircea Eliade."
- **Russell T. McCutcheon** — *Manufacturing Religion: The Discourse on Sui Generis Religion and the Politics of Nostalgia* (Oxford UP, 1997). Attacks the "sui generis" claim (religion as "distinct, unique, and self-caused") associated with Eliade and the Chicago History of Religions school, arguing it constitutes the object of study as "ahistoric, apolitical, fetishized, and sacrosanct," serving institutional/ideological interests ("skyhooks," "politics of nostalgia"). Targets the privileging of "the sacred," "religious experience," and "hierophanies" as autonomous, irreducible data. McCutcheon: scholars "manufacture a historically isolated and impenetrable datum known variously as power, religion an sich, the sacred."
- **Steven M. Wasserstrom** — *Religion after Religion: Gershom Scholem, Mircea Eliade, and Henry Corbin at Eranos* (Princeton UP, 1999). Argues the three Eranos thinkers "overturned traditional approaches… by de-emphasizing law, ritual, and social history and by extolling" the symbolism of mystical experience; "the most controversial aspect of their theory of religion… is that it minimized the binding character of moral law associated with monotheism." Wasserstrom reads their program as crypto-theological/esoteric. (Note: Wasserstrom was himself criticized in turn — e.g., Gustavo Benavides charged he "becomes a theologian, an advocate of Yahweh… of monotheism" — so the critique is itself contested.)
- General methodological charge across these critics (and Benjamin Beit-Hallahmi): scholars of religion in the Eliadean mode tend to become "caretakers," "celebrators," even "collaborators" with the religions they study, defending the believer's self-understanding rather than explaining it.

**Eliade as NAMED CONTESTED figure:** sources uniformly treat his "religionist"/anti-reductionist, universalizing, ahistorical comparativism as the principal target of the post-1970s methodological turn in religious studies.
