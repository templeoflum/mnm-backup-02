Charles Perrow, *Normal Accidents: Living with High-Risk Technologies* (Basic Books, New York, 1984) — the founding statement of **Normal Accident Theory (NAT)**. Written by a Yale organizational sociologist after he was drawn into analysis of the 1979 Three Mile Island accident, the book argues that systems with two properties — **interactive complexity** (parts interact in unplanned, non-linear, often invisible ways) and **tight coupling** (little slack; failures propagate fast with no time to intervene) — will eventually suffer "normal" or "system" accidents that no amount of added safety devices or operator skill can design away. Adding redundancy can even raise complexity and make things worse.

Contains — the interactive-complexity × coupling framework and its four-quadrant placement of industries (nuclear plants, chemical plants, aircraft, marine, dams, mining, etc.); case analyses of Three Mile Island, petrochemical plants, aviation, marine transport, and dams/mines; later editions add an afterword treating Bhopal, Chernobyl, and Challenger.

Access — a 1999 Princeton University Press edition (ISBN 978-0-691-00412-9) adds a new afterword and a postscript on the Y2K problem; widely held in libraries and available via the Internet Archive. **[confirmed]**
