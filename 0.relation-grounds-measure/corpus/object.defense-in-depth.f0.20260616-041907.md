**statement (plain)** — never trust a single safeguard. Stack several independent barriers between a hazard and a disaster — physical containment, automatic shutdowns, alarms, procedures, trained operators, emergency response — so that if one fails, another still stands. Human and mechanical failures are assumed unavoidable; the layers are there to compensate for them.

**statement (principle)** — defense in depth provides **multiple, independent, redundant, and diverse** layers of prevention and mitigation, designed so that no single point of failure (and ideally no single common cause) can defeat the whole. In nuclear engineering it is organized into successive levels — prevent deviations, detect and control them, control accidents within design basis, manage severe accidents, mitigate offsite consequences. Reason's organizational-accident model casts these layers as the imperfect, hole-bearing "slices" whose alignment a designer is trying to make vanishingly improbable.

**basis** — the term enters U.S. nuclear-safety usage by the late 1950s–1960s (applied to civilian reactor safety in a 1965 AEC statement) and is now codified by regulators (e.g. U.S. NRC) as multiple independent and redundant layers of protection **[confirmed as attribution]** (regulatory/secondary histories; primary AEC documents not opened here). Its organizational-accident form — defenses as layered, dynamically imperfect barriers — is Reason's [[source.reason-1990]]. *Limits* — Perrow's Normal Accident Theory warns that added layers raise interactive complexity and can introduce new common-cause failure paths, so defense in depth is not unboundedly protective; Leveson et al. press the same point from a systems-engineering view [[source.leveson-2009]]. **[confirmed]**
