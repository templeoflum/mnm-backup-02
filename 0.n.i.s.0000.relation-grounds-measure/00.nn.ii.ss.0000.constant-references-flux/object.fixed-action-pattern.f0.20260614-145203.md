**statement (plain)** — some animal behaviors are pre-wired action sequences: a specific cue (a "sign stimulus" or releaser) flips a switch in the nervous system, and once it fires the whole behavior plays out the same way, to the end, even if the cue disappears or conditions change. The switch is the "innate releasing mechanism" (IRM); the action it lets loose is the "fixed action pattern" (FAP). They're species-typical and don't have to be learned.

**statement** — a **fixed action pattern (FAP)** (Lorenz's *Instinkthandlung*) is an instinctive, highly stereotyped, species-characteristic motor sequence that, once released, runs to completion largely independent of further sensory feedback. It is triggered by a **sign stimulus / releaser** — a small set of key features of an object rather than the whole object — acting through an **innate releasing mechanism (IRM)**, a hard-wired perceptual filter linking the releaser to the motor program. An exaggerated, artificially intensified releaser is a **supernormal stimulus**. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — an empirical/theoretical construct of classical ethology, supported by manipulation experiments: Tinbergen's three-spined stickleback (a red underside releases attack; a swollen belly releases courtship, responding to crude models over realistic ones) and the Lorenz–Tinbergen greylag-goose egg-rolling response (released by size/convexity, so a goose will roll a volleyball). The supernormal-stimulus result — dummies more effective than the natural object — is the signature evidence that the response keys on isolable features. The strict "ballistic, feedback-independent" form of the concept has since been softened in modern neuroethology, but FAP/IRM remain foundational vocabulary. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — **Konrad Lorenz** coined *fixed action pattern* (*Instinkthandlung*) and the releaser / innate-releasing-schema framework; **Niko Tinbergen** coined the term *innate releasing mechanism* and supplied the decisive field experiments. Developed jointly through the 1930s–1950s as the core of classical ethology. Primary: [[source.lorenz-1935]] (the Kumpan paper) and its English condensation [[source.lorenz-1937]]; codified for the anglophone discipline in [[source.tinbergen-1951]] (*The Study of Instinct*). Lorenz/Tinbergen/von Frisch shared the 1973 Nobel. **[confirmed]**
