**statement (plain)** — before any science, theory, or reflection, we already live in a world we take for granted — the world of everyday perception, action, and shared meaning. This "lifeworld" is the ever-present ground beneath all our activity, including science itself. Husserl argued that modern science had forgotten this ground, and that recovering it was the cure for the "crisis" of the European sciences.

**statement** — the *lifeworld* (*Lebenswelt*) is the pre-given, intuitively present world of lived experience — historical, cultural, intersubjective — that exists "in advance for us" as the ground of all praxis, theoretical or extra-theoretical. Husserl names it (esp. §9 of the *Crisis*) the "forgotten meaning-fundament of natural science": the objective, mathematized world of Galilean science is an idealizing achievement *built upon* the lifeworld, which it then mistakes for self-subsistent reality. The lifeworld is foundational for both the natural and the human sciences. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — a descriptive, foundational concept, not a formal result; its warrant is phenomenological description of the structure of pre-scientific experience and the genetic dependence of scientific idealizations upon it.

**lineage** — introduced in *Die Krisis der europäischen Wissenschaften* (*The Crisis of European Sciences and Transcendental Phenomenology*, 1936) [[source.husserl-1936]], Husserl's last major work. The lifeworld is the world-correlate of the [[object.natural-attitude]] made into an explicit theme; its uncovering reframes the phenomenological project historically and motivates a distinct "way into" the reduction. Taken up widely after Husserl — e.g. by Schütz in sociology and Habermas in social theory. **[confirmed]**
