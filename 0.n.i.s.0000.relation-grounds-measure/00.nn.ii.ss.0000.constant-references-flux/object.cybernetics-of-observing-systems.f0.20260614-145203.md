**statement (plain)** — apply cybernetics to itself. First-order cybernetics studies how other systems steer, regulate, and feed back. Second-order cybernetics — the "cybernetics of cybernetics" — studies the systems that do the studying: the observing system observing itself. It is cybernetics recursively folded onto its own activity.

**statement** — second-order cybernetics is the recursive application of cybernetics to cybernetics: the discipline takes the *observing system* (including the cybernetician) as its object, rather than only externally *observed systems*. Where first-order cybernetics treats feedback, control, and regulation in a system held at arm's length, second-order cybernetics treats self-reference, circularity, and the inclusion of the observer as constitutive features.

**basis** — the framework operationalizes the [[object.observer-included-in-the-system]] principle into a stance for the whole field. Its hallmarks: circular causality and self-reference taken as legitimate (not pathological); the system studied recognized as an agent interacting with the observer; reflexivity made central. It became the umbrella under which [[object.autopoiesis]] (self-producing systems), conversation theory (Pask), and the constructivist epistemology of von Foerster and Glanville were gathered. Glanville characterized it as "the cybernetics of cybernetics" being itself cybernetic — a self-applying discipline.

**lineage** — Margaret Mead's 1967 ASC address (pub. 1968) named "cybernetics of cybernetics" and supplied the impulse [[source.mead-1968]]. Heinz von Foerster gave it the first/second-order articulation and produced the *Cybernetics of Cybernetics* volume (BCL Report 73.38, Univ. of Illinois, 1974) [[source.von-foerster-1974]]; the mature epistemology is collected in *Understanding Understanding* (Springer, 2003) [[source.von-foerster-2003]]. Gordon Pask and Ranulph Glanville are principal later developers. Some accounts also credit the broader Biological Computer Laboratory (BCL) circle.
