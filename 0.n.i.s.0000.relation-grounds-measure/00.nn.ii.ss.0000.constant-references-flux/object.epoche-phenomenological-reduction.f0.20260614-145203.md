**statement (plain)** — suspend, or "bracket," your ordinary belief that the world exists independently — without denying it. Stop taking the world's reality for granted, and redirect attention from *what* exists to *how* it shows up in experience. This step (the epoché) opens the way to the phenomenological reduction proper, which leads back to consciousness and its structures.

**statement** — the *epoché* (Greek: abstention) is the methodological suspension of the "general thesis" of the natural attitude: belief in the mind-independent existence of the world is neither affirmed nor denied but put out of action, bracketed. The *phenomenological reduction* uses this suspension to lead inquiry back (Latin *reducere*) to the correlation between consciousness and its objects — the *how* of givenness. Carried through, it becomes the *transcendental reduction*, the systematic analysis of how subjectivity constitutes the sense of the world. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — a methodological procedure, not a proposition: justified by what it discloses (the structures of constituting consciousness), defined operationally as the bracketing move plus the turn back to the appearing.

**lineage** — Husserl developed the epoché around 1906; prefigured in *Philosophy as Rigorous Science* (1911); first full statement in *Ideen I* (*Ideas I*, 1913) [[source.husserl-1913]]. Distinguished from [[object.natural-attitude]], which it suspends, and presupposing [[object.intentionality]], whose act–object correlation it brings into view. Later deepened in *Cartesian Meditations* (1931) and the *Crisis* (1936). The term *epoché* is borrowed from ancient (Pyrrhonian/Academic) skepticism but redeployed for a non-skeptical end. **[confirmed]**

**sources** — [[source.husserl-1913]]; SEP "Edmund Husserl"; IEP "The Phenomenological Reduction."
