**statement (plain)** — neither rival picture of evidence is adequate. The Bayesian picture, where a conclusion is warranted by giving it a high probability of being true, measures the wrong thing — belief, not how hard the claim was tested. And the textbook frequentist picture, which treats error rates only as long-run rules for *behaving*, fails to say what a single result actually warrants here and now. Good inference needs probativeness: have the specific ways this claim could be wrong been ruled out?

**statement** — the error-statistical account positions itself against two targets:
- **probabilism (the Bayesian critique)** — the view that probability quantifies plausibility / degree of belief / support in a hypothesis, and that an inference is warranted when H gets a high posterior. Mayo's objection: a highly *probable* hypothesis can be poorly *probed* ("highly probable vs. highly probed"); assigning belief does not show the error possibilities have been ruled out, and probabilism mishandles cherry-picking, optional stopping, and multiple testing because it treats such error-inflating procedures as evidentially irrelevant.
- **behaviorism (the pure Neyman–Pearson / Fisherian critique)** — the view that error probabilities are merely long-run frequencies for accept/reject *actions* with no bearing on the evidential import of the case at hand. Mayo counters Neyman's behavioral construal by giving error probabilities an epistemic, post-data role (severity); she also faults isolated Fisherian p-values for lacking an explicit alternative and thus a measure of test capacity (power). **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the criterion follows from the [[object.severity-principle|severity principle]] and [[object.error-probabilities-as-inference|error probabilities as inference]]: what warrants a claim is that its specific ways of being false have been probed and averted in the case at hand — a standard probabilism does not supply and bare behaviorism declines to provide. Mayo's positive program ("how to get beyond the statistics wars") reconstructs frequentist tools as severity assessments that are neither subjective-Bayesian nor merely behavioral.
