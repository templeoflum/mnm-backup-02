**statement (plain)** — Skinner's unit of analysis is the *operant*: a behavior that "operates on" the environment to produce consequences. Unlike a reflex (which is *elicited* by a stimulus), an operant is *emitted* — it just occurs, and its future frequency is then governed by its consequences. The paradigm studies operants by their *rate* of occurrence over time, recorded automatically in a controlled enclosure (the operant chamber, "Skinner box": a lever for rats or key for pigeons, a food/water dispenser, and a cumulative recorder). This free-operant method is the experimental core of operant conditioning.

**statement** — an operant is a class of responses defined functionally by their common effect on the environment, emitted (not stimulus-elicited) and modifiable by contingent consequences (see [[object.reinforcement-and-punishment]]). The dependent variable is response rate (responses per unit time), captured continuously by the cumulative recorder in a free-operant chamber, which allows the organism to respond at any time rather than in discrete experimenter-initiated trials. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — methodological/empirical: the chamber + cumulative recorder make response rate a continuous, objective, automatically recorded measure, enabling the highly reproducible findings of the experimental analysis of behavior (e.g., schedule effects). **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the term "operant," the emitted/elicited distinction, and the free-operant chamber method were introduced in [[source.skinner-1938]] *The Behavior of Organisms: An Experimental Analysis* (1938) — which gives the paradigm its experimental form descended from the [[object.law-of-effect]] — and elaborated conceptually in [[source.skinner-1953]] *Science and Human Behavior* (1953), where an operant is defined as behavior that "operates upon the environment to generate consequences." The label "Skinner box" was applied by others, not coined by Skinner himself. **[confirmed as attribution]**
