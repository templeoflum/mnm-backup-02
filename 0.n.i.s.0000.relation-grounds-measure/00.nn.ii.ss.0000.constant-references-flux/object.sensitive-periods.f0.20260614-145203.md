**statement (plain)** — a sensitive period is a limited-time window in early childhood when a child is intensely drawn to, and learns with unusual ease, a particular kind of skill (such as language, order, or refined movement). While the window is open the child repeats and perfects the skill almost effortlessly; once it closes, that ease fades and the same learning takes conscious effort.

**statement** — "a special sensitivity which a creature acquires in its infantile state, while it is still in a process of evolution… a transient disposition and limited to the acquisition of a particular trait." In Montessori's application, the child during a sensitive period shows a directed, repetitive interest in work serving one developmental acquisition, is "particularly prone to developing" the related capacity, and loses interest once it is gained. Sensitive periods occur largely within the first six years and overlap with the [[object.absorbent-mind]].

**basis** — an empirical-developmental claim grounded in Montessori's classroom observation of children's spontaneous, repeated absorption in specific activities. The matching of these inner windows to a well-stocked [[object.prepared-environment]] is the operative mechanism: when inner impulse meets external opportunity, learning is maximal. **[confirmed]**

lineage — Montessori adopted the term "sensitive periods" from the Dutch biologist **Hugo de Vries** (1848–1935), who described such periods in the development of animals (commonly recounted via the larva/butterfly example); Montessori extended the concept to human children. The de Vries borrowing is uniformly reported in Montessori scholarship and AMI materials; the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy notes de Vries as a biological influence on Montessori but does not itself trace the specific term to him — carried here as attribution. **[confirmed as attribution]** Set out in *The Secret of Childhood* (1936) [[source.montessori-1936]] and *The Absorbent Mind* (1949) [[source.montessori-1949]].
