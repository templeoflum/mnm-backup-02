**statement (plain)** — a record has a life with stages, like a living thing. It is created and used a lot, then used less and put in storage, then either destroyed or moved to an archive to be kept permanently. Records management handles the early stages; archives handle the end.

**statement** — the records life-cycle model holds that records move through a fixed sequence of stages from creation to final disposition: active creation and use → semi-active (reduced-use) storage → disposition, which is either destruction or permanent preservation in an archives. Commonly stated as creation, classification/maintenance, and disposition. It frames records management and archival management as **distinct, successive phases** of a record's existence. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the biological metaphor organizes the management of records at scale: it locates [[object.appraisal]] at the transition to disposition (deciding destruction vs. permanent retention) and assigns responsibilities to records managers (early stages) and archivists (end stage). Its sequential, custodial framing is also its main criticism — it implies a linear, deterministic progression that fits paper better than reactivated, fluid, distributed digital records, motivating the [[object.records-continuum]] as an alternative. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — conceived by Emmett J. Leahy (joined the US National Archives July 1935), with Philip C. Brooks, across the late 1930s and 1940s as records management emerged as a discipline in North America ([[source.leahy-1940s]]); the Emmett Leahy Award (est. 1967) commemorates his role. Reinforced by Schellenberg's appraisal/disposition framework ([[source.schellenberg-1956]]); Richard Berner later argued for integrating records-management and archival goals. **[confirmed as attribution]**

**sources** — [[source.leahy-1940s]]; [[source.schellenberg-1956]]; [[source.saa-dictionary]].
