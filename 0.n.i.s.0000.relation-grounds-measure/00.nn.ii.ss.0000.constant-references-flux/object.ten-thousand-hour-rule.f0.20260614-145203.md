**statement (plain)** — the widely repeated idea that about 10,000 hours of practice is "the magic number" that makes someone world-class at almost anything. It is catchy and largely wrong as stated: the number was an *average* among elite violinists in one study (not a threshold), the original research was about *deliberate* practice (not hours alone), and the researcher whose work it draws on rejects the simplified version.

**statement** — coined by Malcolm Gladwell in *Outliers* (2008) as "the magic number of greatness," the rule popularized Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993). In the source study, ~10,000 hours by age ~20 was the *average* accumulated practice of the best violinist group — with wide spread (some far less, others over 25,000 hours) — not a sufficient or necessary threshold. Ericsson publicly called it "a popularized but simplistic view of our work" that wrongly suggests anyone accumulating enough hours automatically becomes an expert; he stressed it is not how much you practice but how deliberate and purposeful it is. **[confirmed]**

**basis & limits** — carried as a *popularization with caveat*, not an established finding. The threshold framing fails empirically: the [[source.macnamara-2014]] meta-analysis shows deliberate practice explains only ~12% of performance variance overall and varies by domain, so no single hour-count predicts mastery. The legitimate core beneath the slogan is the [[object.deliberate-practice]] concept; the "10,000-hour rule" itself is a media artifact, not a law. **[confirmed as attribution]** (Gladwell's authorship of the phrasing) / **[candidate, threshold-claim disconfirmed]** (the rule as a sufficiency claim).

**lineage** — phrasing & popularization: Gladwell, *Outliers* (2008) [[source.gladwell-2008]]. Underlying research: Ericsson, Krampe & Tesch-Römer (1993) [[source.ericsson-1993]]. Author's repudiation: Ericsson & Pool, *Peak* (2016). Empirical bound: [[source.macnamara-2014]].
