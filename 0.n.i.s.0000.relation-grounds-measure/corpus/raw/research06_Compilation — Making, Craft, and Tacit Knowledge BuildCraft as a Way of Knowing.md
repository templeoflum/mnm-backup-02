## TARGET 3A — Technê / poiesis / praxis (the classical anatomy of making)

### 3A.1 — Aristotle's technê vs. phronesis: what kind of knowledge craft IS, and what it is NOT

**Canonical source:** Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* Book VI (esp. VI.3–VI.5), c. 4th century BCE.

- In *NE* VI.3, Aristotle lists five states "by virtue of which the soul possesses truth by way of affirmation or denial": **technê** (art/craft), **epistêmê** (scientific knowledge), **phronêsis** (practical wisdom/prudence), **sophia** (philosophic wisdom), and **nous** (intuitive reason/intellect). (Source: *NE* VI.3, 1139b15ff; George Mason University course notes; Internet Classics Archive.)

- **What craft (technê) IS:** In *NE* VI.4 (1140a1–24), Aristotle defines art as "a state of capacity to make, involving a true course of reasoning" (Ross translation). Verbatim (Internet Sacred Texts / nothingistic.org translation): "since architecture is an art and is essentially a reasoned state of capacity to make … art is identical with a state of capacity to make, involving a true course of reasoning. All art is concerned with coming into being … with contriving and considering how something may come into being which is capable of either being or not being." Technê concerns **things that can be otherwise** (the variable), not the necessary.

- **What craft is NOT:** Technê is NOT epistêmê (scientific knowledge): the object of scientific knowledge is "of necessity," "eternal," and cannot be otherwise (*NE* VI.3). Technê is also NOT phronesis. Per the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry "Episteme and Techne," Aristotle nonetheless "refers to technê or craft as itself also epistêmê or knowledge because it is a practice grounded in an 'account' — something involving theoretical understanding." SEP notes Aristotle distinguishes experience (empeiria) from technê in *Metaphysics* 981a5–30: technê "has a rational account, which explains what it does," and "we think the craftsman wiser than the empiric because of his knowledge of the account."

- The Bryn Mawr Classical Review (2011.10.36), reviewing **Tom Angier, *Technê in Aristotle's Ethics: Crafting the Moral Life***, states verbatim: "'Technē,' usually translated as craft, art, or skill, appears 377 times in Aristotle's corpus." It adds: "Craft knowledge pertains to production (poiēsis), thereby distinguishing it from practical wisdom (phronēsis), which is concerned with action (praxis)."

### 3A.2 — Why Aristotle refuses to collapse virtue into craft: poiesis (making) vs. praxis (doing/action)

**Canonical source:** *NE* VI.4 and VI.5 (1140a–b).

- **The core distinction (verbatim, Ross translation, sacred-texts.com):** "while making has an end other than itself, action cannot; for good action itself is its end." The Ken Archer paper quotes Terence Irwin's translation of *NE* VI.5, 1140b7: "For production has its end in something other than itself, but action does not, since its end is acting well itself." This echoes *NE* I.1: "the ends appear to differ; some are activities, and others are products apart from the activities."

- **VI.4 production-vs-action contrast (verbatim, Internet Classics Archive):** "In the variable are included both things made and things done; making and acting are different … so that the reasoned state of capacity to act is different from the reasoned state of capacity to make. Hence too they are not included one in the other; for neither is acting making nor is making acting."

- **Why virtue ≠ craft (verbatim, *NE* VI.5, Ross, sacred-texts.com):** "while there is such a thing as excellence in art, there is no such thing as excellence in practical wisdom; and in art he who errs willingly is preferable, but in practical wisdom, as in the virtues, he is the reverse. Plainly, then, practical wisdom is a virtue and not an art." The stpeter.im commentary on VI.5 adds: the craft itself "is not a virtue or excellence," and "you can forget a craft but there is no forgetting (lēthē) of wisdom."

- Phronesis is defined (*NE* VI.5, 1140b4–5) as "a true and reasoned state of capacity to act with regard to the things that are good or bad for man."

- **Secondary framing (flagged as commentary, not Aristotle):** Hannah Arendt (*The Human Condition*, 1958, p.32) and Alasdair MacIntyre (*After Virtue*, 1984, p.159) are cited by the Archer paper as drawing on this distinction. The Academia.edu "Poiesis, Praxis, Aisthesis" piece gives the standard examples: "housebuilding (poiêsis) versus harp-playing (praxis)." NOTE: A counter-reading exists — a Bryn Mawr/Tri-College thesis argues via Zhuang Zi that "Aristotle's distinction … is not that clear after all." Several blog/Medium sources (derekneighbors.com, formaetcultus, Matt Hopkins) popularize the distinction with modern examples (software, leadership); these are non-scholarly and flagged as such.

### 3A.3 — The "craft analogy" in Aristotle's physics/biology/metaphysics (nature as craftsman)

**Canonical sources:** Aristotle, *Physics* II (esp. II.2 194a, II.3, II.8 198b–199a); biological works; *Metaphysics*.

- **The craft analogy is pervasive:** The Bryn Mawr Classical Review (Angier review) states: "The characteristic language of craft analogy … is ubiquitous in the biological texts, where Aristotle often speaks figuratively of nature as an agent 'crafting' (dēmiourgein) her products. … technê-models crucially shape many aspects of Aristotle's philosophy—particularly his biology, physics, and metaphysics."

- **Art imitates / completes nature (verbatim, *Physics* II.8, 199a15; via SEP "Philosophy of Technology"):** "generally technè in some cases completes what nature cannot bring to a finish, and in others imitates nature." The fuller passage (tehnosofia blog, 199a): "if a house … had been a thing made by nature, it would have been made in the same way as it is now by art; and if things made by nature were made not only by nature but also by art, they would come to be in the same way as by nature."

- **The house/builder model and four causes:** Aquinas's *Commentary on Aristotle's Physics* Book II summarizes: "the builder considers the form of the house and also the bricks and the wood which are the matter of the house … it belongs to the same natural science to know both the matter and the form." *Physics* II.3 uses the sculptor/statue and builder/house to illustrate the four causes (material, formal, efficient, final).

- **"Nature does nothing in vain"** (Greek: hē phúsis oudèn poieî mátēn; Latin: natura nihil facit frustra). Per the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy, this teleological principle underlies the living/inanimate distinction (cited at *Generation of Animals* 741b5). Scholarly treatment: James Lennox, "Nature Does Nothing in Vain" (2001); Mariska Leunissen, *Explanation and Teleology in Aristotle's Science of Nature* (Cambridge, 2010), which distinguishes "primary" and "secondary" teleology and treats such principles as "heuristic devices"; Gottlieb & Sober, "Aristotle on 'Nature Does Nothing in Vain'," *HOPOS* 7(2), 2017, p.246.

- **Scholarly debate flagged:** Joachim Schummer ("Aristotle on Technology and Nature") argues "technology imitates nature" functions as a *premise* in *Physics* II for a "structural analogy between natural and technological production," and that Aristotle's main concern there "is with nature and the science of nature and not with technology." A separate reading (Academia.edu "Physics 199a8–12") presents "an alternative reading" in which the teleology argument need not rest on the art–nature analogy. Tim Ingold (see 3B.3) explicitly opposes "Aristotelian hylomorphism" (form imposed on matter).

## TARGET 3B — Tacit & embodied knowledge (Polanyi and after)

### 3B.1 — Michael Polanyi's tacit knowledge: "we know more than we can tell"

**Canonical sources:** Michael Polanyi (1891–1976), *Personal Knowledge: Towards a Post-Critical Philosophy* (1958) and *The Tacit Dimension* (1966).

- **Core claim (verbatim, opening of *The Tacit Dimension*):** "I shall reconsider human knowledge by starting from the fact that we can know more than we can tell." (Widely quoted as "We can know more than we can tell.")

- **Standard examples:**
  - **Recognizing a face (verbatim, *Tacit Dimension*):** "We know a person's face, and can recognize it among a thousand, indeed among a million. Yet we usually cannot tell how we recognize a face we know. So most of this knowledge cannot be put into words."
  - **Riding a bicycle** — introduced by Polanyi; the Crevels/TACK essay calls it "perhaps the most common example" of tacit knowledge.
  - **The driver** (quoted via Autor 2014): "The skill of a driver cannot be replaced by a thorough schooling in the theory of the motorcar; the knowledge I have of my own body differs altogether from the knowledge of its physiology."
  - **The probe / blind man's stick (verbatim, *Tacit Dimension*):** "To see more clearly the separation of a meaning from that which has this meaning, we may take the example of the use of a probe to explore a cavern, or the way a blind man feels his way by tapping with a stick. For here the separation of the two is wide."

- **Proximal/distal & subsidiary/focal structure:** Polanyi identifies a "from-to" structure. The **proximal term** = subsidiary awareness (the clues/particulars/tools we rely on but do not focus on); the **distal term** = focal awareness (the comprehensive entity we attend to). We "attend *from* subsidiary particulars *to* the focal whole." Key consequence: focusing attention directly on the subsidiaries destroys the meaning (the typing/finger-movement and stereoscopic-image examples).

- **Indwelling (verbatim, Polanyi):** "When we use a tool or a probe … we extend our bodily equipment … Tacit knowing now appears as an act of indwelling by which we gain access to a new meaning. When exercising a skill we literally dwell in the innumerable muscular acts which contribute to its purpose … since all understanding is tacit knowing, all understanding is achieved by indwelling." Also: "all knowing is personal knowing—participation through [embodied] indwelling."

- **Why craft knowledge resists full explication (verbatim, Polanyi):** "While tacit knowledge can be possessed by itself, explicit knowledge must rely on being tacitly understood and applied. Hence all knowledge is either tacit or rooted in tacit knowledge. A wholly explicit knowledge is unthinkable."

- **Scholarly interpretation flagged:** **Stefania Ruzsits Jha**, "A New Interpretation of Michael Polanyi's Theory of Tacit Knowing: Integrative Philosophy with 'Intellectual Passions'," *Studies in History and Philosophy of Science* 28(4), 1997, pp. 611–631, identifies three progressively refined models — the **Gestalt-Perception Model**, the **Action-Guiding Model**, and the **Semiotic Model** (the last giving tacit knowing its "from-to structure") — "tied together by the central feature of Intellectual Passions as integrator."

- **Frontier work (2014–2026):** **David Autor** (MIT) named "**Polanyi's Paradox**" in "Polanyi's Paradox and the Shape of Employment Growth" (NBER Working Paper 20485, Sept. 2014): "our tacit knowledge of how the world works often exceeds our explicit understanding" — framing the limits of automation. **Subbarao Kambhampati** ("Polanyi's Revenge," *Communications of the ACM*, 2021) argues neural networks have swung to "learning tacit knowledge from data." A 2022 *Frontiers in Psychology* paper (Wuytens et al., DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2022.892223) introduces "Tacit Entrepreneurial Knowledge."

### 3B.2 — Dreyfus & Dreyfus five-stage skill acquisition model

**Canonical sources:** Stuart E. Dreyfus & Hubert L. Dreyfus, original 1980 18-page report (UC Berkeley Operations Research Center, for the USAF Office of Scientific Research); elaborated in *Mind Over Machine: The Power of Human Intuition and Expertise in the Era of the Computer* (1986; Free Press). Recent restatement: Stuart E. Dreyfus & B. Scot Rousse, "Revisiting the Six Stages of Skill Acquisition," in *Teaching and Learning for Adult Skill Acquisition* (2021).

NOTE ON NAMING: The 1980 report named stages "novice, competent, proficient, expert and master"; *Mind Over Machine* (1986) settled on the standard five — **novice, advanced beginner, competence, proficiency, expertise** (a sixth "mastery" stage appears in later articulations). Flagged as a minor terminological inconsistency across sources.

**The five stages (precise):**
1. **Novice:** The instructor decomposes the task into "context-free features that the beginner can recognize without the desired skill"; the beginner follows "rules for determining actions on the basis of these features, just like a computer following a program." Performance is rule-governed, detached, "slow, clumsy."
2. **Advanced Beginner:** Through experience, the learner recognizes "situational" aspects (not just context-free features) and applies "experience-based maxims." Dreyfus: "Unlike a rule, a maxim requires that one already have some understanding of the domain to which the maxim applies (Polanyi, 1958)."
3. **Competence:** Faced with an overwhelming number of recognizable features, the learner adopts a plan/perspective to organize the situation; analytic and "planful"; becomes emotionally involved and "takes responsibility" for choices.
4. **Proficiency:** An "intuitive perspective" emerges — "a direct sense of what is relevant and called for." The proficient performer "spontaneously sees" what is going on but "has not yet had enough experience with the outcomes" to react automatically; still falls back on rules for the action itself.
5. **Expertise:** Fluid, intuitive, non-deliberative performance. Verbatim (Stuart Dreyfus / *Mind Over Machine*): "when things are proceeding normally, experts don't solve problems and don't make decisions; they do what normally works." And: "normally an expert does not calculate. He or she does not solve problems. He or she does not even think. He or she just does what normally works and, of course, it normally works."

**Rules → situated intuitive expertise:** Maxims (stage 2) "require that one already have some understanding of the domain." To progress to proficiency/expertise, the learner "must take the risk of letting go of the application of rules and procedures, thus involving themselves more directly and emotionally."

**The chess illustration (with figure correction):** *Mind Over Machine* (1986) states: "Excellent chess players can play at the rate of 5 to 10 seconds a move … At this speed they must depend almost entirely on intuition." The 1986 book estimates a master "can distinguish roughly **50,000** types of positions." **CONFLICT FLAGGED:** the **100,000** figure appears in Rousse & Dreyfus's 2021 restatement; some sources conflate the two. The chunking estimate traces to **Herbert A. Simon** (chunking theory); per *Scientific American*'s "The Expert Mind," "Simon estimated that a typical grandmaster has access to roughly 50,000 to 100,000 chunks of chess information." The Dreyfuses' supporting experiment "pitted international chess master **Julio Kaplan** against a slightly weaker player in a game of lightning chess" while Kaplan did rapid mental addition; per Dreyfus & Dreyfus (1986, p.33), "even with his analytic mind completely jammed by adding numbers, Kaplan more than held his own against the master." **Patricia Benner** later applied the model to nursing (*From Novice to Expert*). The model originated as a critique of rule-based AI.

### 3B.3 — Craft as "perceptive-cognitive enactment"/attunement; Kuijpers and the Merleau-Ponty thread

**Maikel Kuijpers (Dutch archaeologist, Leiden University):**
- Key works: **Maikel H.G. Kuijpers, *An Archaeology of Skill: Metalworking Skill and Material Specialization in Early Bronze Age Central Europe* (Routledge, 2018)**; and Kuijpers, "A Sensory Update to the Chaîne Opératoire in Order to Study Skill: Perceptive Categories for Copper-Compositions in Archaeometallurgy," *Journal of Archaeological Method and Theory* (2017, DOI 10.1007/s10816-017-9356-9), which introduces "**perceptive categories**" centered on the human senses.
- Kuijpers defines craft as "**a way of exploring and understanding the material world**" (quoted by Eric Crevels in "Coarse epistemes").
- Kuijpers's verified terminology (via Crevels): skill is "a form of 'recognition of and response to material'"; an "intimate relationship between the maker and material … achieved through the hands, eyes, and tools"; a "sensate understanding"; "craftspeople aim to express the qualities rather than properties of a material."

- **IMPORTANT VERIFICATION FLAG:** "**Coarse epistemes**" is the title of an essay by **Eric Crevels** (on the TACK / "Tacit Knowledge and Architecture" platform — "Coarse epistemes: Skill, craftsmanship and tacit knowledge in the grit of the world"), NOT a Kuijpers term. It is **Crevels** who develops "the hypothesis that craftsmanship is … a perceptive-cognitive enactment within the making process, a form of attunement with production." The phrases "perceptive-cognitive enactment," "attunement with production," and "coarse epistemes" should therefore be attributed to **Crevels** (drawing on Kuijpers), NOT to Kuijpers directly. The task's attribution of "coarse epistemes" terminology to Kuijpers is **not verified and appears to be a misattribution.**

- **Blacksmith example (Crevels):** smiths "measure the steel's temperature by colour … without the need for a scientific explanation of the quantum mechanics and chemistry involved" — knowing phenomena "in action, without knowing them in descriptive terms."

**Related craft-theory figures (named):** Tim Ingold (anthropologist) — "The Textility of Making" (*Cambridge Journal of Economics*, 2009) and *Making: Anthropology, Archaeology, Art and Architecture* (2013); argues making is a "form-generating process"/"morphogenesis," opposing "Aristotelian hylomorphism." Richard Sennett, *The Craftsman* (Yale, 2008). David Pye, *The Nature and Art of Workmanship* — "workmanship of risk." Trevor Marchand, *Craftwork as Problem Solving* (2017). Donald Schön, *The Reflective Practitioner* — "knowing-in-action," "Technical Rationality." Gilbert Ryle, *The Concept of Mind* (1949) — "knowing how" vs. "knowing that," which influenced Polanyi.

**Merleau-Ponty embodiment thread:**
- Canonical source: **Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception* (1945; *Phénoménologie de la perception*).**
- **Body schema:** described as "neither an idea nor a physiological-physical fact … rather a practical diagram of our relationships with the world, an action-based norm." Per Cambridge readings (Romdenh-Romluc; T.D. Mooney), the body schema is "sub-reflective," organizing "postural changes and our phenomenal fields towards habitual or novel outcomes in a horizonal and context-sensitive manner."
- **Motor intentionality / "operative intentionality":** Merleau-Ponty's term for skilled bodily understanding distinct from reflective "act intentionality" (the term "operative intentionality" derives from Husserl/Fink). Illustrated via the **Schneider case** (a brain-injured patient). The "intentional arc" "unifies sensory experience with bodily action, illustrating how the body is a knowing subject engaging with the world."
- **Tools as body extension:** Crevels notes Merleau-Ponty (with Mauss and Polanyi) treats tools "as extensions of the body" — "a chisel allows one to 'feel' the wood in its resistances."

## TARGET 3C — Grammars of craft workflow & documentation

### 3C.1 — "(De)composing Craft: An Elementary Grammar for Sharing Expertise in Craft Workflows"

**VERIFICATION — CONFIRMED.** arXiv:2506.10891 [cs.HC].
- **Authors:** Ritik Batra, Lydia Kim, Ilan Mandel, Amritansh Kwatra (all Cornell Tech); Jane L. E (Stanford University); Steven J. Jackson (Cornell University); Thijs Roumen (Cornell Tech).
- **Dates/venue:** Submitted 12 June 2025 (v1); v2 revised 12 March 2026. To appear in *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction* (CSCW track), 2026. 29 pages, 7 figures.
- NOTE: One ResearchGate listing erroneously lists Ritik Batra as "University of California, Berkeley"; the paper itself lists Cornell Tech. The interview N is stated as N=16 in the intro and as 13 survey + 3 in-depth in Section 3 (minor internal phrasing inconsistency); evaluation N=7 expert crocheters is unambiguous.

**The grammar — primitives/elements:** Builds on Knight & Stiny's "making grammar" foundational elements (**stuff, doing, sensing, things** — "doing and sensing with stuff to make things") and is "oriented by five design guidelines: Generalizability, Extensibility, Customizability, Accessibility, and Practicality." The grammar is "composed of **seven patterns** occurring in craft workflows":

1. **Granularity Shifts** — assigns each workflow state a level of detail ("low, medium, and high"). "A high level of granularity might include detailed descriptions of tool usage, material handling, and subtle nuances in movement … a low level … might simply state the action," serving experts and novices differently ("adjustable lenses in craft documentation").
2. **Reflective Loops** — "represent the iterative process of experimentation and adjustment … the grammar captures the non-linear nature of craft, where decisions are made in response to emerging challenges." Grounded in Schön's reflective practitioner; captures "cycles of action, observation, and adjustment" (the dialogue between *doing* and *sensing*).
3. **Note-to-Self** — "a flexible structure for craftspeople to add information and context that does not fit into the grammar's defined patterns and elements" (e.g., "the precise amount of force applied"); "situated annotations provide a freeform way to embed crucial context" — the "why" behind actions.
4. **External Links** — "connect with external sources of knowledge — websites, videos, online forums" (e.g., Ravelry); "the grammar acknowledges that craft practice often involves drawing on a network of information that extends beyond a single document."
5. **Segments** — "a structural pattern that enables the division of a craft workflow into distinct sections, mirroring the creation of individual components within the final artifact (e.g., the sleeves of a sweater)"; supports collapsible navigation.
6. **Branches** — "document these deviations … represent the non-linearity of real-world craft workflows, acknowledging the alternative paths that craftspeople often explore" (comparing the original pattern with the actual workflow to identify points of divergence); captures "the underlying intention and creative decision-making process."
7. **Revision Loops** — document "two critical aspects of iteration": (a) "prototyping phases," and (b) instances where craftspeople "sense something is off … undo recent steps, and redo them differently." This "undo-redo cycle preserves the 'paths-not-taken' (Goveia da Rocha et al., 2022)"; emphasizes "repair" (Jackson, 2014).

(Distinction per the paper: Branches = divergent paths from a pattern; Reflective Loops = sensing→adjusting dialogue; Revision Loops = prototyping + undo/redo/repair.)

**Interface:** **CraftLink** "transforms unstructured craft videos into graph-based visual representations." MLLM used: **Google's Gemini 1.5 Pro**, prompted with the grammar as a system prompt; outputs JSON; graph built with ReactJS + ReactFlow, color-coded by stuff/doing/sensing/things, with sequences expressed algebraically (e.g., T₁ + D₁ = T₂). External Links supplemented via the Custom Google Search API. Evaluation: N=7 expert crocheters (≥5 years' experience), symmetrical creator/consumer two-part study; transcription via Whisper large-v2; hybrid inductive-deductive thematic analysis (4 themes / 77 codes).

### 3C.2 — Why linear step-documentation FAILS to transmit craft

From the paper (verbatim, abstract and intro):
- "most documentation focuses solely on the linear steps leading to final artifacts, neglecting the tacit knowledge necessary to improvise, or adapt workflows to meet the unique demands of each craft project. This omission limits knowledge sharing and **reduces craft to a mechanical endeavor, rather than a sophisticated way of seeing, thinking, and doing.**"
- Static formats "convey formal specifications and mechanical procedures, [but] they fail to transmit the situated and tacit knowledge needed to navigate dynamic or uncertain contexts (Suchman, 1987)."
- **Rule-based vs. rule-bound (citing Andrew Abbott, 2014, *The System of Professions*):** "Andrew Abbott has demonstrated how these actions are *rule-based*, not *rule-bound*. This distinction marks the difference between a practice of craft that is rote, mechanical and static and one that is live, dynamic, and evolving." "*Rule-bound* approaches communicate the explicit rules of craft practice but cannot capture the nuanced improvisation and adjustment that experts perform … They can show practice in the *standard case*, but rarely the myriad deviations and adjustments." (Per the authors' summary: *rule-bound* = rigid mechanical adherence to explicit rules, captured by static documents like recipes; *rule-based* = experts use rules as a flexible foundation that can be amended or broken with "interpretive freedom.")
- **What the grammar captures that recipes don't:** improvisation, situated adaptation, deviations ("Branches"), reflective sensing loops, prototyping and undo/redo/repair, the tacit "why," and connections to external knowledge networks — i.e., it aims to "externalize, without freezing, or standardizing, the tacit knowledge." Anchored in Polanyi ("We can know more than we can tell"), Schön ("reflect-in-action"), Goodwin ("professional vision"), Ingold (craft as "ongoing dialogue between maker, materials, and environment"), Pye ("workmanship of risk"), and Sennett.
- The baker/recipe example: an expert "views the recipe as a starting point, not a strictly determinate sequence" and annotates "slap and fold" vs. "stretch and fold" by dough hydration; a less-experienced baker "may approach the recipe as immutable truth."
- **Residual limit acknowledged by the authors ("What is Lost?"):** evaluation participant C4 said, "I just like feel [it] in my body … I have trouble putting it into words" — embodied/sensory knowledge the grammar still cannot fully capture; C1 called real-time responsiveness "an unavoidable trade-off."

### 3C.3 — Situated action (Suchman) and distributed cognition (Hutchins)

**Lucy Suchman, *Plans and Situated Actions: The Problem of Human-Machine Communication* (Cambridge University Press, 1987; expanded 2007 as *Human-Machine Reconfigurations*).** Suchman is an anthropologist/ethnomethodologist (PhD UC Berkeley, 1984); the work emerged from her ethnographic study at Xerox PARC of office workers struggling with a photocopier.
- **Core claim / critique of plans:** "Lucy Suchman argues that the planning model of interaction favoured by the majority of AI researchers does not take sufficient account of the situatedness of most human social behaviour." Plans do not control or determine action. Verbatim (confirmed in the 1987 text): "however planned, purposeful actions are inevitably situated actions." Plans function as resources for action, not its governing structure.
- **The navigation contrast:** Suchman contrasts the **Trukese** (Micronesian) "ad hoc" navigation — objective first, course adjusted continuously in response to conditions — with the **European** tradition of forming a plan/course and adhering to it, altering the plan first if conditions change. She uses this to argue that action is "concrete and embodied."
- Her copier study documented interaction "breakdowns" caused by the machine's "inherent inability to understand various contexts."

**Edwin Hutchins, *Cognition in the Wild* (MIT Press, 1995).** Hutchins is a cognitive anthropologist (and open-ocean racing sailor/navigator).
- **Core claim — distributed cognition:** Cognitive processes are "not solely confined to the internal workings of an individual's brain" but "distributed across individuals, artifacts, and the environment." "Cultural activity systems have cognitive properties of their own that are different from the cognitive properties of the individuals who participate in them." The needed move is "to shift the unit of analysis beyond the skin of the individual."
- **The ship-navigation example:** an ethnographic study of navigation aboard the US Navy amphibious helicopter transport **USS Palau** entering San Diego Harbor. "Tasks like position fixing and plotting are not performed by a single individual in isolation"; they involve "the coordinated efforts of multiple individuals, each contributing their expertise and utilizing a range of tools and procedures." "The computational challenges in the USS Palau were solved through a cognitive process that was widely distributed across a team of navigators and their cognitive artefacts." Hutchins applies "cognition as computation" (adopting David Marr's paradigm) and compares modern Western with Micronesian navigation.
- **Cognitive artifacts:** tools and technologies "that embody knowledge and facilitate cognitive processes" (charts, alidades, the fathometer, the "fix cycle").

2. **Kuijpers's own verified terms:** "perceptive categories" (2017 paper); craft as "a way of exploring and understanding the material world"; skill as "recognition of and response to material"; "sensate understanding"; "qualities rather than properties" (*An Archaeology of Skill*, 2018).
3. **arXiv:2506.10891 fully verified** — authors, venue (CSCW 2026 / PACM HCI), dates (v1 12 Jun 2025; v2 12 Mar 2026), seven-pattern grammar, CraftLink/Gemini 1.5 Pro, N=7 evaluation. One affiliation listing error noted; interview-N phrasing inconsistent (16 vs. 13+3).
4. **Dreyfus chess figure conflict:** *Mind Over Machine* (1986) says ~**50,000** position types; Rousse & Dreyfus (2021) restate as **100,000**; Simon's chunking estimate is "50,000 to 100,000." Sources frequently conflate these.

6. **Tom Angier** is the author of *Technê in Aristotle's Ethics: Crafting the Moral Life*, the book reviewed in BMCR 2011.10.36 (source of the "377 times" figure).
7. Several popular/non-scholarly sources (Medium, Substack, personal blogs) were encountered for the Aristotle material; primary translations (Ross via MIT/sacred-texts; Irwin via Hackett) and SEP were prioritized for verbatim quotes.
