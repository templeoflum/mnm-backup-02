# The Work That Directs the Work: Stigmergy as the Unifying Mechanism Behind Your Cable-Pull Line

## TL;DR
- **Your intuition is correct and has a name: stigmergy** — coordination achieved indirectly through traces left in a shared medium, first defined by Pierre-Paul Grassé in 1959 studying termites, and now treated by researchers like Francis Heylighen as a near-universal coordination mechanism spanning ants, slime molds, neurons, markets, Wikipedia, kanban, and physical construction work.
- **Your cable-pull line is the purest "sematectonic" case** — the work medium (the rope's tension/slack) *is* the signal, exactly as the half-built termite pillar is the signal; this is distinct from "marker-based" stigmergy (ant pheromone, a kanban card, a walkie-talkie) where a dedicated signal is added on a separate channel. This distinction sharpens your own observation precisely.
- **The cross-domain unification is genuinely a shared mechanism at the abstract level (trace-in-medium + stimulation rule + feedback), but ranges from literal to metaphorical** as you move from insects to humans — and the most honest researchers (Christensen, Parunak, Marsh & Onof) say human stigmergy is real but always *supplements* rather than replaces explicit communication.

## Key Findings

**1. Stigmergy is a real, formally-defined concept — not a loose analogy.** French zoologist Pierre-Paul Grassé coined *stigmergie* in 1959 while studying nest reconstruction in the termites *Bellicositermes natalensis* and *Cubitermes* sp. (the species named in his *Insectes Sociaux* paper). His original definition: "the stimulation of workers by the very performances they have achieved." The term fuses Greek *stigma* (mark, sign, sting/goad) and *ergon* (work). The principle is that the state of the work environment — not direct messaging between agents — coordinates the agents. Grassé used it to solve what Theraulaz and Bonabeau later called the "coordination paradox": how insects of very limited intelligence, with no apparent communication, collaboratively build complex structures.

**2. The sematectonic vs. marker-based distinction is the key to your insight.** E.O. Wilson (in *Sociobiology*, 1975) distinguished **sematectonic** stigmergy — coordination via the work product itself or its physical state (the height of a termite pillar, the partly-built wall, a worn footpath, the tension in a rope) — from **sign-based / marker-based** stigmergy, where agents deposit a dedicated signal that is not itself part of the work (pheromone, a kanban card, a flag). Your cable-pull line is sematectonic: the medium being worked (the cable under tension) simultaneously carries the coordinating signal. Your walkie-talkie is the opposite — a separate marker channel layered on top. Heylighen and others add a second orthogonal axis: **quantitative** (traces differ in strength/degree, like pheromone concentration or rope tension) vs. **qualitative** (discrete different stimuli trigger different actions, like the wasp *Paralastor*'s nest-building stages).

**3. Ant trails and Ant Colony Optimization are the canonical marker-based case, and they share their deep dynamic with slime molds and neural pruning.** Ants lay pheromone; more traffic → more pheromone → more attraction (positive feedback), while evaporation provides negative feedback / forgetting that prevents lock-in to bad paths. Marco Dorigo formalized this as Ant Colony Optimization (ACO) beginning in 1991–1992, translating it into a metaheuristic for hard problems. The reinforce-by-use-plus-decay dynamic is the same abstract loop you saw in slime-mold trails and synaptic pruning.

**4. Stigmergy is environmental memory — it offloads cognition into the medium.** The trace is simultaneously a *record of what's been done* and a *signal for what to do next*. The slime mold *Physarum polycephalum* literally uses its own extracellular slime trail as an externalized spatial memory. This connects to the "extended mind" / distributed cognition tradition (Clark & Chalmers 1998; Hutchins).

**5. Human stigmergy is well-documented but contested at the edges.** Kanban/Toyota pull systems, open-source software, Wikipedia, swarm robotics, and physical construction work all show coordination through shared artifacts with minimal explicit talk. But scholars disagree about how far to stretch the term.

## Details

### 1. Origin and definition — Grassé and the coordination paradox

Pierre-Paul Grassé (1895–1985), a French zoologist who edited a major multi-volume treatise of zoology, introduced *stigmergie* in a 1959 paper in the journal *Insectes Sociaux* (vol. 6, pp. 41–81). Studying how termites repair and build nests, he noticed that workers initially deposit mud pellets more or less randomly, but each deposit raises the probability that other workers will add their pellets at the same spot. Small heaps become columns; columns lean together into arches; arches into the "cathedral" of the nest — with no blueprint, no foreman, and no direct worker-to-worker communication. As Grassé put it (in the French original): *"La coordination des tâches, la régulation des constructions ne dépendent pas directement des ouvriers, mais des constructions elles-mêmes. L'ouvrier ne dirige pas son travail, il est guidé par lui."* — "The coordination of tasks, the regulation of construction, do not depend directly on the workers, but on the constructions themselves. The worker does not direct his work; he is guided by it." This is precisely your formulation: *the work directs the work.*

Two readings of the etymology coexist and, as Heylighen notes, illustrate the bidirectional feedback loop at the heart of the concept. In Grassé's reading, the *product of work (ergon)* acts as a *stimulus (stigma)* for the next action. In H. Van Dyke Parunak's (2006) reading, *action (ergon)* leaves a *mark (stigma)* that other agents sense. Heylighen's own consolidated definition (in the Lewis & Marsh volume *Human Stigmergy*): "stigmergy is an indirect, mediated mechanism of coordination between actions, in which the trace of an action left on a medium stimulates the performance of a subsequent action." He decomposes it into components: an **agent/action**, a **medium**, a **trace**, and a **stimulation rule** (condition → action) linking traces to subsequent actions, where the condition need only make the next action *more probable*, not certain (P(action|condition) > P(action)).

**What's debated.** Definitions in the literature are, by Heylighen's own admission, "vague, restricted in scope, and mutually incoherent." A recurring complaint (Dipple, Raymond & Docherty 2014) is that casual treatments make stigmergy seem like a "simple phenomenon that can be easily dismissed as an environment mediated, and indirect communication mechanism" when the mechanics (feedback, persistence, semantics of the trace) are actually subtle. There is also genuine biological debate about termites specifically: Facchini, Rathery, Douady, Sillam-Dussès & Perna, "Substrate evaporation drives collective construction in termites" (*eLife*, 2024; 12:RP86843, DOI 10.7554/eLife.86843), studying *Coptotermes gestroi*, argue that substrate curvature and water evaporation — not necessarily a building pheromone — drive collective construction, and state verbatim that "several years since Grassé's early observations, there is still not a consensus on the exact nature of the stigmergic stimuli involved in regulating termite construction."

### 2. Sematectonic vs. marker-based — and why your rope is the deep case

The distinction matters because it separates two genuinely different ways the signal relates to the work:

- **Sematectonic stigmergy** (Wilson's term, from *sema* "sign" + *tekton* "builder/craftsman"): the coordinating stimulus *is* the work product or its physical state. Examples: the geometry of a growing termite pillar; the completion-state of one stage of a wasp's mud funnel triggering the next; a dirt footpath worn across a lawn that "intimates a shortcut"; the partly-built partition wall that tells the next tradesperson what to do. **The tension/slack in your cable line is sematectonic** — there is no separate message; the medium you are physically working is itself the carrier of the coordinating information.
- **Marker-based (sign-based) stigmergy**: agents deposit a dedicated signal that is *not* a contribution to the work itself. Archetype: ant pheromone trails. In human work: a kanban card, a flag, a sticky note, a status field in an issue tracker. **Your walkie-talkie is a marker channel** — explicitly added, separate from the cable.

A nuance worth knowing: Grassé's own termites turned out to be a *mixed* case. Because termites are blind, what actually stimulates them is largely the *pheromone* mixed into the mud by co-workers, not the visible mud shape — so the textbook "pure sematectonic" example is partly marker-based in reality. This is a useful caution: real systems often blend both. Your line is unusually pure precisely because the signal cannot be separated from the medium.

Heylighen adds the orthogonal **quantitative vs. qualitative** axis (from Theraulaz & Bonabeau's 1999 "A Brief History of Stigmergy," *Artificial Life* 5(2):97–116). Quantitative: stronger traces elicit stronger/more-probable responses (pheromone concentration; rope tension; the depth of a worn path). Qualitative: different *kinds* of trace trigger different *kinds* of action in sequence (the wasp *Paralastor* builds its funnel in discrete stages S1→S5, each completed stage being the cue for the next). Your cable line is primarily quantitative-sematectonic; a kanban board is qualitative-marker-based. The languagehat discussion records a real terminological grumble — some linguists consider Wilson's "sematectonic" "ugly and unnecessary," an instance of the etymological fallacy — but the *conceptual* distinction it marks (work-product-as-signal vs. dedicated-marker) is widely accepted and appears in Bonabeau, Dorigo & Theraulaz's 1999 book *Swarm Intelligence*.

### 3. Ant trails, ACO, and the reinforce-plus-decay family

The canonical marker-based example: foraging ants deposit pheromone; the trail-laying-and-following behavior creates **positive feedback** — trails with more pheromone attract more ants, which deposit still more. The seminal empirical basis is the **double-bridge experiment** by Jean-Louis Deneubourg and colleagues (Goss et al. 1989, *Naturwissenschaften* 76:579–581, on the Argentine ant): given two bridges of different lengths between nest and food, the colony converges on the shorter path because ants traverse it faster, so pheromone accumulates faster there. **Evaporation** supplies negative feedback — a "useful form of forgetting" that prevents premature convergence to a suboptimal path and lets the colony adapt when conditions change.

Marco Dorigo formalized this into **Ant Colony Optimization (ACO)**: his 1991 technical report "Positive Feedback as a Search Strategy" (with Maniezzo and Colorni), his 1992 PhD thesis, and the founding "Ant System" paper (Dorigo, Maniezzo & Colorni, 1996, *IEEE Transactions on Systems, Man, and Cybernetics – Part B*, 26(1):29–41). Variants include Ant Colony System (Dorigo & Gambardella 1997) and MAX–MIN Ant System (Stützle & Hoos 2000). In ACO, artificial "ants" build solutions on a graph; pheromone is laid in proportion to solution quality and evaporates each iteration. Without evaporation the algorithm fails to converge; with it, it reliably finds short paths.

**Real-world deployments** (well-established, not speculative): the traveling salesman problem; vehicle routing — Gambardella, Taillard & Agazzi's 1999 MACS-VRPTW was applied to real fleet problems, and a 2007 *Swarm Intelligence* review (Rizzoli et al.) reports applications including a VRP-with-time-windows for a Swiss supermarket chain, pickup-and-delivery for an Italian distribution company, and a time-dependent VRP for freight distribution in Padua, Italy; telecommunications routing (Schoonderwoerd et al. 1996; British Telecom work by Appleby & Steward in 1994); and the first commercial use by companies Eurobios and AntOptima around 2001.

**The shared dynamic across your domains.** The same abstract loop — *reinforce what gets used, let the unused decay* — recurs in:
- **Slime molds**: trails that lead to food get reinforced into thick tubes; unused connections wither (Nakagaki et al.'s maze-solving, *Nature* 2000).
- **Synaptic pruning**: the "use it or lose it" Hebbian principle — synapses that fire together are strengthened; rarely-active synapses are weakened and eliminated in an activity-dependent process. (One honest caveat from the neuroscience: it is not purely "use it or keep it" — in primary visual cortex, pyramidal cells actually *lose* more spines than they grow after the onset of visual experience, so the rule is "even if you use it you still may lose it" depending on region. The decay side is real and sometimes dominant.)
- **ACO pheromone**: reinforce-by-use plus evaporation.

This is a genuine shared *mathematical* structure (positive feedback + decay on a shared substrate), which is why the analogy across these domains is more than poetic — though the *physical substrate* differs entirely (chemical, cytoplasmic, synaptic, digital).

### 4. Externalized / environmental memory and the extended mind

Stigmergy stores information *in the environment* rather than in the agents' heads. The trace is dual: a **memory** of what's been done and a **signal** for what's next. Heylighen calls cognition itself a kind of "interiorization" of individual stigmergy — using external traces to register the state of work provides an *external memory* that lets a single agent (or a swarm) tackle a complex project without holding the whole plan internally.

The cleanest empirical demonstration is **slime mold spatial memory**: Reid, Latty, Dussutour & Beekman, "Slime mold uses an externalized spatial 'memory' to navigate in complex environments," *PNAS* (2012, 109(43):17490–94), DOI 10.1073/pnas.1215037109. *Physarum polycephalum*, which has no brain or neurons, lays down extracellular slime wherever it has been and *avoids* areas already coated — using the trail as an external map to solve the U-shaped trap problem (a standard robotics navigation test). The effect size is striking: on blank-jelly dishes almost all the slime moulds eventually reached the goal in an average of just under 60 hours, whereas in dishes pre-coated with slime (which masks the organism's own trail) only about a third reached the goal, those that did took around 75 hours, and they spent more than ten times as long re-traversing places they had already explored. The authors propose externalized spatial memory may be "the functional precursor to the internal memory of higher organisms." Philosophers (e.g. the PhilSci-Archive paper "Externalized memory in slime mould and the extended (non-neuronal) mind") treat this as a case of **memory without learning** — the trace was not laid down through learning, yet functions as memory — and as a clean instance of the **Hypothesis of Extended Cognition (HEC)**.

This links to **Clark & Chalmers' "The Extended Mind"** (*Analysis*, 1998, 58(1):7–19) and their Otto-and-Inga thought experiment: Otto's notebook functions as biological memory would, so the cognitive process extends beyond the skull into the environment. It also links to **Edwin Hutchins' distributed cognition** (cognition spread across people and artifacts). **Leslie Marsh & Christian Onof**, "Stigmergic epistemology, stigmergic cognition" (*Cognitive Systems Research*, 2008, 9(1–2):136–149), explicitly join stigmergy to the extended-mind tradition, proposing "social stigmergy as the extra-cranial analog of an artificial neural network providing epistemic structure." (The extended-mind thesis itself is contested — Adams & Aizawa's "coupling-constitution fallacy" objection holds that being causally coupled to a process doesn't make the external object part of the cognition. So when you say your creative thinking "runs on signal-in-the-medium," that's a defensible position in an active philosophical debate, not settled fact.)

### 5. Human stigmergy — including your own trade

**Kanban / Toyota pull systems.** Kanban (Japanese for "signboard") was developed by Taiichi Ohno at Toyota, inspired by American supermarket restocking. A card (or bin, or digital ticket) attached to a container of parts is a **pull signal**: when the container empties, the card travels back upstream and authorizes replenishment — production is triggered by *actual downstream consumption*, not a central forecast. This is textbook marker-based, qualitative stigmergy: the card is a dedicated signal deposited in a shared workflow that coordinates upstream and downstream stages with minimal verbal communication. Work-in-progress limits (the fixed number of cards) are the system's built-in throttle.

**Open-source software.** Bolici, Howison & Crowston studied Free/Libre Open Source Software (FLOSS) projects and found, in their words, "little evidence of overt coordination of the development, i.e., FLOSS developers seemed to rarely communicate." They theorize that "work can be coordinated through the outcome of the work itself, a mode of coordination analogous to the biological process of stigmergy": the codebase, the issue tracker, and the build state *are* the coordinating medium. Their key papers: "Coordination Without Discussion? Socio-Technical Congruence and Stigmergy in FLOSS Projects" (ICSE workshop, 2009) and "Stigmergic coordination in FLOSS development teams" (*Cognitive Systems Research* 38, 2016, pp. 14–22). Howison & Crowston's "Collaboration through open superposition" (*MIS Quarterly*, 2014, 38) is the fuller theory. This is largely sematectonic (the work product coordinates) blended with marker-based elements (issue labels, status flags).

**Wikipedia.** Heylighen's prototype of human stigmergy: an imperfect article is itself the stimulus — a reader is "stimulated to improve and expand the writings of previous contributors," and the article (not a conversation) coordinates thousands of editors who never meet. This is close to pure sematectonic stigmergy: the work product is the medium.

**Swarm robotics.** Engineers deliberately build stigmergic robots using "digital pheromones" — virtual traces deposited on a shared map or on the robots themselves, with tunable decay rates. Parunak pioneered digital-pheromone coordination of unmanned vehicles. A recent physical implementation, **Phormica** (Salman, Garzón Ramos, Hasselmann & Birattari, *Frontiers in Robotics and AI*, 2020), has e-puck robots project UV light onto a photochromic floor to lay and sense artificial pheromone. A cautionary empirical result: Hunt, Jones & Hauert, "Testing the limits of pheromone stigmergy in high-density robot swarms" (*Royal Society Open Science*, 2019, 6(11):190225). Repellent-pheromone dispersal had previously only been demonstrated for small swarms (N < 30); when the authors tested real Kilobots an order of magnitude larger (N > 300) and simulated up to N = 400, they found that "a simple stigmergic avoidance algorithm becomes first no better, then inferior to, the area coverage of non-interacting random walkers." Earlier termite-style material-deposition robots likewise showed *worsening* performance at larger group sizes (four or five versus three) due to time-consuming physical interactions. Stigmergy is powerful but not automatically scalable; the medium can get congested.

**Physical construction work — directly your world.** Lars Rune Christensen conducted 14 months of ethnographic fieldwork on Copenhagen building sites (including a glass/steel/concrete harbour-side building for a publishing house, with architects PLH Arkitekter and contractor PHIL & Søn) and published the most relevant scholarship to your situation:

- Christensen, "Stigmergy in Human Practice: Coordination in Construction Work," *Cognitive Systems Research* (2013), vol. 21, pp. 40–51, DOI 10.1016/j.cogsys.2012.06.004.
- Christensen, "Practices of Stigmergy in the Building Process," *CSCW* (2014), vol. 23(1), pp. 1–19, DOI 10.1007/s10606-012-9181-3.

His definition: "stigmergy refers to the phenomenon that distributed cooperative work tasks may be partly integrated by virtue of individuals paying heed to the material evidence of work previously accomplished by others while performing their own tasks." (He frames stigmergy as a "heed concept" in Gilbert Ryle's sense, and explicitly warns that to apply it to humans "we must exorcise or disassociate the concept of stigmergy from any stimuli-response model of action that may linger from the concept's origin in the field of entomology.") His central empirical example is the construction of **interior partition walls** by a sequence of trades: a carpenter builds the steel frame and clads one side; an electrician arrives later, "pays heed to the work carried out," and aligns the wiring with it; the carpenter returns to close the wall "in accordance with the previous work done"; finally the painter "finds the wall in this state" and paints it. No trade needs to talk to the next — "the individual actor creates and changes the form of the wall in-the-making, not for the purpose of conveying a message, but simply as part of performing their individually allotted tasks; in turn another actor pays heed to and acts upon the material evidence of the work of others." He notes a forward-looking variant directly analogous to your line: an electrician may "let a cable hang conspicuously visible in order to make the carpenter pay heed to it and afford it the required space" — deliberately enhancing the trace for the next worker (so they don't put a nail-gun nail through it). He also grounds *why* this happens: architectural plans are underspecified (Suchman 1987) — "not assembly manuals like those that come with for example IKEA furniture" — so workers must "fill in the blanks," and the pace of work means "as soon as one actor… has completed a task, time does not allow for much standing around and talking to the next."

Crucially, Christensen draws a distinction you will recognize: **stigmergy vs. "articulation work."** Stigmergy is first-order coordination "achieved by acting directly on the evidence of work previously accomplished." **Articulation work** (a term from Anselm Strauss, developed by Kjeld Schmidt) is the *second-order, reflexive* activity of coordinating the work — meetings, schedules, talk. Critically, "actors engaged in articulation work… may stop performing the tasks" to coordinate, whereas in stigmergy the coordination happens *inside* the doing of the task itself. **Your cable-pull line is stigmergy; your weekly site coordination meeting and your walkie-talkie are articulation work.** Christensen concludes that stigmergy, articulation work, and awareness form a "trinity" of distinct-but-interconnected modes, none interchangeable with the others.

### What's genuinely unified vs. what is analogy — the honest accounting

**Genuinely a shared mechanism (literal):**
- The abstract structure — *agent acts → leaves trace in shared medium → trace stimulates next action → feedback (positive amplification + decay)* — is the same across termites, ants, slime molds, ACO, swarm robots, kanban, FLOSS, Wikipedia, and your cable line. Heylighen, Parunak, and the Theraulaz/Bonabeau lineage treat this as one formally-specifiable mechanism, not a family of look-alikes.
- The **sematectonic core** — *the work medium itself carries the signal* — is literally instantiated in termite pillars, worn footpaths, Christensen's partition walls, the Wikipedia article, and your rope. These are the same phenomenon, not a metaphor.
- The **reinforce-plus-decay** dynamic is the same math in ACO, pheromone trails, and (with caveats) synaptic pruning.

**Useful analogy / metaphor (be careful):**
- Calling human institutions "stigmergic" can over-reach. Parunak provocatively wrote that "it would be more difficult to show a functioning human institution that is *not* stigmergic" — but that very ubiquity is a warning sign: a concept that explains everything risks explaining nothing. Several scholars (Dipple et al. 2014; the *Cognitive Systems Research* commentators) flag that loose use makes stigmergy sound trivial.
- Human stigmergy almost never operates *alone*. Christensen is emphatic that stigmergy, articulation work, and awareness form a "trinity" — each a distinct but interconnected mode — and that none is interchangeable with the others. Bolici/Howison/Crowston similarly find FLOSS coordination *blends* implicit/stigmergic and explicit mechanisms. So the claim "humans coordinate stigmergically" is true but partial; the walkie-talkie is not a failure of the system, it's the articulation-work layer doing necessary work the medium can't carry.
- The **extended-mind** leap (your creative thinking "is" distributed into the medium) is philosophically live but contested (Adams & Aizawa's coupling-constitution objection). It's a strong, defensible interpretive frame, not an established fact.
- **Intent differs.** Termites and ants have no intent; humans often deliberately shape traces *for* others (Christensen's conspicuously-hung cable; a well-written commit message). Some theorists argue this intentional, semantic layer means human "stigmergy" is a different beast — qualitative-marker-based with *meaning*, which insects lack. This is the sharpest live debate about whether "human stigmergy" stretches the concept.

## Recommendations

These are framed for someone who wants to *use* this concept — both on the job and as a thinking tool.

**Stage 1 — Name your system precisely (immediately useful).** You are running **quantitative sematectonic stigmergy** on the pull line (the rope tension is the trace; the medium is the signal) with a **marker-based articulation-work backup** (the walkie-talkie). Saying it this way isn't pedantry: it tells you *where to invest*. When the medium can carry the signal cleanly, push coordination into the medium (more people, less talk, lower error). When it can't — ambiguity, exceptions, planning, conflict — that's exactly when you *should* switch to the marker channel (the radio). Don't feel the radio is a failure; Christensen shows articulation work is a necessary, distinct layer.

**Stage 2 — Engineer the trace (the high-leverage move).** The lesson from ants, ACO, and Christensen's "conspicuously hung cable" is that *the quality of coordination is the quality of the trace*. Make the medium more legible: standard tension conventions, deliberate slack signals, physical cues left for the next person (the cable-management equivalent of a pheromone). In ACO terms, you want a clear signal and an appropriate **decay rate** — a stale trace (an old flag, an out-of-date status) is worse than none, because it misroutes the colony. Audit your shared media (boards, tags, physical staging) for stale traces the way Toyota audits kanban boards for "stale work items."

**Stage 3 — Watch for the congestion ceiling.** The robot-swarm research is the honest counterweight: stigmergy does **not** scale infinitely. Hunt/Jones/Hauert showed that beyond a few hundred agents a stigmergic avoidance algorithm became *inferior to non-interacting random walkers*, and termite-style robots got *worse* past three or four units because physical interactions became the bottleneck. Benchmark to change your approach: if adding people to the line *slows* it, or if your shared board becomes noise, you've hit the congestion ceiling — the fix is to *partition the medium* (separate trails/boards/work zones) or add a thin articulation-work layer, not to add more agents to the same trace.

**Stage 4 — Use it as a thinking tool, with epistemic honesty.** Your instinct to unify slime molds, ants, kanban, and the rope is sound *at the level of mechanism* (trace + medium + stimulation + feedback). Read Heylighen's "Stigmergy as a Universal Coordination Mechanism" (in Lewis & Marsh, eds., *Human Stigmergy*, Springer) as the best single synthesis, and Christensen (2013) as the one written about *your exact job*. But hold the unification at the right altitude: it's a shared *abstract structure*, not a claim that a neuron and a kanban card are the same kind of thing. The places where the analogy breaks (intent, meaning, scaling limits, the irreplaceable articulation-work layer) are where the most interesting thinking is.

**The benchmark that should change your mind:** If you find a coordination problem on the line that the *medium itself cannot encode* — something requiring negotiation, forecasting, or shared intent — that's the signal that you've reached the boundary of stigmergy and need explicit communication. The mark of mastery is knowing which layer a given problem belongs to.

## Caveats

- **The concept's breadth is both its power and its weakness.** "Stigmergy explains everything" (Parunak's near-tautology) is a real critique; the rigor lives in specifying the medium, trace, persistence/decay, and stimulation rule for a given case, not in the label.
- **Termites themselves are contested.** The textbook "pure sematectonic" example is partly pheromone-driven (marker-based), and the 2024 *eLife* study (Facchini et al., on *Coptotermes gestroi*) argues evaporation/curvature physics, not a building pheromone, may drive construction. The exact stigmergic stimuli in real termite nests are still not settled.
- **Synaptic pruning is only a partial match.** "Use it or lose it" is real but not universal — some cortical regions lose more than they keep regardless of use. Treat the neural analogy as illustrative of the reinforce/decay *dynamic*, not as identical to pheromone evaporation.
- **The extended-mind framing is philosophically contested** (Adams & Aizawa). It's a strong interpretive lens, not consensus fact.
- **Human stigmergy never works alone.** Christensen's "trinity" (stigmergy + articulation work + awareness) and the FLOSS findings (blended implicit/explicit) both insist that minimal-communication coordination is always supplemented. Your walkie-talkie is part of the system, not an exception to it.
- **Source quality note:** The strongest claims here rest on primary/peer-reviewed sources (Grassé 1959; Theraulaz & Bonabeau 1999; Dorigo et al. 1996; Reid et al. *PNAS* 2012; Hunt et al. 2019; Clark & Chalmers 1998; Christensen 2013/2014; Bolici/Howison/Crowston 2016; Facchini et al. 2024). Popular and encyclopedic sources were used only for orientation and corroborated against primary literature.
