Theory of Constraints (TOC) holds that the throughput of any goal-directed system is limited by a small number of constraints — often a single bottleneck — and that managing the system means continuously locating and elevating that constraint rather than optimizing everything at once.

origin: introduced by Eliyahu M. Goldratt with Jeff Cox in the 1984 business novel *The Goal*, which dramatized finding and managing a factory's bottleneck and the measures throughput, inventory, and operating expense [[source.goldratt-cox-1984]] [confirmed].

codification: named and set out as a methodology — the five focusing steps as a process of ongoing improvement — in Goldratt's 1990 monograph *What Is This Thing Called Theory of Constraints* [[source.goldratt-1990]] [confirmed]; the drum-buffer-rope scheduling logic was given its name a few years earlier in Goldratt & Fox's *The Race* (1986) [[source.goldratt-fox-1986]] [confirmed].

door: the multi-author *Theory of Constraints Handbook* (Cox & Schleier, eds., McGraw-Hill, 2010) surveys the field's components and applications and serves as the standard reference [[source.cox-schleier-2010]] [confirmed].

lineage: founding is uncontested — TOC is attributed throughout the literature to Eliyahu M. Goldratt, who developed it from the early 1980s onward and elaborated it across *The Goal*, *The Race*, and later works.

*grade [s] — origin (*The Goal*, 1984), codification (the five focusing steps in the 1990 monograph, with drum-buffer-rope named in *The Race*, 1986), and door (the 2010 *Theory of Constraints Handbook*) all present.*
