**statement (plain)** — a black box is any system you can only poke at from the outside: you send in inputs and watch the outputs, but you cannot (or do not) see the mechanism inside. The cybernetician's task is to infer as much as possible about the box's behavior from the input–output record alone, without opening it.

**statement** — a black box is a system characterized by the relation between its inputs and its outputs (its transfer behavior over time), with no assumption about, or access to, its internal structure. Investigation proceeds by applying input sequences and recording outputs, building a model of the box's behavior — and, where the structure is later exposed, the box becomes a "white box." **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the black box makes input–output behavior, not internal mechanism, the object of study — essential to cybernetics, where machine, organism, and brain are all approached by what they do at their boundaries. Ashby devotes a chapter of *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956) to the black box, arguing that real objects are *all* black boxes since complete internal knowledge is unattainable [[source.ashby-1956]]; Wiener's *Cybernetics* and its 1961 second edition draw the black-box / white-box distinction [[source.wiener-1948]]. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the term arose in WWII electronics/radar work (a sealed unit handled by its terminals); historians (Galison; von Hilgers) trace it to the MIT Radiation Laboratory and the 1940 Tizard Mission **[confirmed as attribution]** (secondary histories; originals not opened here). Its cybernetic formalization is Ashby's.
