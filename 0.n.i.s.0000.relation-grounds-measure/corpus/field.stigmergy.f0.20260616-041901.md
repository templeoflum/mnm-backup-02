Stigmergy studies how collectives coordinate indirectly: an action leaves a trace in a shared environment, and that trace stimulates the next action. The field spans the original ethology of social insects, the broader science of self-organization and collective behavior, and the engineering of swarm-intelligence and multi-agent systems.

**origin.** The concept and the term *stigmergie* were introduced by Pierre-Paul Grassé in 1959 to explain coordinated nest reconstruction in termites without any individual holding a plan [[source.grasse-1959]]. [confirmed]

**codification.** Theraulaz & Bonabeau's 1999 review "A Brief History of Stigmergy" consolidated the concept for the modern science of collective behavior and articulated the quantitative/qualitative distinction; Heylighen (2016) later generalized it into a component model of coordination spanning natural, human, and artificial systems [[source.theraulaz-bonabeau-1999]] [[source.heylighen-2016]]. [confirmed]

**door.** *Swarm Intelligence: From Natural to Artificial Systems* (Bonabeau, Dorigo & Theraulaz, 1999) is the standard textbook bridging the biological mechanism to its algorithmic descendants, ant-colony optimization chief among them [[source.bonabeau-dorigo-theraulaz-1999]]. [confirmed]

**lineage.** The biological strand (Grassé → Wilson's sematectonic communication → Theraulaz/Bonabeau → Heylighen) and the computational strand (Dorigo's Ant System → ant-colony optimization → swarm robotics) developed in parallel and were unified under "stigmergy" largely in the late 1990s; the founding of the *concept* is uncontested (Grassé 1959), while its elevation to a general coordination principle is a later, distributed development.

*grade [t] — origin, codification, and door all sourced; counted t because codification and door both rest on the same 1999 Bonabeau/Dorigo/Theraulaz authorship cluster rather than fully independent codifying traditions.*
