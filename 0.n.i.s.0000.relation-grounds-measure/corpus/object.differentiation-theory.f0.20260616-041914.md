**statement (plain)** — perceptual learning works by *differentiation*: with experience the perceiver comes to discriminate variables of stimulation that were always there but previously undetected — distinctive features, higher-order invariants, and structure. Perception grows richer in what it discriminates and comes into closer correspondence with the stimulus, rather than being enriched from within by added images, inferences, or associations (the "enrichment" alternative the theory rejects).

**basis** — the Gibsons' 1955 paper poses the differentiation-vs-enrichment dichotomy and argues for differentiation, holding that "perception gets richer in differential responses, not in images" and moves into greater correspondence with stimulation [[source.gibson-gibson-1955]] **[confirmed]**. E. J. Gibson (1969) develops it systematically as extraction of distinctive features and invariants across development [[source.gibson-1969]] **[confirmed]**. Goldstone (1998) lists differentiation as one of four mechanisms, situating it within a broader, no-longer-exclusive account [[source.goldstone-1998]] **[confirmed]**. **Limits:** later work treats differentiation as one mechanism among several (with attention weighting, imprinting, unitization), not the whole of perceptual learning.
