**statement (plain)** — dissociation is a partial or complete disconnection between mental processes that are normally integrated — between awareness and memory, between intention and action, between one stream of perception and another. Dissociated contents (memories, perceptions, fixed ideas, whole behavior systems) operate outside or alongside central consciousness, sometimes inaccessible to it, sometimes running on their own.

**statement (clinical / theoretical)** — Janet's *désagrégation*: under a narrowing of the *field of consciousness* (low psychological tension, often trauma-driven), subsystems of ideas detach from the personal "synthesis" and persist as autonomous **fixed ideas** and automatisms [[source.janet-1889]]. Hilgard's **neodissociation**: cognition is organized as multiple subordinate control structures under an executive; dissociation is a functional barrier (amnesic or attentional) between these structures, demonstrated in hypnotic analgesia via the "hidden observer" [[source.hilgard-1977]].

**basis (empirical / method)** — *support*: a reproducible body of clinical and experimental findings — hypnotic analgesia with concurrent hidden-observer reports, posthypnotic amnesia, dissociative amnesia and fugue, automatic writing under divided attention; dissociation is an operationalized construct in standardized measures and in DSM dissociative disorders. *limits*: the theoretical *interpretation* is contested — the "hidden observer" can be shaped by suggestion (a social-cognitive rather than structural reading), and the boundary between genuine division and strategic role-enactment is not settled. The phenomenon is robust; the mechanism is debated.

**lineage** — descends from the French clinical tradition (Janet, Charcot, Binet) and from earlier "double consciousness" reports; in tension with social-cognitive / role-theory accounts of hypnosis (Spanos, Sarbin) which explain the same data without a structural split. Both are live positions in the literature.

**sources** — [[source.janet-1889]]; [[source.hilgard-1977]]; SEP / standard clinical-psychology references on dissociation; van der Hart & Horst, "The dissociation theory of Pierre Janet," *J. Traumatic Stress* (1989). **[confirmed]**
