**statement (plain)** — Lewis sorts possession cults by their relation to power. **Central possession** is socially valued and often male-dominated; it upholds the established morality and the values of dominant groups. **Peripheral (marginal) possession** clusters among the socially subordinate — frequently women and low-status men — and is associated with amoral, malicious, intrusive spirits. Lewis's **deprivation thesis** reads peripheral possession as an oblique, deniable form of protest and bargaining: recruitment compensates for socio-economic deprivation and "jural impotence," letting the marginalized make demands (attention, goods, redress) through the spirit rather than in their own name.

**basis** — built from comparative ethnography of women's possession cults (East African and Maghrebi *zar* / *bori*, Haitian Vodou) alongside Arctic shamanism [[source.lewis-1971]]; it sits within the broader cross-cultural frame relating possession trance to social position [[source.bourguignon-1973]]. **[confirmed]**

lineage — the thesis is a recognized and influential position, but contested. Empirical tests (e.g. Esther Pressel's and others' Brazilian Umbanda data, and Sheila Walker's critique) found only weak support for deprivation as the recruitment driver; later scholarship treats it as a partial account to be supplemented by performative, therapeutic, and constructionist readings rather than a complete theory. Recorded here as an established scholarly position with its objections carried, not asserted as true.

**sources** — [[source.lewis-1971]]; [[source.bourguignon-1973]]; "A Brazilian Challenge to Lewis's Explanation of Cult Mediumship" (semanticscholar.org / researchgate); SPR Psi Encyclopedia, "Mediumship and Spirit Possession in Cross-Cultural Context." **[confirmed]**
