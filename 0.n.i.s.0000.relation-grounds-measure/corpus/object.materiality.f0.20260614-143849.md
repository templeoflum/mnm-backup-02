**statement (plain)** — not every error in financial statements matters. An error is "material" if knowing about it could reasonably change the decision of a sensible person relying on the statements. Auditors set a materiality threshold while planning so they spend effort on what could actually mislead users, and they judge uncorrected errors against it at the end.

**statement** — misstatements, including omissions, are material if "they, individually or in the aggregate, could reasonably be expected to influence the economic decisions of users taken on the basis of the financial statements." Materiality is a matter of **professional judgment**, affected by the auditor's perception of the financial-information needs of users as a group, and is applied both in planning/performing the audit and in evaluating the effect of identified and uncorrected misstatements when forming the opinion. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — codified in [[source.au-c-320]] (*Materiality in Planning and Performing an Audit*), which mirrors IAASB ISA 320. The "reasonable user" assumption: users have a reasonable knowledge of business and accounting and study the statements with reasonable diligence, understand that statements are prepared and audited to levels of materiality, and recognize the uncertainties inherent in measuring amounts. Judgments turn on both **size and nature** (quantitative and qualitative factors) of a misstatement. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — the decision-influence standard derives from disclosure law and accounting-standards definitions (the "reasonable investor / reasonable user" test); the auditing standards import it as the operating threshold for evidence and evaluation. Materiality feeds directly into the [[object.audit-risk-model]] (audit risk is the risk of an inappropriate opinion *when statements are materially misstated*) and bounds [[object.audit-sampling]] (tolerable misstatement/deviation).
