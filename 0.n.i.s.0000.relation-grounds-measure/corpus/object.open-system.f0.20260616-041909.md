**statement (plain)** — An *open* system continuously exchanges materials and energy with its surroundings, in contrast to a *closed* system, which exchanges only energy, and an *isolated* system, which exchanges neither. Because of this throughput an open system can maintain itself in a *steady state* — a constant composition sustained by continuous inflow and outflow — that is distinct from chemical and thermodynamic equilibrium. Living organisms are the paradigm case: they hold their structure not by reaching equilibrium but by staying far from it through ongoing metabolism. [[source.von-bertalanffy-1950]] **[confirmed]**

**basis** — Bertalanffy's 1950 *Science* paper applies open-system thermodynamics to biology, deriving the steady state and equifinality and arguing the organism is essentially an open system [[source.von-bertalanffy-1950]]; the 1968 book develops the formal treatment (flow equations, steady states, equifinality) [[source.von-bertalanffy-1968]]. **Support**: the concept is foundational across non-equilibrium thermodynamics, systems biology, and ecology; the second law applies to the system-plus-environment, so an organism can lower its internal entropy by importing low-entropy energy and exporting entropy ("negentropy"/dissipative structures, later formalized by Prigogine). **Limits**: "open system" is a defining concept and modeling stance, not a quantitative law in itself; precise predictions require specific flow models, and the open/closed/isolated trichotomy is an idealization of boundaries that real systems only approximate. **[confirmed]**
