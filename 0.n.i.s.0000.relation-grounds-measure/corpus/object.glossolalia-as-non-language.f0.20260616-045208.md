**statement (plain)** — Linguistic and acoustic analysis finds that glossolalia is not a language: although it carries the surface prosody and phonology of speech, it has no stable lexicon, no systematic mapping between sound-units and meaning, and no internal grammatical organization. It is a "facade of language" — speech-like, but not a code that encodes or transmits propositional content.

**statement (formal)** — Operationally: glossolalic utterance exhibits phonological structure (a constrained phoneme inventory, syllabic and intonational patterning) but fails the criteria of a natural language — no recurrent form-meaning correspondences, no productive morphology/syntax, and no systematic resemblance to any attested living or dead language.

**basis (empirical)** — Samarin's corpus analysis establishes that the syllable strings form no words, the speech stream is not internally organized, and there is no systematic relation between units of speech and concepts [[source.samarin-1972]] [confirmed]. The psycholinguistic/acoustic study by Osser et al. independently analyzes glossolalic samples against General American English and finds patterned but non-lexical structure, distinguishing "formulaic" (stereotyped) from "innovative" forms [[source.osser-1973]] [confirmed]. Goodman's cross-cultural phonological analysis corroborates that the regularities are phonetic/suprasegmental, not those of a natural language [[source.goodman-1972]] [confirmed].

**limits** — The finding is about linguistic structure, not about value or authenticity of the experience: it says glossolalia is not a natural language, not that it is meaningless to the speaker. The studies sample chiefly Pentecostal and comparable ritual settings; the religious interpretation held by practitioners is bracketed, not contradicted, by the linguistic result.
