Glossolalia studies is the empirical investigation of "speaking in tongues" — fluent, speech-like vocal behavior that resembles language but carries no decodable lexical content. The field studies the phenomenon as linguists, phoneticians, anthropologists, and neuroscientists: it describes the sound structure of the utterances, the cross-cultural and altered-state conditions under which they are produced, and the brain activity that accompanies them. The religious significance the behavior holds for its practitioners is recorded as held, not adjudicated.

**origin** — William J. Samarin's *Tongues of Men and Angels* (1972), a sociolinguist's analysis of a five-year recorded corpus of Pentecostal glossolalia, established the field's central empirical claim: glossolalia is phonologically structured human utterance that resembles language only at the surface and bears no systematic relation to any natural language [[source.samarin-1972]] [confirmed].

**codification** — Felicitas D. Goodman's *Speaking in Tongues: A Cross-Cultural Study of Glossolalia* (University of Chicago Press, 1972) generalized the inquiry across cultures, comparing Pentecostal and non-Christian samples and tying glossolalia's phonetic and suprasegmental regularities to an altered (trance) mental state [[source.goodman-1972]] [confirmed].

**door** — Cardeña, Lynn & Krippner (eds.), *Varieties of Anomalous Experience: Examining the Scientific Evidence* (American Psychological Association, 2nd ed. 2014) is the standard scientific survey situating glossolalia among studied dissociative/anomalous experiences and supplying the broader evidential frame [[source.cardena-lynn-2014]] [confirmed].

**lineage** — the linguistic reading (Samarin: surface-only resemblance, "facade of language") and the altered-state reading (Goodman: trance-driven articulation) are complementary rather than rival; the psycholinguistic corpus (Osser et al. 1973) and the SPECT neuroimaging (Newberg et al. 2006) extend the same empirical program into acoustics and brain activity respectively.

*grade [s] — origin (Samarin), codification (Goodman), and door (Cardeña/Lynn/Krippner) all sourced.*
