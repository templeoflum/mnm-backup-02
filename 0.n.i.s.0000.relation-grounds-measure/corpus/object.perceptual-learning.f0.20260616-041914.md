**statement (plain)** — perceptual learning is the set of relatively long-lasting changes to a perceptual system that result from experience and that improve the system's ability to respond to its environment. It is change in *perception itself* — in what information the perceiver picks up and how efficiently — not merely the learning of new responses, concepts, or associations attached to a fixed percept.

**basis** — Goldstone (1998) defines it as "relatively long-lasting changes to an organism's perceptual system that improve its ability to respond to its environment" and reviews four mechanisms: attention weighting, imprinting, differentiation, and unitization [[source.goldstone-1998]] **[confirmed]**. Kellman & Garrigan (2009) cast it as experience-induced change in information extraction and a core contributor to expertise, distinguishing discovery effects from fluency effects [[source.kellman-garrigan-2009]] **[confirmed]**. The construct originates with the Gibsons' 1955 framing [[source.gibson-gibson-1955]] and E. J. Gibson's 1969 monograph [[source.gibson-1969]]. **Limits:** mechanisms are multiple and partly competing; not a single unified process, and the boundary with attention, memory, and conceptual learning is debated.

**sources** — [[source.goldstone-1998]]; [[source.kellman-garrigan-2009]]; [[source.gibson-gibson-1955]]; [[source.gibson-1969]].
