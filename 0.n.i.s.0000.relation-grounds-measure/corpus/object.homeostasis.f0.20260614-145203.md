**statement (plain)** — homeostasis is a system's tendency to keep its vital internal conditions roughly constant despite a changing outside world. The body holds temperature, blood pH, glucose, and fluid balance within narrow ranges; when a sensor detects drift, corrective machinery pushes the variable back. It is the biological face of negative feedback.

**statement** — homeostasis is the maintenance of a steady internal state (a regulated variable held within tolerance) by an organism or system, achieved through control loops in which a *receptor* (sensor) detects deviation from a set point, a *control center* compares it to the reference, and an *effector* acts to reduce the deviation — i.e., negative-feedback regulation. **[confirmed]**

**basis** — Claude Bernard (1865) established that complex life depends on the constancy of the *milieu intérieur* (the internal environment) regardless of external change. Walter B. Cannon coined "homeostasis" in 1926 and developed it through 1929 and 1932 [[source.cannon-1932]] to name the active, coordinated regulation that sustains that constancy. Cybernetics reread homeostasis as a special case of [[object.feedback-loop]] regulation: Ashby's electromechanical *homeostat* (1948) and *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956) treat it as the model of a system that returns to stable states after disturbance [[source.ashby-1956]]. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — *milieu intérieur*: Claude Bernard, 1865 **[confirmed as attribution]** (cited via Cannon and reference works; Bernard's original not opened here). Term "homeostasis": Cannon, 1926 **[confirmed]**.
