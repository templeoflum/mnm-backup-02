**statement (plain)** — generative art is art the artist doesn't make directly. Instead the artist builds a system — a set of rules, a program, a machine, a procedure — and lets it run on its own, and what the system produces is the artwork. The defining move is handing some control to the process; the medium (computer, dice, language) does not matter.

**statement** — Galanter's definition (GA2003): "Generative art refers to any art practice where the artist uses a system, such as a set of natural language rules, a computer program, a machine, or other procedural invention, which is set into motion with some degree of autonomy contributing to or resulting in a completed work of art." [[source.galanter-2003]] **[confirmed]**

**basis** — the definition is deliberately broad and medium-neutral. Two consequences are load-bearing: (1) generative art **predates computers** — Galanter cites the 18th-century *Musikalisches Würfelspiel* (musical dice games) and earlier procedural practices; (2) the hinge is **autonomy** — the artist cedes some authorial control to the running of the system. Galanter frames the field through complexity theory, placing works on a continuum from highly ordered to highly disordered, with the most interesting generative work near the "edge" between order and disorder. **[confirmed]**

**lineage** — young, practitioner-origin, contested field (flagged on [[field.generative-live-visual-art]]). No single consolidating treatise: codification is split between Frieder Nake's information-aesthetics monograph *Ästhetik als Informationsverarbeitung* (Springer, 1974) [[source.nake-1974]] and Galanter's later definitional conference paper. The computer-art origin (the "3N": Nees, Noll, Nake; 1965) is documented but distinct from the *definition*, which arrived decades after the practice. [[source.nees-1969]] [[source.noll-1965]] Some practitioners reject Galanter's breadth as too inclusive — carried as contest, not error.
