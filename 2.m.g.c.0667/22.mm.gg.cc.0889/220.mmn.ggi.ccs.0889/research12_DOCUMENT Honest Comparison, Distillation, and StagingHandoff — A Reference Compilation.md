## TARGET 3A — "The blade": honest structured-vs-flat comparison + statistical reporting

### 3A.1 — Avoiding fitting-to-noise / test-set overfitting / adaptive data analysis

**Dwork, Feldman, Hardt, Pitassi, Reingold & Roth (2015), "The reusable holdout: Preserving validity in adaptive data analysis," *Science* 349(6248):636–638, DOI 10.1126/science.aaa9375.** Companion technical papers: "Preserving Statistical Validity in Adaptive Data Analysis" and "Generalization in Adaptive Data Analysis and Holdout Reuse" (NIPS 2015, arXiv:1506.02629). Problem: standard validity guarantees assume a fixed analysis chosen before seeing data, but real analysis is *adaptive* — new hypotheses are generated based on prior results on the same data, which causes false discovery. The method draws on differential privacy. The named mechanism is **Thresholdout**: it answers queries about the holdout only when the holdout statistic differs from the training statistic by more than a noisy threshold (T+η); otherwise it returns the training answer. This lets a holdout set be reused many times while preserving validity. Their Figure 1 demonstrates "learning an uncorrelated label": the standard holdout overfits as the analyst adapts, while Thresholdout tracks the fresh-set accuracy.

**Blum & Hardt (2015), "The Ladder: A Reliable Leaderboard for Machine Learning Competitions," ICML 2015 (arXiv:1502.04585, submitted Feb 16 2015).** Problem: competition leaderboards (e.g., Kaggle) are adaptive — participants repeatedly submit and overfit the holdout supporting the public leaderboard. The **Ladder mechanism** updates (and reveals) a participant's best score only when a new submission's empirical loss improves on the previous best by more than a statistically meaningful margin; otherwise the old score stands. This limits the information leaked, preventing overfitting, and supports leaderboard-accuracy guarantees in a fully adaptive model. Follow-up: Hardt (2017), "Climbing a shaky ladder: Better adaptive risk estimation" (arXiv:1706.02733) shows a randomized Ladder achieving leaderboard error O(1/n^0.4) vs the previous O(1/n^{1/3}).

**Recht, Roelofs, Schmidt & Shankar (2019), "Do ImageNet Classifiers Generalize to ImageNet?," ICML 2019, PMLR 97:5389–5400 (arXiv:1902.10811).** Method: built brand-new test sets for CIFAR-10 and ImageNet by closely replicating the original dataset-creation pipelines, then evaluated a broad range of existing models. Headline finding: accuracy drops of **3%–15% on CIFAR-10 and 11%–14% on ImageNet** on the new sets. To convey scale of effect, the paper states (verbatim): "On ImageNet, the accuracy loss amounts to approximately five years of progress in a highly active period of machine learning research." Crucially, "accuracy gains on the original test sets translate to *larger* gains on the new test sets," and the authors conclude the drops are **not** caused by adaptive overfitting but by the models' inability to generalize to slightly "harder" images. (This is a notable nuance: despite years of test-set reuse, they did not find evidence of catastrophic adaptive overfitting — the gap is a distribution-shift/hardness effect.) Active researchers: Benjamin Recht, Ludwig Schmidt, Rebecca Roelofs, Vaishaal Shankar (UC Berkeley).

**The prescribed discipline.** The literature converges on: split into train/validation/test; do all model selection and adaptive exploration on train/validation; and **touch the held-out test set as few times as possible (ideally once)**. When repeated evaluation is unavoidable, use a privacy/noise-based mechanism (Thresholdout, Ladder) to bound the information extracted from the holdout.

### 3A.2 — Statistical-comparison practice for ML experiments

**Dietterich (1998), "Approximate Statistical Tests for Comparing Supervised Classification Learning Algorithms," *Neural Computation* 10(7):1895–1923, DOI 10.1162/089976698300017197.** Reviews **five** approximate statistical tests for deciding whether one algorithm outperforms another on a task, comparing their Type I error (falsely detecting a difference). Findings: two widely used tests have high Type I error and "should never be used" — (1) a test for the difference of two proportions, and (2) a paired-differences t-test based on multiple random train-test splits. A paired-differences t-test on 10-fold cross-validation shows "somewhat elevated" Type I error. **McNemar's test** is the only test found to have acceptable Type I error; for algorithms that can be run 10 times, the **5×2cv test** is recommended as slightly more powerful.

**Demšar (2006), "Statistical Comparisons of Classifiers over Multiple Data Sets," *JMLR* 7:1–30.** Addresses comparing classifiers across *many* datasets. Recommends **non-parametric** tests: the **Wilcoxon signed-ranks test** for comparing two classifiers, and the **Friedman test** (ranks classifiers per dataset) with corresponding **post-hoc tests** (Nemenyi) for comparing multiple classifiers over multiple datasets. Introduced the now-standard **critical difference (CD) diagram** for visualizing average ranks. The Friedman statistic χ²_F is computed from average ranks; if the null (all classifiers equivalent) is rejected, Nemenyi decides which pairs differ.

**Dror, Baumer, Shlomov & Reichart (2018), "The Hitchhiker's Guide to Testing Statistical Significance in Natural Language Processing," ACL 2018, pp. 1383–1392.** Establishes significance-testing concepts for NLP, discusses how task/setup/evaluation-measure properties dictate test choice, and proposes a practical decision protocol plus a survey of relevant tests (a companion appendix, arXiv:1809.01448, maps tasks/measures to recommended tests). Survey finding (their ACL 2017 review): of 180 experimental long papers, only 63 checked statistical significance and only 42 named the test used — i.e., "117 out of 180 experimental ACL 2017 papers and 15 out of 33 TACL 2017 papers did not report significance testing." Mentions the **paired bootstrap** and **approximate randomization** tests as common in NLP. Related work by the same group: "Replicability Analysis" for multiple-dataset comparisons in NLP.

**Card, Henderson, Khandelwal, Jia, Mahowald & Jurafsky (2020), "With Little Power Comes Great Responsibility," EMNLP 2020, pp. 9263–9274 (arXiv:2010.06595).** Focuses on **statistical power** (probability of detecting a real effect). Meta-analyzes existing NLP papers/datasets and concludes underpowered experiments are common. Specific quantitative findings: for several GLUE tasks, small test sets mean most attempted SOTA comparisons are underpowered; "For machine translation, we find that typical test sets of 2000 sentences have approximately 75% power to detect differences of 1 BLEU point" (verbatim); typical human-evaluation designs are underpowered for small model differences. Provides best-practice power-analysis guidance and released notebooks.

**Bouthillier, Delaunay, Bronzi, et al. (2021), "Accounting for Variance in Machine Learning Benchmarks," MLSys 2021 (arXiv:2103.03098).** Models the *whole* benchmarking pipeline and its variance sources: **data sampling, data augmentation, parameter (weight) initialization, and hyperparameter choice** — showing all markedly affect results, so single-run (single-seed) comparisons are unreliable. Counter-intuitive result: an estimator that randomizes *multiple* sources of variation approximates the ideal estimator better, at a **51× reduction in compute** versus controlling them. Provides recommendations (randomized trials, resampling) for performance comparisons. (Senior authors include Gaël Varoquaux and Pascal Vincent.)

**Reporting standards that emerge:** use multiple random seeds; report mean and variance/standard deviation (and confidence intervals); run an appropriate significance test (McNemar/5×2cv/paired bootstrap/randomization per setting); for multiple comparisons apply corrections — **Bonferroni** (controls family-wise error) or **Benjamini–Hochberg** false discovery rate (the BH/FDR procedure); report effect sizes and power.

### 3A.3 — Ablations, controlled A/B comparison, matched budgets, reproducibility checklists

**Lipton & Steinhardt (2018/2019), "Troubling Trends in Machine Learning Scholarship," arXiv:1807.03341; Communications of the ACM 62(6):45–53 (2019).** Identifies four recurring failure patterns; the two most relevant: **(i) failure to distinguish explanation from speculation**, and **(ii) failure to identify the sources of empirical gains** — e.g., crediting architecture changes when gains actually come from hyperparameter tuning. Directly motivates ablation discipline: to attribute a gain to "structure," vary that one factor while holding everything else constant.

**Dodge, Gururangan, Card, Schwartz & Smith (2019), "Show Your Work: Improved Reporting of Experimental Results," EMNLP-IJCNLP 2019, pp. 2185–2194.** Argues that test-set scores alone are insufficient. Introduces **expected validation performance** — the expected performance of the best-found model as a function of compute budget (number of hyperparameter search trials / training time). Shows multiple published comparisons would flip conclusions under different compute budgets, and that reported compute to reach a given accuracy varies "from hours to weeks" across papers. Prescribes reporting validation performance, search space, number of trials, and compute budget so comparisons are matched — directly supporting "matched compute/hyperparameter budgets."

**Pineau et al. (2021), "Improving Reproducibility in Machine Learning Research (A Report from the NeurIPS 2019 Reproducibility Program)," *JMLR* 22(164):1–20 (arXiv:2003.12206).** Describes the NeurIPS 2019 program's three components: a **code-submission policy**, a **community-wide reproducibility challenge**, and inclusion of the **Machine Learning Reproducibility Checklist** in the paper-submission process. The checklist (first introduced NeurIPS 2018, updated 2019/2020) asks authors to provide details on datasets, code, hyperparameters, number of training/evaluation runs, definitions of metrics, and computing infrastructure. Reproducibility is defined as obtaining similar results using the same code and data. Lead: Joelle Pineau (McGill/Meta).

## TARGET 3B — Distillation: what transfers, what is left behind

### 3B.1 — Logit-level / response-based KD mechanics and recent LLM practice

**Hinton, Vinyals & Dean (2015), "Distilling the Knowledge in a Neural Network," arXiv:1503.02531 (NIPS 2014 Deep Learning Workshop).** Generalizes Bucilă, Caruana & Niculescu-Mizil (2006) "model compression." Mechanics: a softmax with **temperature T** converts logits z_i to probabilities q_i = exp(z_i/T) / Σ_j exp(z_j/T). Higher T produces a **softer** distribution over classes. The student is trained on a **transfer set** using the teacher's soft targets (computed at high T); the same high T is used on the student's softmax during training, then T is reset to 1 at inference. When true labels are available, the objective is a **weighted average of two losses**: the **distillation loss** (cross-entropy vs the teacher's soft targets at temperature T) and the **student loss** (standard cross-entropy vs the hard ground-truth labels). MNIST result: a large net got 67 test errors, a small unregularized net got 146 errors, but the small net trained also to match soft targets at T=20 got **74 errors**. Also introduced **specialist networks** (trained on confusable class subsets) for large-scale ensembles.

**Sequence-level KD** (Kim & Rush, 2016, "Sequence-Level Knowledge Distillation") extends KD to autoregressive sequence models by training the student on the teacher's generated output sequences (rather than per-token distributions).

**Agarwal, Vieillard, Zhou, Stanczyk, Ramos, Geist & Bachem (2023/2024), "On-Policy Distillation of Language Models: Learning from Self-Generated Mistakes" — Generalized Knowledge Distillation (GKD)," arXiv:2306.13649 (ICLR 2024).** Problem: standard KD for autoregressive models suffers a **train–inference distribution mismatch** (the student sees teacher/fixed sequences in training but its own sequences at inference). **GKD** trains the student on its **own self-generated, on-policy output sequences**, scored by teacher feedback. It allows alternative divergences — **forward KL, reverse KL, and generalized Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD(β))** — and a student-data fraction λ (λ=0 supervised, λ=1 on-policy, λ=0.5 mixed). GKD integrates with RL fine-tuning (RLHF). Evaluated on summarization, machine translation, and arithmetic reasoning (GSM8K). Standard KD is framed as behavior cloning in imitation-learning terms.

**Gu, Dong, Wei & Huang (2023/2024), "MiniLLM: Knowledge Distillation of Large Language Models," arXiv:2306.08543 (ICLR 2024).** Replaces the standard **forward KL divergence** objective with **reverse KLD**, arguing reverse KL is more appropriate for generative LLMs because it prevents the student from **overestimating low-probability regions** of the teacher distribution (forward KL is "mode-covering," reverse KL is "mode-seeking"). Derives a policy-gradient-style on-policy optimization with stabilization tricks (single-step decomposition, teacher-mixed sampling, length normalization). Scales across model families from **120M to 13B parameters**. Reported gains: more precise responses, higher overall quality, **lower exposure bias, better calibration**, higher long-text-generation performance vs standard KD; preserves distinct-4-gram diversity and test-set LM loss.

**Xu, Li, Tao, Shen, Cheng, Li, Xu, Tao & Zhou (2024), "A Survey on Knowledge Distillation of Large Language Models," arXiv:2402.13116 (v4, Oct 2024).** Structured around three pillars: **algorithm, skill, and verticalization**. Frames KD's three roles for LLMs: (1) transferring advanced capabilities from proprietary models (GPT-4) to open models (LLaMA, Mistral); (2) compressing LLMs; (3) self-improvement (a model as its own teacher). Emphasizes **data augmentation (DA)** as the prevalent modern KD paradigm — a small knowledge seed prompts the LLM to generate skill/domain-specific training data. Companion GitHub: "Awesome-Knowledge-Distillation-of-LLMs."

### 3B.2 — Generalization vs memorization: what soft targets carry, and the conflict

**"Dark knowledge"** (Hinton et al. 2015): the information in the soft targets beyond the top-1 label — the relative probabilities assigned to *wrong* classes encode the teacher's learned **inter-class similarity structure** (e.g., a "2" looks more like a "3" than a "7"). This is what hard one-hot labels do not carry, and it acts as a regularizer that helps the student generalize (Hinton reported soft-target training generalized well without needing early stopping).

**The conflict — Stanton, Izmailov, Kirichenko, Alemi & Wilson (2021), "Does Knowledge Distillation Really Work?," NeurIPS 2021 (arXiv:2106.05945).** Distinguishes **fidelity** (how well the student matches the teacher's predictive distribution) from **generalization** (student test accuracy). Key finding: KD often **improves student generalization but does NOT work as commonly understood** — there frequently remains "a surprisingly large discrepancy between the predictive distributions of the teacher and the student," even when the student has enough capacity to match the teacher perfectly. They attribute this to a difficult optimization problem. Paradox: in self-distillation, higher fidelity does **not** always yield better generalization (e.g., enlarging the CIFAR-100 distillation set with GAN samples raised fidelity but *lowered* test accuracy); for large ensemble teachers, fidelity and generalization become more positively correlated but a large fidelity gap persists. (Follow-up "Why does Knowledge Distillation Work? Rethink its Attention and Fidelity Mechanism," Expert Systems with Applications 2024, argues logit-matching optimization improves generalization *at the cost of* fidelity, attributing the effect to teacher attention-map diversity.)

**Memorization / privacy direction — both findings present.**
- *Distillation as a privacy defense:* Shejwalkar & Houmansadr (2019/2021), "Reconciling Utility and Membership Privacy via Knowledge Distillation" / "Distillation for Membership Privacy (DMP)," arXiv:1906.06589 — uses KD to train models resistant to membership-inference attacks (MIAs) while preserving utility better than differential-privacy or adversarial-regularization baselines. Implies distillation can leave memorization behind.
- *Conflicting recent LLM finding:* Zhang et al. (2025), "Membership and Memorization in LLM Knowledge Distillation," arXiv:2508.07054 — systematically studies six LLM KD techniques (KD, SeqKD, GKD, ImitKD, MiniLLM, DistiLLM) across seven NLP tasks and three teacher families (GPT-2, LLaMA-2, OPT). Finding: "all existing LLM KD approaches carry membership and memorization privacy risks from the teacher to its students," though the extent varies by technique. Uses seven MIAs (Min-K%++, Min-K%, Zlib, LOSS, and reference-based attacks). **Flag the conflict:** distillation can be used to *reduce* memorization/membership leakage (DMP), yet under other conditions it *transfers* the teacher's memorization/membership risk to the student.

### 3B.3 — Dataset distillation vs model distillation (two senses of "distillation")

**Model / knowledge distillation** (Hinton et al. 2015 and successors): compress the **MODEL** — transfer a teacher's learned input→output behavior into a smaller student. What is left behind: exact teacher weights/architecture, and (per Stanton 2021) often a substantial fraction of the teacher's exact predictive distribution (the fidelity gap).

**Dataset distillation / dataset condensation** (Wang, Zhu, Torralba & Efros, 2018, "Dataset Distillation," arXiv:1811.10959): compress the **DATA** — synthesize a small set of (often synthetic) training examples such that a model trained on them performs comparably to one trained on the full real dataset. The original method models network parameters as a function of the synthetic data and learns the synthetic data by backpropagating through training steps. Successor methods: **Dataset Condensation with Gradient Matching** (Zhao, Mopuri & Bilen, ICLR 2021); distribution matching; **Matching Training Trajectories** (Cazenavette et al., CVPR 2022); neural-tangent-kernel approaches (Nguyen et al., NeurIPS 2021). Applications: continual-learning replay, neural-architecture search, privacy. What is left behind: the original individual real data points (a privacy benefit, since the synthetic set need not resemble raw data). **Definitional difference:** both are "distillation," but model/knowledge distillation compresses a *trained model's behavior* while dataset distillation compresses a *training set* into synthetic samples.

## TARGET 3C — Scale schema + gate: staging to a larger layer, and the handoff record

### 3C.1 — Staging small→large: curriculum, scaling laws, the escalation gate

**Bengio, Louradour, Collobert & Weston (2009), "Curriculum Learning," ICML 2009, pp. 41–48, DOI 10.1145/1553374.1553380.** Proposes presenting training examples in a meaningful order — **easy/simpler examples first, then progressively harder ones** — analogous to human/animal "shaping." Key claim (verbatim): "curriculum learning has both an effect on the speed of convergence of the training process to a minimum and, in the case of non-convex criteria, on the quality of the local minima obtained: curriculum learning can be seen as a particular form of continuation method." For convex objectives, ordering affects only convergence speed; for non-convex objectives (deep nets), it affects both speed and which local minimum is found. Demonstrated improved generalization and faster convergence on vision and language tasks.

**Kaplan, McCandlish, Henighan, Brown, Chess, Child, Gray, Radford, Wu & Amodei (2020), "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models," arXiv:2001.08361.** Finding (verbatim): "The loss scales as a power-law with model size, dataset size, and the amount of compute used for training, with some trends spanning more than seven orders of magnitude." Test loss L follows smooth power laws in N (parameters), D (tokens), and C (compute) when not bottlenecked by the others; architectural details (width/depth) matter little within a wide range. Compute-allocation claim (verbatim): "optimally compute-efficient training involves training very large models on a relatively modest amount of data and stopping significantly before convergence."

**Hoffmann, Borgeaud, Mensch, et al. (2022), "Training Compute-Optimal Large Language Models" (the Chinchilla paper), arXiv:2203.15556; NeurIPS 2022.** Verbatim abstract: "By training over 400 language models ranging from 70 million to over 16 billion parameters on 5 to 500 billion tokens, we find that for compute-optimal training, the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally: for every doubling of model size the number of training tokens should also be doubled." This is widely interpreted as **~20 training tokens per parameter** (derived from the paper's tables/coefficients, not a verbatim headline figure — flag). Demonstration: **Chinchilla (70B parameters, 1.4 trillion tokens)** trained with the same compute as **Gopher (280B parameters, 300B tokens)** — i.e., 4× smaller, ~4× more data — "uniformly and significantly outperforms Gopher (280B), GPT-3 (175B), Jurassic-1 (178B), and Megatron-Turing NLG (530B)." Chinchilla reached "a state-of-the-art average accuracy of 67.5% on the MMLU benchmark, greater than a 7% improvement over Gopher." Core thesis: prior large models were significantly **undertrained** for their compute. **Escalation/gate logic:** scaling laws let a small-scale loss-vs-(N,D,C) measurement be fit and **extrapolated** to predict the loss (and therefore the data/compute requirement) of a larger next layer, before paying for it.

### 3C.2 — Provenance and versioning tooling/standards

**DVC (Data Version Control), Iterative.ai (open-source, first beta 2017).** Records three things: (1) **data and model versioning** — large files/datasets/models are replaced in Git by small metafiles (`.dvc` pointers/hashes) while the actual data lives in remote storage (S3, GCS, Azure, SSH, NAS) — "Git for data"; (2) **pipelines/lineage** — multi-stage DAGs in `dvc.yaml` give explicit data lineage linking code versions to data states and rerun only impacted stages; (3) **experiment tracking** — local, Git-based params/metrics/plots. Design principles: codification, versioning via Git, reproducibility, GitOps. (Flag: secondary reports of a lakeFS acquisition in late 2025 — treat as current/secondary.)

**MLflow Tracking.** Per official docs, it is "an API and UI for logging parameters, code versions, metrics, and output files." Logged entities: **parameters** (key-value inputs/hyperparameters), **metrics** (key-value numerics with full history), **artifacts** (output files of any format — model weights, images, data), **runs** (each with run ID, start/end times, params, metrics, tags, git commit/source), grouped into **experiments**. Backend store (metadata) + artifact store (S3/Azure/local); integrates with the MLflow Model Registry.

**Weights & Biases (W&B) Artifacts & Lineage.** Artifacts are versioned, content-addressed files — datasets, models, evaluation results, pipeline files — with automatic diffing (stores only changes). Lineage (verbatim docs): "W&B tracks the inputs and outputs of runs using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) called lineage graphs," where nodes are runs and artifacts (`name:version`) and edges are inputs/outputs. Stated benefits: reproducibility, version control, auditing ("detailed record of artifacts and transformations to support compliance and governance"), collaboration. SDK methods `logged_by()` / `used_by()` traverse the graph.

**FAIR data principles — Wilkinson et al. (2016), "The FAIR Guiding Principles for scientific data management and stewardship," *Scientific Data* 3:160018, DOI 10.1038/sdata.2016.18.** Emphasizes **machine-actionability**. The four principles: **Findable** (globally unique persistent identifiers; rich metadata; indexed in a searchable resource); **Accessible** (retrievable by identifier via a standardized, open, free protocol; metadata remain accessible even when data are gone); **Interoperable** (formal shared knowledge-representation language; FAIR vocabularies; qualified references to other data); **Reusable** (richly described with accurate attributes; clear data-usage license; detailed provenance; meets domain community standards). Guidelines, not a standard.

### 3C.3 — Documentation/handoff standards (precise prescribed fields)

**Datasheets for Datasets — Gebru, Morgenstern, Vecchione, Vaughan, Wallach, Daumé III & Crawford (2018 arXiv:1803.09010; CACM 64(12):86–92, Dec 2021, DOI 10.1145/3458723).** Modeled on electronics datasheets. Per the CACM paper (verbatim): "Datasheets for Datasets are comprised of 57 questions across 7 categories: 1.) Motivation, 2.) Composition, 3.) Collection Process, 4.) Preprocessing, Cleaning, & Labeling, 5.) Uses, 6.) Distribution, and 7.) Maintenance." The seven categories track the dataset lifecycle.

**Model Cards for Model Reporting — Mitchell, Wu, Zaldivar, Barnes, Vasserman, Hutchinson, Spitzer, Raji & Gebru (2019), FAT* 2019, pp. 220–229, DOI 10.1145/3287560.3287596.** ~30 disclosures across **nine sections**: **(1) Model Details, (2) Intended Use, (3) Factors, (4) Metrics, (5) Evaluation Data, (6) Training Data, (7) Quantitative Analyses (disaggregated by factors, with confidence intervals), (8) Ethical Considerations, (9) Caveats and Recommendations.** Recommends reporting disaggregated, intersectional performance across the chosen factors.

**Data Statements for NLP — Bender & Friedman (2018), "Data Statements for Natural Language Processing," *TACL* 6:587–604, DOI 10.1162/tacl_a_00041.** Definition (verbatim): "a characterization of a dataset that provides context to allow developers and users to better understand how experimental results might generalize." Provided in a **long form** and a **short form**. Schema elements include: **Curation Rationale; Language Variety (with a BCP-47 tag); Speaker Demographic (age, gender, race/ethnicity, native language, socioeconomic status, number of speakers, disordered speech); Annotator Demographic; Speech Situation; Text Characteristics; Recording Quality; Other; Provenance Appendix.** (Flag: the exact label of the eighth element was not surfaced verbatim; the nine-element long-form count is confirmed by secondary scholarship and later UW Tech Policy Lab schema versions.)

**The Dataset Nutrition Label — Holland, Hosny, Newman, Joseph & Chmielinski (2018), "The Dataset Nutrition Label: A Framework To Drive Higher Data Quality Standards," arXiv:1805.03677 (Data Nutrition Project).** A **modular** diagnostic label, analogous to a food nutrition label, giving an overview of dataset "ingredients" before modeling. Verbatim: "the Label is comprised of diverse qualitative and quantitative modules generated through multiple statistical and probabilistic modelling backends, but displayed in a standardized format." Seven prototype modules: **(1) Metadata, (2) Provenance, (3) Variables, (4) Statistics, (5) Pair Plot, (6) Probabilistic Model (BayesDB backend), (7) Ground Truth Correlations.** Later iteration: Chmielinski et al. (2022), "The Dataset Nutrition Label (2nd Gen)," arXiv:2201.03954.

## Cross-cutting verification flags
- **Recht et al. 2019** explicitly found the ImageNet/CIFAR accuracy drops were *not* adaptive overfitting but distribution/hardness ("approximately five years of progress") — a nuance often misread as confirming test-set overfitting.
- **Chinchilla "20 tokens/parameter"** is the community interpretation of the equal-scaling rule, not a verbatim headline number; the equal-scaling/doubling rule and the Chinchilla-70B-1.4T / Gopher-280B-300B figures ARE from the paper (arXiv:2203.15556).
- **Distillation + memorization conflict (3B.2):** DMP (Shejwalkar & Houmansadr 2019) uses KD to *reduce* membership leakage, while Zhang et al. (2025) find all six studied LLM KD methods *transfer* membership/memorization risk — the directionality is condition-dependent and contested.
- **Stanton et al. 2021 fidelity-vs-generalization paradox** is itself debated by later 2024 work attributing the gap to teacher attention diversity rather than purely optimization difficulty.
- **Bucilă/Caruana 2006** "model compression" is the acknowledged precursor that Hinton et al. 2015 generalize.
- **Data Statements eighth schema element** ("Other") label not verified verbatim; **DVC lakeFS acquisition** is a secondary, fast-moving product detail (current as of mid-2025). All other author lists, venues, years, DOIs, and verbatim quotes were verified against primary sources (arXiv, ACL Anthology, JMLR, MIT Press/Neural Computation, *Science*, NeurIPS/ICML proceedings, Nature *Scientific Data*, ACM DL).
