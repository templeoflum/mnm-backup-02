The primes and the zeros through one lens.

These two are bound to each other by the explicit formula — each fully encodes the other. We've measured the zeros' texture six ways and never once measured the primes'. So here they are in the same instrument, same units. The result isn't a rhyme: they sit at opposite ends of the texture dial. The frequencies are maximally ordered; the events are maximally disordered.

what the comparison shows

The zeros forbid close neighbors (a hole at zero separation), their fluctuations are frozen — stiff, repelling, blue, near the crystal end. The primes do the opposite: they pile up at small gaps and their fluctuations climb the diagonal — clumpy, Poisson, white, at the random end. Two objects that fully determine one another, wearing opposite textures.

That's the thing worth seeing. You might expect the dual of an ordered object to be ordered; it isn't. The explicit formula relates them, but it does not make their textures match — it inverts them. The maximally-even spectrum of frequencies belongs to a maximally-uneven scatter of events.

Honest scope. The opposite-texture contrast is measured here (≈150k primes near 10⁶, ≈400 zeros), and it's accurate. Two caveats kept in view: the primes' Poisson character is the Gallagher / Hardy–Littlewood heuristic, and the very smallest-gap pile-up is real arithmetic structure (twin and cousin primes), not smooth randomness — so "white" is the texture at the scale we're reading, with genuine fine structure beneath it. And the texture-inversion is an observation about two linked objects, not a claimed theorem. As always: comparing and seeing, not concluding.
