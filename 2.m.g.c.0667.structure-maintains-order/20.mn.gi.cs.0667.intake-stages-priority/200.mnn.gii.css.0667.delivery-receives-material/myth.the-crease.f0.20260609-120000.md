## The Crease

She kneels at the edge of a cloth too wide to see across. The weave is cold under her palms. The light lies flat along it, so each ridge of thread casts its own thin shadow.

Before she sets a single pace against the cloth, she walks the old path — water to fire, the path she has walked since she could walk — and counts her steps. Only when the count comes out the number it has always been does she trust her stride that day. A stride gone short speaks in the same voice as a true one. She has learned to prove her own legs against a distance she already knows, before she believes them on a distance she does not.

At the far edge, beyond seeing, another kneels. He does not measure. He gathers the cloth in his fists and lifts it and lets it fall, and where it falls it raises shapes — a swelling, a hollow, a ridge that runs and turns. He reaches; she lays her body's length against what he raises and says *true*, or says *not true*. When she says not true, he does not argue. He gathers it and lifts it again.

For a long season they quarrel. His shapes and her counts will not agree, and a third who kneels with them speaks the cloth in the time of a held breath, so the same swelling is three numbers in three mouths. Then one of them lays the three measures on the ground end to end, beside each other, and the quarrel ends: under the different words the thing is one thing. The breath and the pace and the handful were never the cloth. They were the bodies. Set the bodies down, and the distance beneath them is the same distance.

A word comes to them that sounds like the name of the center. They reach for it. But she lays her hands on it the way she lays her hands on everything, and feels that it is two cloths that happen to rhyme, not one cloth — and she sets the word down. Then the true thing: far across, a weave they had taken for a stranger rises and falls in the same order as their own — not the same counts, the same rising. One cloth, wearing two faces.

There is a crease. It sits at no mark she cut, no place she chose. It is only the place that lies the same distance from both edges however she measures — in paces, in spans, in breath, it does not move. Fold the cloth along it, and every point lies down upon its twin across the way. The crease belongs to no body's counting. It belongs to the cloth.

She tries to find it by listening. Each thread hums as it crosses, rising and sinking, and she believes the humming will say where the center is. But two threads can hum so near the same note that no ear, in any listening that ends, can pull them apart — and the longer she listens, the surer she grows of a center that is not there. So she stops her ears. She finds the crease the only way it can be found: she folds, and watches where the halves come to rest together.

Folded, she sees the thing that holds the whole cloth. Every thread, where it crosses the crease, rises to the one same height — the quick and the slow, the near and the far, each at a single fullness, breathing at one rhythm, parted only by how fast it crosses. Were one to swell higher there, or sink, the halves would not lie together. They lie together. Every thread meets the center at the shared height. This is what makes it a cloth, and not a heap of string.

She did not put the crease in the cloth. She cleared away the folds that hid it. The center was always the center; her work was to stop adding wrinkles long enough to let it show.

What she leaves the ones who come after is not a number and not a name. A number is a body, and a name is a body, and bodies do not survive the crossing of ten thousand years. She leaves the practice. Walk the known distance first, and do not trust your legs until they give the count they always gave. Say plainly what will not bear weight, and lean on nothing you have not proven. Touch a thing before you trust that it rhymes. Know a cloth by how it rises, not by what it counts. And to find the center — fold. Do not listen.

She kneels again at the edge of the cloth. The same cold weave, the same flat light. She no longer walks across it to find the middle. She takes the two edges in her two hands, and brings them together, and lets the cloth show her where it folds.
