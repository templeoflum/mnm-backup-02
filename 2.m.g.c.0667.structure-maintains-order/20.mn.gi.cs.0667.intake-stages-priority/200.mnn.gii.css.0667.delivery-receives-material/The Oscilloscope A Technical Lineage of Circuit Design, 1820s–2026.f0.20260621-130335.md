# The Oscilloscope: A Technical Lineage of Circuit Design, 1820s–2026

## TL;DR
- The oscilloscope evolved through five great paradigms — mechanical oscillograph → cold-cathode Braun tube → recurrent/synchronized-sweep electronic scope → **triggered-sweep** (the canonical conventional form, Tektronix 1946–47) → digital storage (DSO) — with each leap forced by a specific bottleneck: mechanical inertia, cathode life, sweep instability, bandwidth, and finally sample rate/memory.
- The single most important conceptual rupture was **triggered sweep** (Tektronix 511, June 1947), which converted the qualitative "oscillograph" into a calibrated measuring instrument; the canonical trigger–gate–disconnect–sweep–holdoff topology (Ropiequet 1953, Kobbe 1954) was so superior that "everybody copied it."
- The field repeatedly **diverged and re-converged** along philosophical fault lines — "build the best possible instrument" (early Tektronix) vs. "design for a defined market" (HP); recurrent vs. triggered sweep; real-time vs. equivalent-time sampling; analog phosphor persistence vs. digital memory — converging today into a Tektronix/Keysight/Teledyne-LeCroy/Rohde & Schwarz oligopoly built on flash ADCs, deep memory, and the Digital Phosphor display that digitally re-creates the "analog feel."

## Key Findings

1. **The pre-CRT mechanical era was a full paradigm (~1820s–1940s), not a prelude.** It established the conceptual vocabulary — point-by-point sampling, equivalent-time averaging, low-inertia transducers, photographic time-axis recording — that the electronic scope later implemented electronically. Blondel's oscillographs and Duddell's moving-coil instrument "remained the best way to record high-speed electrical phenomena for more than 40 years."
2. **The Braun tube (1897) was a physics curiosity, not an instrument.** Turning the CRT into a usable measuring device required four decades of incremental fixes: beam-forming, hot cathodes, internal X/Y plates, high vacuum, electrostatic focus, and intensity modulation.
3. **WWII radar was the forcing function** that pulled fast triggered-pulse and driven-sweep circuitry into existence; Howard Vollum carried that radar knowledge directly into the Tektronix 511.
4. **The triggered sweep + modular plug-in mainframe (Tektronix 530/540, 1954) defined the conventional scope** for thirty years.
5. **HP's divergence into equivalent-time sampling** (185A, 1960) using step-recovery diodes pushed bandwidth into the GHz range that brute-force real-time amplifiers could not reach.
6. **The "first DSO" is genuinely contested and definition-dependent** — LeCroy WD2000 (1971, first real-time digitizer-in-a-box), Nicolet 1090 (1973, first complete drop-in DSO), LeCroy 9400 (~1985, first high-speed DSO).
7. **Modern convergence** reunited the analog "feel" with digital flexibility via the Digital Phosphor Oscilloscope (Tektronix, late 1990s) and dissolved the instrument into software via PC/USB scopes (Pico Technology, 1991).

## Details

### 1. The Pre-CRT Mechanical Oscillograph Era (~1820s–1940s)

The history of waveform display "reaches back to the first recordings of waveforms with a galvanometer coupled to a mechanical drawing system in the second decade of the 19th century." The intellectual lineage begins with Oersted's 1820 discovery that current deflects a magnetic needle — the basis of the galvanometer (named for Galvani) — and the central engineering problem of the entire era was **inertia**: a mechanical pointer or coil cannot follow a rapidly alternating signal.

**Point-by-point / contact methods.** The earliest waveform "imaging" was a laborious manual process of measuring the voltage of a spinning rotor at many points around its axis with a galvanometer, then hand-plotting. This was partially automated by **Jules François Joubert**, whose step-by-step method used a single-contact commutator on the shaft of a spinning rotor; the contact could be advanced around the cycle following a precise degree scale, the galvanometer reading hand-graphed at each position. Because the waveform was built up over several thousand cycles, it produced only a rough approximation — but it was the first systematic waveform sampling.

**Hospitalier's Ondograph (1903) — an ancestor of equivalent-time sampling.** Édouard Hospitalier automated Joubert's idea. A synchronous motor, geared to run one revolution per minute slower than the signal's periodicity, drove a contact that **charged a capacitor from a slightly later point on the wave each cycle**; the capacitor then discharged into a pen-galvanometer drawing on a paper drum. The instrument "automatically charged a capacitor from each 100th wave, and discharged the stored energy through a recording galvanometer, with each successive charge of the capacitor being taken from a point a little farther along the wave." This averaging-over-many-cycles to reconstruct one composite waveform is conceptually the direct ancestor of **sequential equivalent-time sampling** used in microwave sampling scopes 60 years later — though, as period sources note, "most electrical signals varied too quickly for the ondograph's mechanical system."

**The mirror oscillographs — reducing inertia.** The decisive move was to shrink the moving element to a tiny mirror. **André Blondel** (France) coined the term "oscillograph" and, seeking to solve "the problem of integral synchronization" using Cornu's theory, invented two variants ~1891–1893: the **bifilar (moving-coil)** type and the **soft-iron (moving-iron)** type. In the bifilar form a small loop of conductor carrying the signal current sat in a strong magnetic field, with a tiny mirror reflecting a light beam onto photographic film or a screen; a rotating mirror supplied the time axis. By minimizing the mass of the moving system, Blondel pushed frequency response far beyond the pen recorders. The instruments "won the grand prize at the St. Louis Exposition in 1904" and "remained the best way to record high-speed electrical phenomena for more than 40 years."

**William Du Bois Duddell** (Britain), a student of W. E. Ayrton, engineered Blondel's concept into a practical, portable product around 1899: the moving-coil oscillograph, in which the lightweight phosphor-bronze coil/strip was **damped in a viscous oil bath** to suppress resonance and a vibrating mirror threw the trace onto a rotating photographic drum. The first detailed description appeared in *The Electrician* (1897), and "within a year the Cambridge Scientific Instrument Company was producing the first commercial" units, manufacturing many models until the cathode-ray scope displaced them. The shared goal across both men, per Phillips' history, was "to reduce inertia and improve the frequency response."

**The electrophysiology lineage.** Parallel to the power-engineering line ran a biomedical one descending from Du Bois-Reymond, Bernstein, and Burdon-Sanderson. Its apex was **Willem Einthoven's string galvanometer (1901–1903)**: a fine silver-coated quartz filament stretched in a strong magnetic field, whose shadow was photographed on a moving plate. It became the first practical electrocardiograph; the early machine "weighed 600 pounds," needed water cooling, and "required 5 people to operate." Einthoven won the 1924 Nobel Prize and gave us the P-QRS-T nomenclature still used today. (Some sources note the French engineer Clément Ader had built a similar string device in 1897 for telegraphy, but it lacked the sensitivity for clinical work.)

**Flame, optical, and persistent UV methods.** Other branches included the manometric **flame** method (audio modulating a gas flame's height, viewed in a rotating mirror) and purely optical methods (Fröhlich, and Braun's optical work). Moving-paper **UV oscillographs** using advanced mirror galvanometers and UV-sensitive paper persisted as multi-channel recorders into the mid-20th century — and, remarkably, light-beam oscillographs were still in service in the USSR into the late 1970s. The authoritative reference for this entire paradigm is **Vivian J. Phillips, *Waveforms: A History of Early Oscillography***.

### 2. The CRT Birth and Early Electronic Era (1897–1930s)

**Braun's tube (1897).** Karl Ferdinand Braun, at Strasbourg, was "the first person to conceive of the possibility of using the discharge tube as a visual indicator." His tube used a flat **cold cathode**, a diaphragm with a small hole to constrict the divergent beam into a narrow stream, and a Willemite-coated mica target. It required ~50 kV from a hand-cranked electrostatic generator, used a soft (gas) vacuum, and applied only vertical deflection — the time axis came from an external **rotating mirror**. Braun also introduced the "cyclographic" method (two right-angled deflections to produce stationary patterns). Cathode life was a few tens of hours due to ion bombardment of the unheated cathode.

**The incremental fixes.** That same year J.J. Thomson's tube first used internal **deflector plates** and a bulb-coated screen. The refinements then cascaded:
- **Jonathan Zenneck (1899)** replaced the rotating mirror with an *electrical* time base — a motor-driven rotary-potentiometer arrangement producing a linear sawtooth current in the deflecting solenoid — "in the manner of to-day's instruments," and added beam-forming plates.
- **Arthur Wehnelt (1905)** introduced the lime/oxide-coated **hot cathode**, slashing the required accelerating voltage from 50–80 kV to 1–10 kV and increasing deflection sensitivity — the key to portability.
- **D. Roschansky (1911)** mounted **two pairs of internal deflector plates** (horizontal and vertical) with side connections "in the manner familiar to us to-day" — the canonical X/Y arrangement.
- **A. Dufour (1914)** built "the first high speed cathode ray tube," sealing photographic plates *inside* the vacuum so the beam wrote directly on the emulsion, running at 60 kV with deflection plates outside the tube to avoid gas ionization. Designed to capture **single-shot lightning surges** on transmission lines, it was "always used in a single sweep mode," reaching writing speeds over 3000 cm/µs by 1922. **Dufour thus inaugurated the single-shot-transient vs. repetitive-waveform tension** that recurs through the entire history.

**Toward a usable tube.** Early CRTs "suffered from poor stability of the vacuum and the cathode emitters." In 1922 **J.B. Johnson** at Western Electric (building on Van der Bijl's gas-focusing work) designed the first practical low-voltage, long-life **gas-focused** tube — the Western Electric Type 224 — operating at ~300 V with a helical filament shielded from ion bombardment, extending life to 200–300 hours and selling as a complete sealed unit. This made the first true packaged oscilloscope possible: the **H.C. Burt Co. instrument (Bedell & Reich, 1927)**, which was "the first time that this designation ['Oscilloscope'] had been used." (The word "oscilloscope" itself was coined in 1907.) The decisive CRT improvement came when **Manfred von Ardenne (~1930)** and others introduced the **high-vacuum, electrostatically focused** tube using the Wehnelt cylinder as a focusing grid, which permitted intensity modulation (essential for television) without defocusing — driving the abandonment of gas-focused tubes by ~1934–35. **V.K. Zworykin** described a permanently sealed high-vacuum thermionic CRT in 1931, enabling General Radio to build a scope usable outside the lab.

**Time-base generation.** The synchronization problem dominated. After Zenneck's mechanical sweep, **Rogowski (1920)** used opposed saturated diodes as a constant-current source to charge/discharge a capacitor (triangular wave); **Kipping (1924)** used a neon glow-discharge lamp's strike/extinction hysteresis to make a large sawtooth; and gas-filled **thyratron** relaxation oscillators became the standard sweep generator. All were **free-running and synchronized** (not triggered) — the sweep was nudged into lock by injecting a sample of the signal, requiring "quite frequent adjustments" and making single-shot capture impossible.

### 3. Interwar Commercialization (1930s–1945)

**The first commercial scope makers.** In the US, **Allen B. DuMont** founded DuMont Laboratories in 1931, introduced his first oscilloscope in 1932, and by the model 137 (1934) had "clearly what we would today understand as an oscilloscope" with an engraved graticule and carrying handle; the 1940 Type 208 reached DC-coupled vertical response, regulated supplies, and symmetric deflection. DuMont became the prewar/early-postwar US market leader. **RCA** entered in 1935 targeting service technicians; **General Radio** built lab instruments around Zworykin's stable tube. In Britain, **A.C. Cossor** (which had made its first tube in 1903) became market leader, and its split-beam dual-trace tube (model 3339, 1939) became the general-purpose instrument used throughout WWII in Britain — "extremely useful for examining the performance of pulse circuits" in radar work, though uncalibrated. In Europe, **Philips** (GM3150, 1936) was dominant.

**WWII radar as forcing function.** Radar demanded fast, calibrated, *driven* sweeps started by a transmit pulse — the "A-scan" for range measurement — and pulled millimicrosecond pulse circuitry into existence. A handful of wartime scopes had **driven sweeps**: dormant until an external pulse unblanked the CRT and launched a constant-speed (calibrated) trace, then reset. The commercially available **DuMont 248 (1945)** had this feature and a limited delayed sweep.

**Sweep/timebase circuit evolution.** The British contribution was foundational. **O.S. Puckle** developed a hard-valve timebase that raised repetition rate from ~40 kHz (gas tubes) to over 1 MHz and stabilized amplitude. **Alan Blumlein** at EMI, designing test gear for the BBC 405-line television system, invented the **long-tailed pair** (differential amplifier, for common-mode rejection of studio interference) and the **Miller integrator sweep** — which, by exploiting Miller capacitance and high negative feedback to hold the charging current constant, produced a highly linear sweep with low output impedance. W.S. Percival (EMI) patented the **distributed amplifier**. "All of these circuits have since become standard oscilloscope building blocks."

### 4. The Triggered-Sweep Paradigm — The Core Conventional Form (1946–1960)

**The conceptual leap.** Essentially all 1945 scopes used a free-running synchronizable timebase, suited only to simple repetitive waveforms and needing "more or less constant adjustment." Triggering, by contrast, "is far less sensitive to variations in frequency and amplitude, it allows satisfactory observation of complex waveforms," gives a choice of trigger polarity/slope, maintains calibrated sweep speed, and removes any requirement that the signal be periodic. "The change in triggering was perhaps the single most important advance during these years."

**Tektronix and the 511.** Tektronix was founded December 1945 (as "Tekrad," renamed 1946) by **Howard Vollum, Jack Murdock, Miles Tippery, and Glenn McDowell**, each contributing $2,600. Vollum — a Reed College physics graduate who had worked on radar in England during the war — was chief engineer. The **Type 511** was introduced in June 1947 as Tektronix's first oscilloscope (its second product overall, after the commercially unsuccessful Type 101 Video Calibrator); it was designed by **Frank Hood, Logan Belleville, and Howard Vollum**, and the first production unit (serial number 101) shipped in July 1947 to Dr. A. R. Tunturi at the University of Oregon Medical School. It was the first scope with **triggered sweep** and a calibrated time base, selling for $795. (Sources conflict on the price comparison with DuMont: vintagetek.org states the 511 cost $795 "compared to $1,800 for a two-piece Dumont scope that was mostly pre-war technology," whereas Wikipedia recounts that Allen DuMont, after trying the 511 at a show, told Vollum the $795 price "was about twice as much as his equivalent model" and that Tek would struggle to sell many — i.e., framing the DuMont as the *cheaper* unit. The discrepancy likely reflects different DuMont models being compared; both agree the 511 was the technically superior, triggered instrument.) The 511's 10 MHz vertical was still AC-coupled, and it had real limitations: it "did not effectively work with frequencies below 60 Hz," lacked sufficient vertical gain, lacked voltage regulation (deflection sensitivity varied with the mains), and crucially **could not show the trigger portion of the waveform** because it had no signal delay line.

**Calibrated timebase and delay.** Improvements came fast: the **511A** (1948) added a regulated CRT supply and calibrator; an optional **L-C delay line** (24 sections) became the 511AD. The follow-on **513** added a built-in calibrated time base, and by ~1950 the **513D** had a DC-coupled 20 MHz amplifier, a delay line, and a 12 kV CRT.

**The canonical sweep topology — "everybody copied it."** The conventional triggered-sweep architecture crystallized as a chain: **trigger comparator (long-tailed pair) → pulse generator (Schmitt trigger) → sweep gate → disconnect diode → Miller sweep generator → sweep-length limiter → hold-off**. The sweep-length limiter cuts the sweep into flyback at a precisely adjustable level; the **hold-off** circuit locks out the next trigger until the sweep generator has fully reset, preventing premature re-triggering and "much less fussy operation." This "wide-band sweep circuit" is attributed to **Dick Ropiequet** (first used in the Type 315, 1953) and was refined by **John Kobbe** for the 530 series (1954). As one restorer put it, the 511A's primitive timebase "really confirms just what a breakthrough the trigger – gate – disconnect diode – sweep generator – length limiter – hold-off topology really was, everybody copied it!"

**The modular mainframe + plug-in architecture.** The **530/540 series (1954)** introduced the interchangeable **vertical plug-in**: fewer mainframes, with the customer choosing the front-end (highest-performance single channel, dual-trace, differential, or high-gain) at purchase or later. The series also brought the **dual time base / delayed sweep** covering a full range of delay, giving up to ~1000× improvement in time resolution. The **545 (1955, 30 MHz)** became the canonical high-performance lab scope, and dual-trace (time-multiplexed chopping/alternation) overtook dual-beam.

**Vertical amplifier bandwidth.** Pushing bandwidth required the **distributed (traveling-wave) amplifier**: multiple tubes whose input (grid) and output (plate) capacitances are absorbed into tapped L-C delay lines, so the tubes' parasitic capacitance becomes part of the transmission line rather than rolling off the response. The 545 used a 6-stage push-pull distributed output amplifier driving the CRT through a T-coil delay line; **T-coils** (m-derived filter sections) gave roughly 3× bandwidth improvement over no peaking. The 85 MHz **581/585 (1959–60)** added the first **distributed-deflection-plate CRT** in a general-purpose scope, dividing the deflection plates into segments connected by delay lines matched to the electron transit velocity — defeating the transit-time bandwidth ceiling.

### 5. The HP Challenge and Technical Divergence (1956–1970s)

**The philosophical fault line.** By 1955 HP's catalog "listed nearly 250 measuring instruments of every type except one — the oscilloscope." Bill Hewlett and Dave Packard's philosophy "was not to build a 'me-too' product," but pressure from independent reps (whose Tektronix lines were being pulled as Tek went direct-sales) pushed them in. The first two products (1956) embodied two strategies: the **130A** (DC–300 kHz) was a high-sensitivity low-frequency scope "that filled a niche that Tektronix didn't occupy"; the **150A** (DC–10 MHz, dual-channel plug-in) was a deliberate head-on assault that "offered no improvement in speed or cost over the initial Tektronix 511 family introduced nearly a decade earlier." The 150A flopped — "existing Tektronix users found no reason to switch." This crystallized the enduring divergence: **Tektronix's "build the best possible instrument" engineering culture and direct-sales model vs. HP's "design for a defined market" and independent-rep model.**

**HP's pivot to sampling.** Unable to beat Tek at conventional real-time scopes, HP leapfrogged into **equivalent-time sampling** to reach frequencies real-time amplifiers could not. The principle: it is possible to generate a much shorter *pulse* than the risetime achievable in a linear amplifier; that pulse drives a **sampling gate** to grab one thin slice of the waveform, taking a different slice on each repetition and reconstructing the composite — exactly Hospitalier's ondograph logic in nanosecond electronics. The enabling components were the **step-recovery (snap-off) diode** and **hot-carrier (Schottky) diode** from HP Associates: the SRD stores charge then "snaps off" abruptly, generating picosecond pulses. The **HP 185A (1960)**, designed by Roderick Carlson and others, delivered ~500 MHz equivalent bandwidth with dual-trace and high-impedance probes; SRDs soon pushed the 185-series to 1 GHz and beyond. Sampling came in two flavors: **sequential** (each sample a fixed increment later than the last, needing a signal delay line to see the trigger) and **random/equivalent-time** sampling (samples taken asynchronously and re-sorted using a measured time-to-trigger, eliminating the delay line and allowing pre-trigger view).

**Tektronix in parallel.** Tek responded within roughly six months at WESCON with the **Type N sampling plug-in** for the 530 series, and developed its own sampling line, introducing the first **random-sampling plug-in (3T2) in 1967**. HP's John Minck recounts that HP felt Tek "never did catch up" on sampling technology — but more broadly Tek matched "virtually every move HP made in scopes," and customers "were willing to wait for Tek to come up with an answer." Sampling scopes, despite unmatched bandwidth+sensitivity, never exceeded ~5% of the market because they were expensive, idiosyncratic, and only worked on repetitive signals.

### 6. Analog Branchings (1960s–1970s)

**Storage CRTs** addressed the single-shot/low-repetition problem without photography. Two types dominated: the **mesh storage tube** (a charge pattern written on a coated mesh behind the phosphor, made visible by a flood gun; first described by A.V. Haeff 1947, improved by Smith & Brown 1953), which supported **variable persistence**; and the **bistable phosphor storage tube** (R.H. Anderson, 1960–62), lower-cost and allowing split storage/non-storage screen areas. Hughes' "Memo Scope" (1956) used a Memotron mesh tube; **Tektronix introduced the 564 bistable Direct-View Bistable Storage Tube (DVBST) scope in 1963**, and HP followed with the variable-persistence 141A (1966). Writing speed — the key spec — improved ~25,000× between 1964 and 1976, culminating in **transfer storage tubes** (Hutchins & Templeton, ~1973, two targets: a fast mesh for capture, a bistable target for long viewing, as in the Tek 466/7834). The ultimate analog single-shot instruments used **micro-channel-plate** electron multipliers: the **Tek 7104** — a 1 GHz mainframe (introduced 1978–79; sources differ), the highest-bandwidth analog oscilloscope Tektronix ever built, with "trace brightness about one thousand times that of conventional oscilloscopes" — could photograph a single-shot event at 200 ps/division.

**Solid-state portables.** Germanium then silicon transistors enabled portability. The **Tektronix 321 (1960/61)** was the first practical fully portable, battery-capable scope (5 MHz, 10 lb). The computer-service market drove the rugged **422 (1964)** and especially the **453 (1965, 50 MHz dual-trace delaying sweep)** — a hugely influential design. By 1967 silicon had displaced tubes in new designs; the **7000-series (1969)** carried plug-ins in both axes, custom Tektronix ICs, and could become a sampling scope by swapping plug-ins. Triggering speed kept pace via **tunnel diodes** (from 1962) then high-speed silicon ICs; HP's 1969 **DC-to-VHF scope reached 250 MHz** using custom small-scale ICs per stage.

### 7. The Digital Rupture — ADC and the DSO (late 1970s–1980s)

The DSO replaced phosphor persistence with **digital memory**: input → attenuator/amplifier → **sample-and-hold → analog-to-digital converter → acquisition memory (circular buffer) → microprocessor → display**. Its decisive advantages over analog storage were freedom from phosphor wear, infinite retention, post-acquisition math/zoom, and — uniquely — **pre-trigger capture**, since the circular buffer is always recording and the trigger merely marks where to stop. Sampling theory now governed design: the **Nyquist criterion** (sample > 2× highest frequency to avoid **aliasing**), and **sin(x)/x interpolation** to reconstruct the waveform between samples. Digital scopes use **real-time sampling** (one-shot capable) or **equivalent-time** (repetitive only).

**The contested "first DSO."** The attribution is genuinely disputed and depends entirely on the definition used:
- **LeCroy WD2000 Waveform Digitizer (1971)** — the earliest by date, "recognized as the world's first real-time digital storage oscilloscope." Walter LeCroy adapted high-energy-physics digitizer expertise from **CERN**: it packed a real-time ADC, memory, and a 3-inch CRT in one box, but used ~20 slow-but-accurate "Wilkinson rundown" ADCs in parallel and had only a ~20-sample memory depth — so many discount it as "not a full integrated oscilloscope."
- **Nicolet Instrument Corp. Model 1090 (1973)** — the strongest claim for first *complete* DSO designed as a "direct replacement for analog oscilloscopes." Per Nicolet's own 1973 data sheet: **12-bit, 1 MSPS ADC, 4K record length, two simultaneously sampling channels, mid-signal (pre-trigger) triggering.** Wikipedia: "The first digital storage oscilloscope (DSO) was built by Nicolet Test Instrument of Madison, Wisconsin... low-speed analog-to-digital converter (1 MHz, 12 bit) used primarily for vibration and medical analysis."
- **LeCroy 9400/9400A (~1985)** — the **first high-speed DSO**, and the most-cited industry milestone. Per LeCroy's own 9400A Operator Manual: "The LeCroy 9400A provides 175 MHz bandwidth, 100 megasamples/sec 8-bit ADCs, ±2% DC accuracy (±1% optional), 32K memory per channel, and up to 192K of waveform storage memory" (with an interleaved sample rate of 5 GS/s). Wikipedia summarizes it as "the first high-speed DSO (100 MHz, 8 bit)... developed by Walter LeCroy." Many secondary sources (SparkFun, ToolBoom) conflate this 1985 high-speed milestone with the "first DSO," wrongly ignoring the 1971 and 1973 devices.

**Tektronix and HP digital entries.** The **Tektronix R7912 transient digitizer (Sept 1973)** reached 500 MHz (1 GHz direct) but used a **scan-converter tube** (writing/reading beams on a silicon diode target) rather than ADC sampling — a digitizer, not a self-contained DSO. The **Tektronix 468 (1980)** bolted a 25 MS/s 8-bit digital storage section onto the analog 465B (100 MHz). The **HP 1980A/B (1980; featured in the Sept 1982 HP Journal)** is often miscalled "the first fully digital oscilloscope," but it was actually a **microprocessor-controlled analog scope** — only the front panel, control, and storage were digital, the acquisition path analog. HP's first true high-speed digitizing scope was the **HP 54100A (1 GHz, 1985)**, which used **random repetitive (equivalent-time) sampling** reaching a 100 GHz equivalent rate.

**The enabling circuit technology** was the **flash (parallel) ADC** — a bank of comparators digitizing in one clock step — plus fast **sample-and-hold** front ends, high-speed acquisition RAM, and microprocessor integration. Early flash ADCs were limited to ~100 MHz at 6–8 bits, expensive and power-hungry; their steady improvement (and cost decline) is what allowed the DSO to overtake first the slow CRT-storage scopes (by ~1975) and ultimately all analog scopes.

### 8. Modern Convergence and Consolidation (1990s–present)

**Re-converging on the analog "feel."** Early DSOs lost something analog scopes had: the **intensity grading** that revealed how often a given amplitude recurred (and so exposed glitches and modulation). Tektronix's **Digital Phosphor Oscilloscope (DPO)** — commercialized in the late-1990s **TDS3000** and later DPO families — reintroduced this digitally, using a fast hardware rasterizer to capture waveforms at very high rates and display "three dimensions of signal information: amplitude, time, and distribution of amplitude over time" with real-time color/intensity grading. On Tektronix's higher-end DPO7000C series, the proprietary **FastAcq** engine "delivers a fast waveform capture – greater than 250,000 waveforms per second – that enables you to see glitches and other infrequent transients within seconds." This **converged** the analog "feel" with digital flexibility, resolving the long analog-vs-digital "feel" debate.

**MSO and PC-based scopes.** The **Mixed-Signal Oscilloscope (MSO)** merged scope + logic analyzer (analog channels plus many digital channels with bus/protocol decode); HP's 54645D (~1998) is often cited as an early mixed-signal instrument. Meanwhile the instrument **dissolved into software**: **Pico Technology (founded 1991)** pioneered PC-based oscilloscopes, releasing PicoScope software in 1992 and building USB scopes that use the host PC's screen and processor — "the instrument dissolving into software." Pico's sampling-scope line now reaches the highest frequencies: its **PicoScope 9400A SXRTO series (announced July 2025)** offers up to 33 GHz, "achieves rise and fall times of under 12 picoseconds," provides "four channels with 12-bit voltage resolution," and specifies "less than 1.5 picoseconds RMS" jitter.

**The modern oligopoly and corporate lineages.** Consolidation produced a handful of leaders: **Tektronix** (founded 1946; acquired by Danaher 2007, spun into **Fortive** 2016, and now a subsidiary of **Ralliant**, a Fortive spinoff); **Keysight** (the HP test-and-measurement lineage: HP → spun off as **Agilent** in 1999 → test-and-measurement split off as **Keysight** in 2014); **Teledyne LeCroy** (LeCroy, acquired by Teledyne in 2012); and **Rohde & Schwarz**, a German RF house that entered scopes more recently. With Yokogawa, Iwatsu, Siglent, and Rigol filling mid- and low-range tiers, the modern instrument is a convergence of every prior paradigm: a triggered, deep-memory, flash-ADC real-time digitizer with equivalent-time sampling for the highest bandwidths and a digital-phosphor display that re-creates the analog past.

### Cross-cutting analytical threads

**Divergence/convergence in design philosophy.** The deepest fault line was **recurrent vs. triggered sweep** — the latter won totally and defined the conventional scope. A second was **"best possible instrument" (early Tektronix) vs. "design for a defined market" (HP, and later Tektronix itself)**, mirrored in **direct sales (Tek) vs. independent-rep sales (HP)**. A third was **modular mainframe+plug-in (530/540, 7000) vs. integrated single instrument (portables, and most modern scopes)** — the plug-in era flourished 1954–~1980 then receded. A fourth was **analog phosphor persistence vs. digital memory** — which diverged sharply in the 1980s and then *re-converged* in the DPO. A fifth was **real-time vs. equivalent-time sampling**, which coexist to this day (real-time for single-shot, equivalent-time for the highest repetitive bandwidths).

**The single-shot vs. repetitive tension** runs unbroken from **Dufour (1914)** — who needed single lightning surges and therefore built a high-voltage single-sweep tube — through storage CRTs, through the sampling scope's restriction to repetitive signals, to the modern DSO's circular-buffer single-shot capture. **Each bottleneck drove the next innovation:** mechanical inertia → low-mass mirror galvanometers; cathode life → hot/oxide cathodes and high vacuum; sweep instability → triggered sweep + hold-off; amplifier bandwidth → distributed amplifiers and distributed-deflection CRTs; amplifier risetime ceiling → equivalent-time sampling; phosphor's inability to store → storage CRTs → digital memory; and ADC speed → flash converters and modern multi-GS/s real-time acquisition.

## Recommendations

For a reader using this report to brief, write, or build a course:

1. **Anchor the narrative on triggered sweep as the pivot.** Everything before 1946 is the story of *making a stable, calibrated display possible*; everything after is *extending bandwidth, storage, and analysis*. State the June 1947 Tektronix 511 as the hinge.
2. **Treat the "first DSO" question explicitly as contested.** Do not assert a single winner. Present the three-way split (WD2000 1971 / Nicolet 1090 1973 / LeCroy 9400 1985) and note that the answer depends on whether "DSO" means "first real-time digitizer-in-a-box," "first complete drop-in replacement instrument," or "first high-speed DSO." Flag that many popular sources wrongly collapse this to "LeCroy 1985."
3. **Use the ondograph→sampling-scope link as the report's intellectual spine.** The most elegant through-line is that equivalent-time sampling (HP 185A, 1960) is conceptually Hospitalier's 1903 capacitor-charging averaging method re-implemented with step-recovery diodes. Lead with this if the audience is technical.
4. **For sourcing depth, prioritize:** Kreps & Dalton's *A History of the Analog Cathode Ray Oscilloscope* (vintagetek.org) for circuit detail; Phillips' *Waveforms* for the mechanical era; the HP Memory Project / John Minck narrative for the HP–Tek rivalry; primary HP Journal and Tektronix manuals (and TekWiki) for specs; Wikipedia's "History of the oscilloscope" as a structural spine. Treat Grokipedia and reseller blogs as leads to verify, not as authorities (they contain identifiable errors, e.g., misattributing the Nicolet 1090 to LeCroy).

**Benchmarks that would change these recommendations:** If a primary Nicolet data sheet earlier than 1973, or a documented complete-instrument DSO predating the WD2000, surfaced, the "first DSO" framing would shift. If primary EMI/Blumlein or Ropiequet patents were consulted directly, some attribution dates (filing vs. issue) could move by 1–3 years.

## Caveats

- **Dates often refer to different events** (invention vs. patent filing vs. patent issue vs. commercial introduction vs. journal coverage). Notable examples: the HP 1980A is dated 1980 (manual/HP Memory) but 1982 (HP Journal issue); the Tek 7912 is Sept 1973 but sometimes cited as 1974; the Tek 7104 is variously dated 1978 (TekWiki/Wikipedia) and 1979 (vintagetek.org).
- **Blondel's oscillograph date is given variously as 1891 or 1893** across sources (an 1898 paper references an "original invention of the oscillograph in 1891"); both reflect a multi-year development.
- **The DuMont/511 price comparison conflicts between sources** — vintagetek.org frames the DuMont as the costlier (~$1,800) two-piece unit, while Wikipedia/TekWiki frame DuMont's equivalent as roughly half the 511's $795. This likely reflects comparison of different DuMont models; the qualitative point (the 511 was the superior, triggered instrument) is undisputed.
- **The HP 1980A was not a true DSO** despite frequent claims; its acquisition path was analog. Secondary sources overstate this.
- **Some "first" claims are marketing-inflected.** "First practical portable," "first mass-produced storage scope," etc., carry implicit qualifiers; the Hughes 104D/Memo Scope predates the Tek 564 in storage but was made in tiny numbers.
- **Source-quality flag:** Grokipedia, ToolBoom, SparkFun, and equipment-reseller pages contain internal contradictions and at least one outright misattribution (Nicolet 1090 → LeCroy). Where this report cites specifics, they are corroborated by primary data sheets, manufacturer manuals, TekWiki, the HP Memory Project, or Kreps & Dalton; remaining single-sourced popular claims are flagged in context.
- **Bandwidth/spec figures for the earliest digital devices** (e.g., WD2000 sample rate/bit depth) conflict between sources; the architectural descriptions are more reliable than the headline numbers.
