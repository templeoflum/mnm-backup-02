# DIVE 1 — Mind, Memory, and the External Hold: A Source Compilation

*Compilation note: This is a reference compilation reporting what sources say, organized strictly by target and question. It does not synthesize across targets or draw conclusions. Conflicts and unverifiable items are flagged explicitly.*

## TL;DR
- TARGET 1A (Extended Mind): Clark & Chalmers' 1998 *Analysis* paper, the parity principle, the Otto/Inga thought experiment, the "glue and trust" criteria, and the three-waves literature (Sutton, Menary, Kirchhoff/Kiverstein) are all richly and consistently sourced.
- TARGET 1B (Active inference / niche construction): Constant, Clark, Kirchhoff & Friston (2020/2022, *Mind & Language*), Sterelny's scaffolded mind, the Tooby & DeVore "cognitive niche," and Friston's free-energy generative-model claims are well sourced.
- TARGET 1C (Reconstructive memory + AI memory gap): Bartlett (1932), Schacter (seven sins), Nader (2000 *Nature* reconsolidation), and Donald/Sutton exograms are well sourced; the specific tri-disciplinary "convergence" claim about AI lacking self-modifying memory is flagged as a synthesis, not a single-source claim.

## Key Findings

### TARGET 1A — The Extended Mind and its waves

**1A.1 — Original thesis, parity principle, Otto/Inga.** The foundational paper is Andy Clark & David J. Chalmers, "The Extended Mind," *Analysis* 58(1):7–19 (1998). The opening question is "Where does the mind stop and the rest of the world begin?" The authors call their position "active externalism, based on the active role of the environment in driving cognitive processes." The **parity principle** (p. 8) states: "If, as we confront some task, a part of the world functions as a process which, were it done in the head, we would have no hesitation in recognizing as part of the cognitive process, then that part of the world is (so we claim) part of the cognitive process." The **Otto/Inga** thought experiment: Inga has normal biological memory and recalls that MoMA is on 53rd Street; Otto has mild Alzheimer's and records information in a notebook he always carries, retrieving the address from it. Clark & Chalmers claim both have a dispositional belief with the same content but different vehicles (biological memory vs. notebook). The **"glue and trust" criteria** (so dubbed by Clark 2010) for the notebook to count as belief-realizing: (1) the notebook is a constant in Otto's life; (2) information is directly available without difficulty; (3) upon retrieval he automatically endorses it; (4) the information was consciously endorsed at some past point and is there as a consequence. Clark & Chalmers (1998, p.17) note the fourth criterion's status "is arguable" and it was subsequently de-emphasized. The view is also grounded in functionalism (mental states individuated by causal/functional role).

**1A.2 — Three waves and active externalism.** John Sutton, "Exograms and Interdisciplinarity: History, the Extended Mind, and the Civilizing Process," in Richard Menary (ed.), *The Extended Mind* (MIT Press, 2010, pp. 189–225; DOI 10.7551/mitpress/9780262014038.003.0009), describes "two compatible but distinct movements or 'waves'" and gestures toward a third. **First wave** = parity-based: external resources count as cognitive if functionally equivalent to internal processes (Clark & Chalmers 1998). **Second wave** = Sutton's **complementarity principle**: external vehicles can be "radically unlike" internal ones; exograms (external memories) have different properties and roles from engrams (internal memories), yet make *complementary* contributions (what Menary calls "integration"). **Third wave** (Michael Kirchhoff, "Extended cognition and fixed properties: steps to a third-wave version of extended cognition," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 11:287–308, 2012; and Kirchhoff & Kiverstein) challenges the idea that internal vs. external properties are fixed; it emphasizes diachronic co-constitution, decentralized agency, and enculturation transforming the brain's representational capacities. "Active externalism" = the thesis that environmental features play an active, ongoing (not passive/distal) role in driving cognition, distinguishing it from Putnam/Burge semantic externalism. Other named third-wave figures: Gallagher (socially extended/legal institutions), Menary (cognitive integration, writing systems), Kiverstein, Cash, Malafouris.

**1A.3 — The cognition/consciousness split.** Clark & Chalmers (1998) were careful not to claim consciousness extends; they noted truly mental states like experiences may be brain-determined. Andy Clark, "Spreading the Joy? Why the Machinery of Consciousness is (Probably) Still in the Head," *Mind* 118(472):963–993 (2009), reviews arguments for an "extended conscious mind" and finds them flawed: arguments for extended cognition "do not generalize" to consciousness. Clark develops a suggestion from Chalmers (2008 Foreword to *Supersizing the Mind*) that "consciousness requires direct access to information on an extremely high-bandwidth," whereas our "low-bandwidth conscious connection to the environment seems to have the wrong form." David Chalmers, "Extended Cognition and Extended Consciousness" (in *Andy Clark and His Critics*, 2019), argues the extended-mind thesis is best framed in terms of perception and action, and that no plausible cases of extended consciousness have been found that meet the required criteria, though he argues some conscious experiences are constituted diachronically. The dissenting third-wave position is Michael D. Kirchhoff & Julian Kiverstein, *Extended Consciousness and Predictive Processing: A Third-Wave View* (Routledge, 2019), and "Attuning to the World: The Diachronic Constitution of the Extended Conscious Mind," *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:1966 (2020), which argue phenomenal consciousness can extend across brain, body, and world via predictive processing, with constitution understood diachronically rather than synchronically.

### TARGET 1B — Active inference / predictive processing & cognitive niche construction

**1B.1 — Extended active inference.** Axel Constant, Andy Clark, Michael Kirchhoff & Karl J. Friston, "Extended active inference: Constructing predictive cognition beyond skulls," *Mind & Language* 37(3):373–394 (DOI 10.1111/mila.12330; first online 2020, in volume 2022). Abstract: "Cognitive niche construction is the process whereby organisms create and maintain cause–effect models of their niche as guides for fitness influencing behavior." The paper presents "an active inference formulation that views cognitive niche construction as a cognitive function aimed at optimizing organisms' generative models. We call that process of optimization extended active inference." It introduces "cognitive uploading" (vs. Clark & Chalmers' offloading): uploading refers to creating novel cognitive functions taken on board by the niche itself. Organisms install "epistemic cues" in the environment as ecological legacies that guide future epistemic foraging (external niche inheritance).

**1B.2 — Cognitive niche construction (navigate-build loop).** The "cognitive niche" term originates with John Tooby & Irven DeVore, "The reconstruction of hominid evolution through strategic modeling," in W.G. Kinzey (ed.), *The Evolution of Human Behavior: Primate Models* (SUNY Press, 1987), defining the "core of our zoological distinctiveness" as "conceptually abstracting from a situation a model of what manipulations are necessary to achieve proximate goals that correlate with fitness." Steven Pinker, "The cognitive niche: Coevolution of intelligence, sociality, and language," *PNAS* 107 (Suppl. 2):8993–8999 (2010), develops it. Kim Sterelny offers the "scaffolded mind" alternative: "Minds: extended or scaffolded?" *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 9(4):465–481 (2010), and "Social intelligence, human intelligence and niche construction," *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 362(1480):719–730 (29 April 2007), DOI 10.1098/rstb.2006.2006. Biological niche-construction theory: Odling-Smee, Laland & Feldman, *Niche Construction: The Neglected Process in Evolution* (Princeton, 2003). The active-inference niche-construction loop is also developed in Constant, Ramstead, Veissière, Campbell & Friston, "A variational approach to niche construction," *Journal of the Royal Society Interface* 15(141):20170685 (published 1 April 2018), DOI 10.1098/rsif.2017.0685.

**1B.3 — Generative model / free-energy principle.** Karl Friston, "The free-energy principle: a unified brain theory?" *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 11(2):127–138 (2010). Per the FEP, self-organizing biological systems minimize variational free energy (variously translated as surprise, prediction error, or model–world discrepancy). Adaptive organisms "are said to be embodied generative models of their environments." Friston: the brain "is or entails a statistical model of its world," and brain and world synchronize via interaction — "In perception and learning, part of the agent becomes more like the world; and in action, the world becomes more like the agent." The model is optimized by perception (changing the model to fit the world) and action (changing the world to fit the model). Earlier statement: Friston, Kilner & Harrison, "A free energy principle for the brain," *Journal of Physiology–Paris* 100:70–87 (2006).

### TARGET 1C — Reconstructive memory + the "AI lacks persistent memory" argument

**1C.1 — Reconstructive memory.** Frederic Bartlett, *Remembering: A Study in Experimental and Social Psychology* (Cambridge, 1932), established that memory is reconstruction, not reproduction: through the "War of the Ghosts" serial-reproduction studies, he argued remembering is governed by **schemas** ("effort after meaning"), and that schema is "not merely something that works the organism, but something with which the organism can work" (p.206). Daniel Schacter, "The seven sins of memory: Insights from psychology and cognitive neuroscience," *American Psychologist* 54(3):182–203 (1999), and the book *The Seven Sins of Memory* (2001), classifies memory failures into seven "sins": **transience, absent-mindedness, blocking** (sins of omission/forgetting); **misattribution, suggestibility, bias** (sins of commission/distortion); and **persistence** (intrusive memories). Schacter argues these are "byproducts of otherwise adaptive features of memory," not design flaws. Karim Nader, Glenn Schafe & Joseph LeDoux, "Fear memories require protein synthesis in the amygdala for reconsolidation after retrieval," *Nature* 406:722–726 (2000), showed that consolidated fear memories, when reactivated, "return to a labile state" requiring de novo protein synthesis (blocked by anisomycin in the lateral/basal amygdala) to re-stabilize — the **memory reconsolidation** hypothesis, which contradicted the view that consolidated memories are fixed/indelible. ("Traces" here = engrams; the reconstructive view holds remembering rebuilds from stored fragments rather than replaying a recording.)

**1C.2 — The convergence argument (FLAGGED).** Per dedicated investigation, the individual pieces are well sourced but the *specific tri-disciplinary convergence* framing — that philosophy of mind, neuroscience, AND AI all land on "persistent, self-modifying memory that closes operational loops across time" — is NOT stated verbatim by any single named source; that exact phrasing is a synthesis. Well-sourced individual pieces:
- (a) **Yann LeCun** told the Financial Times (June 2025, ahead of VivaTech Paris) that LLMs "do not understand the physical world, do not have persistent memory, cannot reason in any reasonable definition of the term and cannot plan . . . hierarchically" (reported by VentureBeat, June 12, 2025); his startup AMI Labs (2026) is built around systems that "have persistent memory, the ability to reason and plan."
- (b) **Google Research** — Behrouz, Zhong & Mirrokni, "Titans: Learning to Memorize at Test Time" (arXiv:2501.00663, 2025), introduces a neural long-term memory module that updates its own weights at test time; Behrouz, Razaviyayn, Zhong & Mirrokni, "Nested Learning: The Illusion of Deep Learning Architectures," arXiv:2512.24695 (submitted Dec 31, 2025; also published at NeurIPS 2025), abstract verbatim: "there are fundamental challenges and unanswered questions about how such models can continually learn/memorize, self-improve, and find effective solutions."
- (c) **Mathis Pink, Qinyuan Wu, Vy Ai Vo, Javier Turek, Jianing Mu, Alexander Huth & Mariya Toneva**, "Position: Episodic Memory is the Missing Piece for Long-Term LLM Agents," arXiv:2502.06975 (submitted 10 Feb 2025; Max Planck Institute for Software Systems & UT Austin) — the closest title-level match — abstract verbatim: LLM agents "must address the challenge of continually learning and retaining long-term knowledge. Many biological systems solve these challenges with episodic memory, which supports single-shot learning of instance-specific contexts."
- (d) **Dwarkesh Patel**, "Why I don't think AGI is right around the corner" (June 3, 2025): "LLMs don't get better over time the way a human would. This lack of continual learning is a huge, huge problem."
- (e) **Richard Sutton** (Dwarkesh Podcast, 2025) frames LLMs' inability to learn continually from experience as a dead end.

A neuroscience↔AI (two-way) convergence IS explicitly stated (e.g., "AI Meets Brain," arXiv:2512.23343, Dec 2025, integrating cognitive neuroscience with LLM agents and a "closed-loop lifecycle" of memory). **FLAG:** the philosophy-of-mind leg of the convergence is the weakest — no named philosopher of mind (Clark, Chalmers, Gallagher, Sprevak) was found explicitly arguing that the specific element LLMs lack is self-modifying memory loops. The full three-way convergence onto a single ingredient is therefore an editorial synthesis, not a directly attributable claim.

**1C.3 — Exograms (Donald, Sutton).** Merlin Donald, *Origins of the Modern Mind: Three Stages in the Evolution of Culture and Cognition* (Harvard University Press, 1991), introduces the **exogram** as an external memory record, contrasted with the internal **engram**. Donald's three transitions: mimetic culture (Homo erectus, motor representation), mythic culture (spoken language, Homo sapiens), and theoretic/technology-supported culture (external symbolic storage via reading/writing). Per Donald, exograms last longer than engrams, have greater capacity, are more easily transmissible across media and context, and can be retrieved/manipulated by a greater variety of means; "the modern mind is thus a hybrid structure built from vestiges of earlier biological stages as well as new external symbolic memory devices." John Sutton develops the exogram concept within the second-wave complementarity framework (Sutton 2010, above; also "The feel of the world: exograms, habits, and the confusion of types of memory," in *Philosophers on Memento*, 2009), noting exogram properties "are not fixed and might change depending on format and implementation." Related: Sutton, Harris, Keil & Barnier, "The psychology of memory, extended cognition, and socially distributed remembering," *Phenomenology and the Cognitive Sciences* 9(4):521–560 (2010).

**Definition cross-references.**
- *Parity principle* — first-wave test for cognitive extension based on functional equivalence (Clark & Chalmers 1998, p.8).
- *Complementarity principle* — second-wave: external (exogram) and internal (engram) resources differ in kind yet contribute jointly (Sutton 2010).
- *Diachronic constitution* — third-wave: constitution of cognition/consciousness is a temporally extended dependence relation, not synchronic (Kirchhoff 2015; Kirchhoff & Kiverstein 2019, 2020).
- *Cognitive offloading vs. uploading* — offloading places existing cognition into the world (Clark & Chalmers 1998); uploading creates novel cognitive functions taken on by the niche (Constant et al. 2020/2022).
- *Embodied generative model* — the organism's phenotype "entails" a probabilistic model of how sensory states are generated by external states, optimized by minimizing variational free energy (Friston 2010).

## Caveats
- Several quotations of the 1998 paper (parity principle, glue-and-trust criteria) are sourced via reliable secondary literature (Frontiers, Springer, PhilArchive, Wikipedia entries that quote the original) rather than the paywalled original *Analysis* text; wording is consistent across multiple independent sources.
- The *Mind & Language* "Extended active inference" paper carries both a 2020 (online-first) and 2022 (print volume 37(3)) date; both appear in citations and are noted here.
- TARGET 1C.2's tri-disciplinary convergence claim is the most diffuse/speculative item in this dive; the philosophy-of-mind leg is unverified as a directly attributable claim and is treated as editorial synthesis. The "closes operational loops across time" phrasing was not found verbatim in any source.
- Some retrieved AI-memory items are very recent preprints (2025–2026) that may not yet be peer-reviewed; "Nested Learning" is reported as also published at NeurIPS 2025, and several arXiv IDs (e.g., 2512.x, 2602.x, 2603.x) reflect late-2025/2026 submissions.
- Donald (1991) and Bartlett (1932) book-internal page citations beyond p.206 (Bartlett) were corroborated via secondary scholarly summaries rather than primary pagination.
