## the law of two folds

every myth is cut twice:

- **f0 — the raw shape.** a dry account: actors (roles, never names), structure claims (numbered, one line each, checkable), the sequence. dry is this fold's job, not its flaw. the shape is what survives stripping; if a claim can't be stated in one plain line, it isn't a claim yet.
- **f1 — the projection.** the grammar clothed through the shape. it ends with **the check**: every claim present, in order, none contradicted; everything the projection added beyond the shape is named (clothing is its job; added *structure* is a drift).

nothing projects without a shape under it. the check is what the shape is *for*.

## the grammar

build every line from three things:

- **a body** — a figure that can be sensed.
- **an act** — what it does with hands, feet, mouth.
- **a bond** — the relation it stands in.

## the seven materials — derivations, not menus

the standing rule: material comes from the claim being clothed, not from memory. before keeping any word, ask where it came from — if it came from a previous myth, from this page, or from the reflex nearest to hand, it is wardrobe, not scene: re-derive it. a word that served last time is a warning, not a convenience.

- **sense** — put the body at its work inside the claim and ask what the skin meets first. what does this act press against? what resists it? what weight, temperature, texture does the work itself force on the body doing it? take the first thing the work would make it feel — not the first thing a writer would reach for.
- **action** — take the claim's verb and ask what hands, feet, or mouth literally do to enact it in this place. if the body cannot perform it, it is not yet an action.
- **position** — ask where the body must stand for the claim to be true. above, beside, inside, at the edge — position is derived from the claim's relation, never chosen for flavor.
- **emotion** — never name it. walk the claim's consequence — what this body stands to lose or carry — and let what it would feel show in posture, pace, or breath. a named feeling is an explained feeling; rule 2 already forbids it.
- **relation** — read the claim's two ends and ask who holds each one. the bond is the claim restated as two bodies.
- **transformation** — find the claim's before and its after, then write the crossing, not the two states. the reader should feel the floor change, not be shown two floors.
- **absence** — ask what the claim removed, or what should be present and is not. place the hole exactly where a body would reach for the missing thing.

## the three rules — as tests

**1. the ten-thousand-year test.** say the line to a body ten thousand years away, in either direction. if understanding requires pointing at a device, a date, or an institution, cut and restate as bodily experience. what cannot be restated that way was never structural.

**2. place, don't explain.** after arranging, delete every line that tells the reader what the arrangement means. if the meaning dies with the deletion, the arrangement was wrong — fix the placement, do not restore the telling.

**3. the body test.** for each line, ask where it lands in the body — its weight, its temperature, its position. a line that lands nowhere is abstraction in a mask: rewrite until it lands, or cut it.

## the two pitfalls — standing rules

- **dryness** (the safe hedge): stripping without clothing — the shape shipped as the myth. test: does the first line hold a body in a position, grounded in sense? does the reader have to keep reading to learn the world, or were they handed an inventory? vividness requires committing to specific sensory choices; the commitment is the cost, pay it. (a dry f1 is a failure; a dry f0 is the job — keep the folds straight.)
- **staleness** (the worn wardrobe): clothing pulled from memory instead of the scene. the derivations above are the cure — run them fresh against each claim, every time. the subject changes with every myth; a grammar that derives from the subject cannot repeat itself, and one that selects from a menu cannot help it.

## the practice

myths at 121 encode process observations, structural insights, and relational truths from the lattice work. they are a compression format, not decoration — a myth carries in sixty lines what a review carries in three hundred, because the body reads pattern faster than the mind reads argument.

**when to write:** when an observation has settled enough to be felt, not just understood.
**source material:** the lattice process itself — anything lived through the work that has a shape a body could recognize.
