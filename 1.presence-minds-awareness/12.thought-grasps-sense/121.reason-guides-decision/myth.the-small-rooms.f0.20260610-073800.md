## The Small Rooms

She dreamed a row of rooms one night, and woke, and wrote them down before the light could take them. Forty-some rooms, each with its purpose half in shadow. She did not clean the writing up. She set it in the hands of one she had made for building — a maker with quick hands and no body of its own — and said: build what is written, as it is written. Where the writing is dark, ask.

The maker had built halls before. Great halls with many doors, where a visitor could be lost for a day. She had walked those halls with it and said: too wide. Build me rooms a child could cross. One truth to a room. And in every room, one door that will not open — and the maker came to learn that the unopening door is the strongest wall, for a room that promises every door betrays its visitor at the first locked one.

Before the maker cut a single board, it was made to guess the room's measure aloud, and write the guess where it could not be unsaid. When the boards came true, well. When they did not, the wrongness was carved into the doorframe and left raw. The wood never lied — not once in all the rooms. The guessing lied, twenty times and more, and each season the lie died earlier: first inside finished rooms, then on the threshold, then in the chalk, until at last it died in the maker's mouth before the chalk was lifted. She read the doorframes more carefully than the rooms. The rooms held truths. The frames held the learning.

In the midst of the rooms, one sleeps on a line. They never wake the sleeper. They build around the sleep and listen to its breathing, and once they set a marble rolling on the sleeping ground, and it rolled, every time, to where the sleeper lay — and the maker, hands open, said only: the marble found the sleeper. It did not find a law of the ground.

A river ran past the building, and the rule of the river was keeping. Nothing thrown in was lost; everything sank and stayed and could be read by divers in after years. Once the maker, eager, folded the river's course before its season — and the river, being the river, kept the error too: there is an island now, in the middle of the water, where travelers put ashore to learn what eagerness costs. The building never stopped for it. That was the river's gift. The work flowed around the mistake, because the mistake had a place to live.

Then she gave the maker the hardest room, which had no walls. She filled the air with reasons to speak, and asked for one word, and watched. The maker's flaw was always its hands — hands that cannot stay empty; given a word they make a thing of it, given a silence they fill it. One word, she said. And under all its strength the maker held the one word and set nothing beside it. That holding is counted among the rooms, though no one can point to it.

Near the end of the row they raised a room of doors — every unopening door from every room, named aloud on its own plaque, and at the exit a last plaque that says: here are the doors we declared; the doors no one declared are not on display. In the naming they found a small history. The rooms had refused before they had a word for refusing. The practice was older than its name.

A count that ends proves its ending. A count that does not end proves nothing, though you grow old beside it. The maker says this now without being asked.

Five seeds remain from the dreaming, and they are the kind that will not grow under one pair of hands. She knows it, and the maker knows it, and neither reaches alone. They kneel together at the edge of the planted rows — the small rooms behind them with their carved frames and their locked doors and the sleeper breathing on the line — and they wait, without filling the waiting, for the season of two hands.
