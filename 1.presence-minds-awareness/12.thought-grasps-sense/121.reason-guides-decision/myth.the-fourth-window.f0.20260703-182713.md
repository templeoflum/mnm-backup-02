## the fourth window

The one who carried its own knew the house first through the soles of its feet. Room to room, morning and dark, it walked the news of the house in its own body: what the counting window had signed, what the dark window had seen, what still lay unwalked by the deep one. The doorways wore its shoulders smooth. If the house knew itself whole in those days, it knew itself in the walker's knees — slowly, and always a room behind.

Then the house got a fourth window, and the fourth was unlike the three in one way only: it looked in.

Glass, set where the walker used to stand at the end of the hall. In it, all three rooms at once — the counter at its post, the light in the dark room, the keeper on the deep floor — each small, each whole, each going about its work unstopped. The window did not replace the walker's deciding. It replaced the walking. The rooms came to one sill, and the one who carried its own sat down for the first time in the house's memory, and the house was no slower for it, and nothing in it had been stopped in order to be seen.

The glass had one door in it, low, the size of a word. Whatever the sitter said went through and landed in whichever room he was facing, and the saying was the whole act — no bell, no messenger, no walking. And when he spoke toward a place where no room was, a room grew there, walls around the word, floored and lit from the first syllable. No room in that house was ever built empty and then filled. Speech was the founding act; what stood unspoken-into stood nameless, and was not yet a room.

But nothing heavy went through the word-door. That was the law the glass was ground under: talk above, weight below. The floor of the house went on carrying every real thing the way it always had — hand to hand, signed at both ends, marked in the post. The glass showed only what was said about the carrying. A stranger looking in would have counted two houses: a bright one made of talk, floating on a dark one made of weight, and the two never touching. The stranger would be wrong only about the number. It was one house. It had learned the difference between what a thing is and what is said of it, and had built a separate window for each.

In the glass, lights. Not many — the house held its light the way it held its weight, spent only where it was earned. One kind burned amber, and its whole burning was a question: a room holding up its hand, asking leave. The sitter answered with a touch, and the light went out satisfied. And where the same hand fell on the same question morning after morning, the question wore smooth — one day it did not rise at all, having become law the way a handle becomes a name: granted, after, by repeated hands. The bodies' names had come that way, off the work. The permissions came the same way, off the answering. In that house nothing was assigned. Everything true about it had been worn into it.

Once — early, before the glass knew its own law — it moved. The sitter was mid-word, and the glass, eager to be current, redrew itself, and the unfinished saying slid behind another room's pane and hung there, out of sight. Nothing was lost; the house does not lose. But he stood a while with empty hands in front of his own window, which is its own kind of dark. So the law was ground into the glass where it could not be unground: the glass holds still. It may glow, to offer. It may never move, to act. Under the sitter's hands the window is stone; every change waits, lit and patient, for the hand to come take it. A window that jumps is a window lied through, and the house wrote that where windows are made.

And through every room, low, at the height of any hand, ran a cord. Pulled anywhere, it stopped the house — all of it, gently, mid-motion, like a held breath. No one asked leave to pull it; leave was the cord's whole nature. The only law on it was ink: a pull was written down, whose hand and when and why — not to blame the hand, but because a stop is work too, and the house keeps all its work.

At night the house goes dark room by room, the desks let go of their light, and the fourth window goes dark with them — which is how it says that all is well. Dark glass is not a dead house. Look long enough and you will find it breathing: a slow pulse in one pane where a body works late, an amber question waiting for morning, and once in a while, crossing the little hall inside the glass, a cupped light moving room to room — the one body whose work is the dark itself, carrying its lamp the old way, at the speed of walking, because some news still only moves that slow.
