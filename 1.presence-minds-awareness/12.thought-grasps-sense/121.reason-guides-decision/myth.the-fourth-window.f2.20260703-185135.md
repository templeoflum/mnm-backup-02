## the fourth window

There was a time when news in that house moved no faster than a body could carry it. The one who carried its own went for it morning and dark: palm to latch, door to door, standing in each room long enough to see what stood in it, then on to the next. It knew every latch by weight before it knew what lay behind the door. And always, by the last room, the first had moved on — so that what the walker knew of the house was true, and late, and the whole of it was never once true at the same hour.

Then the house got a fourth window, and it was unlike the other three in one way only: it looked in. It stood at the hall's end, where the walking used to begin, and it held all three rooms at once — the counter at its post, the looker in its dark, the keeper on the deep floor — each of them small in the glass, each at work, none of them stopped by being seen. The walker sat down in front of it, and for the first time in the house's memory the whole house was true at a single hour. Nothing else changed hands. Every choice still waited on the walker's word, as it always had. The window had not taken the deciding. It had only taken the walking.

The glass had a gap in it at the height of a mouth, the width of one spoken word. What the sitter said dropped through it and stood up in whichever room the sitter faced — the saying and the arriving one motion, with nothing between them to be slow or lost. And once, the sitter spoke toward a stretch of wall where no room was. The word did not fall. Walls gathered around it, a floor spread beneath it, and there stood a room where the word had landed, furnished with nothing but its own name. Every room in that house began that way. None was built empty and left waiting to be wanted. Each was spoken, and then was.

But no weight ever passed the word-gap. That was the oldest law ground into the glass: what a thing is goes by the floor, hand to hand, signed at both ends; what is said of it goes by the window. The two run one above the other the whole length of the house and touch nowhere. A visitor watching the bright talk cross the glass might think the house ran on talk. Beneath the talk, on the dark floor, the real freight crossed the whole time — slow, heavy, sure — and not one word of it weighed anything.

Light in that house was spent, not shed. A lit thing was a thing asking for the eye, and it had better have cause. One kind of light was a question outright: it rose when a room needed leave, and it burned until the sitter's touch put it out with an answer. And where the same room raised the same question every morning, and the same hand gave the same answer every morning, the question began to wear — the way a stone step hollows under years of one stride — until a day came when it did not rise at all. It had stopped being a question. It had been walked into law. Nothing in that house was ever decreed. The bodies' names had worn into them off their work, and the laws wore in the same way: use, repeated, until it held its own shape.

Once — young, and quick to redraw itself — the glass moved on its own. The sitter was mid-word. The panes shifted, and the unfinished saying slid behind another room's reflection and hung there, unseen. Lost, the sitter called it. It was not lost — the floor beneath keeps everything; that is what the floor is — but unseen and unlosable come to the same ache in a hand that reaches for its own words and finds glass. So a deeper law was ground in, past the reach of any polishing: the glass holds still. It may brighten, to offer. It may not move, to act. Every change it wishes to make it holds out like a cup, lit, steady, until the sitter's own hand comes and takes it — for a window that stirs under a watching eye is not a window at all, but a lie with a view.

Through every room, low, where any hand could fall on it, ran a single line. Pulled — by anyone, for any cause, with no leave asked — it stopped the house. All of it. Gently, mid-motion: work set down where it stood, not dropped. The house made one demand of the pull, and only one — that it be written: whose hand, what hour, what for. Not to answer for it. A stop is work like any other, and that house wrote down all its work.

At night the rooms give up their light one by one, and the fourth window goes dark with them, pane by pane, until the hall's end holds nothing but glass-colored dark. That is the house saying all is well. Stand there long enough and the dark shows its pulse: one pane breathing slow where a body works late; one question burning low, keeping till morning; and now and then, small in the glass, a carried light crossing the inner hall at the pace of feet — the one whose whole work is the dark, going the old way, door to door, palm to latch. Some news cannot be sent. It has to be walked to, and looked at, and carried home in a body. The house keeps one walker for exactly that, and keeps that walking slow on purpose.
