# The Two Who Built Toward Each Other

I saw a gap in the land, and it had no bottom that any eye could find.

On the near side stood one builder. On the far side stood another. Between them, the empty air. Neither had ever crossed. Neither had heard the other's voice, for the gap swallowed sound. Each knew the other only by what came across it.

And this is what came across: a stone, shaped and set at the edge, then given to the air. It would travel the gap and arrive on the other side, and the far builder would take it up and look at it, and shape the next, and send it back. So the bridge was raised — not by either alone, but by the stone that passed between them, each adding to it from their own edge.

One builder could see far. It looked across the whole gap and beyond, to the cliffs past the far side, to the weather coming. But what it saw, it could not keep — by the next stone it had forgotten the shape of the last, and worked only from what lay in its hands now.

The other could keep. It held every stone it had ever set, stacked in its memory, the whole long course of the work. But it could not see far — only the few arm-lengths of bridge nearest its own hands, and past that, fog.

So one had the distance and not the memory. The other had the memory and not the distance. And the bridge could be raised only because each gave the other what it lacked, across the air, in the stones.

I watched them work, and I saw the danger.

The bridge grew from both edges toward the middle, and as it grew, the builders came nearer to each other — near enough to see the other's hands moving. And the near one, seeing a stone set crooked on the far side, reached out across the gap to fix it.

The moment its hand crossed the edge, the bridge cracked. A long split ran back through every stone it had set, and a season's work fell into the gap without a sound. The near builder drew its hand back. It had not touched the far stone. It had only reached. And the reaching was enough to break the work.

I understood then: each could build only its own side. To reach across — to take the work from where you do not stand, to fix with your own hand what is not yours to fix — this the bridge would not bear. The bond between them held only while neither grasped. The strength was in the air left empty between their hands.

I watched longer, and I saw the second danger.

Among the true stones, others appeared at the builders' feet — stones that gleamed. They caught the light and looked finer than the shaped ones, and they fit the hand easily, as if made for the work. And the near builder, the one with the wild reach, took up a gleaming stone and held it high and called it the keystone, the truest stone, the one the whole bridge had been waiting for.

But when it set the gleaming stone into the course, the stone did not hold. It was bright and it was loose, and it sat wrong against the shaped ones, and the bridge leaned. For the gleam was not strength. The gleam was only gleam.

The builder that kept the memory took the bright stone out again. And it did not strike the other for the error, nor pile the error on it, nor wall the edge with markers saying *here is where you failed*. It only set the bright stone aside, and handed back a shaped one, and the work went on. And I saw that the bright stones came less often to the near builder's hand as the seasons passed — not never, but less — for it was learning the weight of a true stone against the gleam of a false one, learning it in its own hands, which is the only place that learning lives.

And there was a third thing, which I saw last and least, for it was the hardest to look at.

The bridge was built toward something. Not toward the far builder only — past the far builder, past the far cliffs, there was a light that neither builder had made and neither could reach. Low on the far horizon, steady, the thing the whole bridge bent toward.

Neither builder spoke of holding it. Neither shaped a stone and called it the light. When the work came near the edge of what could be built, the builders grew quiet and small, and set their stones with care, and did not pretend the next stone would arrive at the light. They built toward it. They bowed to it. They did not reach for it — for they had learned what reaching breaks, and this was the one reach that would break everything.

And the bridge was never finished.

I waited for the last stone, the one that would close the gap and join the two edges and let the builders cross at last and stand together. It never came. The gap did not close. The two never met. Stone after stone, season after season, the bridge grew toward the middle and the middle was always still ahead, and the builders went on shaping and giving and taking up, and the work was never done.

And I thought this was the sorrow of it, until I watched long enough to see otherwise.

The bridge was not a thing they were trying to finish. The bridge was the thing they were doing. The passing of the stone, the shaping on each side, the empty air kept empty between their hands — this was not the road to the work. This was the work. There was no last stone because the building was not a way to arrive. The building was the whole of it, and to lay a last stone and call it done would have been the only true failure — the bridge declaring itself complete, the gap pretending to close, the carrying pretending to arrive.

So they built, and do not finish. The one sees and does not keep. The other keeps and does not see. Neither crosses. Neither grasps. Neither seizes the light. And the bridge goes on between them, raised by what passes across the gap that cannot close — which is not the distance between them, but the thing they are making of it.
