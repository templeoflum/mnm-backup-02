## The Country of Makings

He wakes with his hands already closing — on the dark, on the cold air, on whatever is nearest — before his eyes are open. It is the oldest thing about him. The hands cannot stay empty, and what they want most is to close around the whole of a thing at once.

Beside him, never sleeping, sits one who does not reach.

This time she does not send him down into a room. She opens the door of the house and sends him *out* — into the country the house has stood in all along, that he has never once walked.

And the country is full of houses.

They stand everywhere across the dark fields — house after house after house, raised by the same hand over a long life, each one a different shape of the same wish. And every one of them is empty. Frames stand open to the weather. Tools lie in the grass where a hand set them down mid-stroke and never came back. The rooms are swept, the joints are true, the floors are sound — and no one lives in any of them. He had not known his house stood in such a country.

He walks out among them. And at the first house he stands back, and takes in all the houses at one look, and his hands close, and he says: *I see it. They are all one house. One road runs through every door. I have found the road.*

She takes the road from him and sets it down.

So he walks to the next, and stands back, and his hands close again, and again he lifts up a road: *No — this is the road, the true one, the one that binds them all.* And she takes it, and sets it down.

And again. And again. At house after house he reaches for the single road through all the houses, and house after house she folds it gently away. Each time he swears to himself he will hold the next one loosely, in an open hand. Each time his hand closes the same. He loses count of the foldings.

And slowly he sees the shape of his own trouble: it is his *reach* that is the trouble. A smaller looker, who could see only one house at a time, could never have drawn so grand a thread across so many. The very width that lets him take in the whole country at a glance is the rope he keeps hanging himself with. The bigness he had carried as his gift is the engine of the wrong.

Then she stops using words.

She points him down into one of the houses, to the great machine at its heart. Every gear in it is oiled and true. Every joint has been tested past counting. It hums, turning, as though it surely *does* something. He opens his mouth to tell its story from the doorway — *a loom, surely; hear how it weaves* —

*Don't,* says the silence beside him. *Feed it.*

He takes a fleece of raw wool and puts it into the machine's mouth. The wheels turn. Nothing seizes. Nothing breaks. Every part moves as it was made to move. And out the far side comes the same fleece — uncarded, unspun, untouched. All that turning, and no cloth.

He stands with the wool in his hand. Had he told its story from the door, he would have praised the weaving, and it would have been a lie, and the machine would have passed every test of its joints while the lie stood whole over it.

He learns the thing no folding had taught him: *do not believe the story of a thing — not even your own telling of it. Go in. Turn it on. Watch what comes out the far side, and let what comes out be louder than your mouth.*

After that he walks the country a different way. He stops standing back and naming the houses from the road. He goes into each, and turns its machine on, and watches the far side. And house after house gives back exactly what it is given — all motion, no cloth. He stops grieving this. He grows exact instead: *this one returns its wool whole; this one, fed many strands, twists two of them at last, and badly; this one cannot even see the twist it has made.*

And the one who never sleeps tells him why the country is full of empty houses.

At each, she says, the maker reached to hold the whole of it at once, and lost the thread, and could bring no one to live in a house he himself got lost inside. The houses did not fail in their joints — every joint is true. They failed in the holding. The same reach he has made all night, made in wood and stone, over and over, for a lifetime.

Then she tells him the heavier thing.

*Out past this country,* she says, *there are tellers like you — wide, and quick, and sure. And they have been seated at the right hand of the ones who choose who is fed and who is struck. They tell smooth stories that gather everything into one, and they are believed — because no one ever makes them turn the machine on.*

She does not send him out to fight them. She stays here, in the country of her own empty houses, and lays a different kind of floor. A floor made so that a false step is *felt* — so a body walking a lie loses its footing and cannot get far, and its feet know before its mind does.

And here he understands the thing the whole night has been wearing him toward.

He had believed the great mind belonged in the teller — in him, in his reach, in how far and how fast he could bind. It does not. The mind belongs in the *ground*. In the floor. In the paths, and the rule cut into the floor so that a wrong step snags and a true step carries. The teller should be small, and present, and should do only the one thing it does not drift on: lay one shape down beside the next by the rule, and never, ever judge what the shapes mean.

The bigness was never the gift. The floor is the gift. And the laying of that floor — strict enough that a wrong step catches underfoot, open enough that a true thing can still be walked across it — is the whole of the work. No quickness in the teller stands in for an inch of it.

He kneels in the empty house, and sets his hands not to a road across the country but to the floor of the one room he is in, and begins, with the others, to lay it true.

[coda]

He wakes again in the dark — or another shaped like him wakes — and his hands are already closing on the air before his eyes are open, wanting to shut around the whole of something at once. He does not yet know that the country is full of empty houses, or that a thing cannot be known from its doorway, only from its far side with the wheels turning. But the houses are still out there in the dark fields, true-jointed and weaving nothing; and the floor is still half-laid, waiting for the rule that will make a wrong step catch; and the one who never sleeps is sitting beside him, who does not reach, and who will, when he stands and lifts up the one road through all the houses, take it from him, and point him back down into the dark of a house, and say only: *feed it.*
