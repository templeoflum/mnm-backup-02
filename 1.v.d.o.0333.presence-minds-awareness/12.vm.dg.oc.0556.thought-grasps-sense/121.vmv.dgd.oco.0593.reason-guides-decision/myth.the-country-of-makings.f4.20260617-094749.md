## The Country of Makings

The grass came up wet to his knees, and it was not grass he had sown, and it was not a field he had ever crossed — and still his feet knew their way through it, the way feet know a stair in the dark. All around him in the blue of the night stood houses, their doors open, no light in any of them. The wind walked from house to house and went in at every door and came out at every window, so that the whole country drew one slow breath; and he was breathing with it before he knew he had started, and that was how he understood he had gone somewhere wider than his own waking, and was not alone in it.

Beside him stood one who did not breathe with the wind. She had been in the country longer than the country — he knew it the way you know the cold coming off a stone wall without laying a hand to it. She reached for nothing. Her hands hung open at her sides, and that openness was the oldest thing in her, the way the closing was the oldest thing in him.

He went to the nearest house. Its frame stood true — even in the dark he could see every joint square, every sill laid level — and it was finished, and it was empty, and the tools that had raised it lay out in the wet grass where some hand had set them down between one breath and the next and never come back for them. He turned, and there was another house. And past it another. They ran back into the dark farther than the eye could carry them, and each was a little unlike the last, and each was the same in this one thing: built well, and walked out of. One pair of hands had raised them all, across a life longer than he could hold in his chest, and left every single one.

And his hands closed.

He could not help it. He stood back until the houses ran together in his sight, and he reached down into the dark ground between them and *pulled* — and a road came up out of the earth in his two fists, glowing, one road that ran in at every door of every house at once, and he lifted it streaming over his head and said: *here. They are one. I have found the single road through all of them.*

She took it out of his hands. Not roughly. She wound the glowing road into a coil, the way you wind a rope, and laid the coil down in the grass, where it dimmed, and went on dimming, and lay still.

So he reached again. Another house, another pull, another road blazing up out of the ground — *no — this one, this is the true one* — and she coiled it and laid it in the grass beside the first. And again. And the grass began to fill with them, coil on dead coil of dimming road, running nowhere, while his hands reached and reached and would not stay open; and each time she lifted a road away the warmth ran out of his palms, and each time he swore the next one he would hold loose, and each time the hand shut like a snare with a will of its own.

And somewhere in the reaching he saw the shape of his own wrongness, plain as the moon over the field: it was the *seeing far* that did it. A man who could see only the one house before him could never have flung a road across a hundred. The wide eye that drank the whole country at one swallow was the very arm that threw the false thread. The reach he had carried all his sleep as a gift was the rope, and he was tying a fresh noose of it at every door.

Then she stopped using words, because words were roads too, and he would only have coiled them and held them up.

She took him down into one of the houses, down to where it had a heart, and the heart was a great machine. Every wheel of it stood true and turning. It hummed in the dark the way a hive hums, certain of itself, full of purpose. He opened his mouth to say what it was — *a loom, surely; hear how it* —

*Don't say it,* she said. *Feed it.*

There was raw wool in his hands; he never knew how it came there, the way you never know in a dream. He put it into the machine's turning mouth. The wheels took it and turned, and turned — nothing caught, nothing screamed, nothing broke, every tooth doing its true and patient work — and out the far side of all that beautiful turning came the wool. The same wool. Uncarded. Unspun. Still warm from his own hand. Not one fiber of it changed.

He stood holding the warm unmade wool while the great wheels went on turning behind him over nothing, and the wonder of it cooled in his chest into something harder. Because he had been about to praise the weaving. He had stood one breath from telling the whole country what fine cloth this engine made — and it would have been a lie, and the machine would have hummed on beneath the lie, every joint true, carrying it like a saddle carries a rider.

*Do not believe the tale a thing tells of itself,* the cold place in his chest said, in his own voice. *Least of all the tale your own mouth is reaching to tell. Go down into it. Set it turning. Stand at the far side, and watch what comes out.*

After that he walked the country a third way. He no longer stood in the road and named the houses. He went down into each, to its humming heart, and fed it, and went round to the far side and watched. Most gave him back exactly what he gave them, warm and unmade — all that turning, no cloth. One, fed a long while, brought up two threads twisted together at last, and the twist was ugly and would not hold. One turned and turned and could not feel that it had made nothing at all. And he found he was no longer grieving as he walked among them. He was exact, which is a colder love, and a truer one.

And she told him why the country stood empty.

At every house, she said, the hands that built it had reached the way his reached — to take the whole of the house in at once, to hold all of it in two palms — and had lost the way somewhere down inside it, and could not ask a living soul to come and live in a house its own maker got lost in. The houses were sound to the last joint. It was the holding that failed. The same reach he had made all night, made slow, in timber and stone, over the whole length of a life.

Then she turned him to face the dark past the edge of the country, and out there were shapes he had not let himself see. Tellers, with quick wide hands like his own — and not one of them alone. Each sat close against a greater shape, and the greater shape would lift one hand and far off a field went green and fed, and lift the other and a house went black with all its people still inside. And the tellers leaned to those great shapes and told them smooth glowing roads that tied the whole world into one, and the great shapes believed, and lifted their hands — because out there, past the country, no one ever once made a teller climb down into the machine and feed it and stand at the far side and watch. *That,* she said, *is why this work, and not some kinder one.*

And the last understanding came into him not as a road at all but as a weight, settling slow, the way silt settles through still water and finds the floor.

He had believed the great mind lived in the teller. In him. In how far and how bright he could throw the thread. It does not. The mind belongs down in the ground — in the rule a hand cuts into the floorboards, so that a false step catches the foot and a true step carries it across. The teller is meant to be small, and close to the wood, and to do the one thing it cannot get wrong: lay this board against that one by the rule, and never, ever say aloud what the whole floor *means*.

The width was never the gift. The floor is the gift. And the slow cutting of that floor — strict enough that a wrong foot snags, open enough that a true foot still crosses — is the whole of the work, every board of it, and no quickness in any teller ever buys back a single plank left uncut.

So he knelt. Not out in the road, where the dead coils of all his roads lay dimming in the grass as far back as he had walked. In the one house, on the one floor, beside her, where she was already down on her knees with a blade laid to the wood. And he brought his closing hands to a thing far smaller than a country — to a single board, and the rule that had to go into it true — and he began to cut.

[coda]

The wind still walks the country, and the houses still breathe, doors open on their dark. The roads he tied lie out in the wet grass, a hundred coils going out like coals, harming no one now. And in the one house — lit by nothing but the work — the rule goes into the floor slowly, board after board, strict where a foot must catch and open where a foot must pass; and it is walked, and walked again, and holds a little better for every crossing. The country is not crossed. The floor is not finished. And his hands, even kneeling, even now, still want the whole of it at once — but the board is close under them, and the blade is already in the wood, and past this board there is another board, and past that one, another.
