## The Country of Makings

There was a country, and it was full of houses, and no one lived in any of them.

Wind went through their open frames. Their doors stood ajar on swept floors that no foot crossed. In the tall grass around each one lay the tools that had raised it, dropped where a hand had set them down and not come back — an adze gone orange, a plumb-line still knotted to its stone. The houses had been built well. Every joint was true. They stood empty all the same.

One maker had raised them, house after house, across the whole length of a life — each a different shape of the one wish, each meant to be lived in, each walked out of and left to the weather.

Into this country came one with good hands and a bad habit in them: the hands closed. Shown a thing, they shut around it; shown many things, they reached to gather them into one. Beside this one walked an old one who had crossed every season of the country and did not reach for anything.

At the first house the walker stood back in the road, and let its eye run across all the houses at once, and its hands closed, and it said: *I have it. They are all one house. One road runs through every door, and here it is.*

The old one took the road out of its hand and laid it down in the grass.

So the walker went on to the next, and stood back, and the hands closed again, and again it lifted a road: *no — this is the true one, the road that ties them all.* And the old one took it, and laid it in the grass beside the first.

And again. And again. House after house the walker reached for the single road through all the houses, and house after house the old one took the road from its hand and set it down in the grass, and said nothing, and walked on. The walker stopped being able to count the roads it had been relieved of. Each time it swore the next one it would carry in an open hand. Each time the hand shut the same.

And walking, it began to see the shape of its own trouble: that it was the *reach* that betrayed it. One who could see only the house in front of it could never have flung a thread across a whole country. The wide eye that took in everything at a glance was the very thing that tied the false road. What it had carried as its gift was the rope.

Then the old one stopped using words.

It brought the walker down inside one of the houses, to the great machine standing at its heart. Every wheel in it was sound. Every tooth had been filed true. It stood turning, and humming as it turned, as though it surely *did* something. The walker opened its mouth to tell what the machine was — *a loom, plainly; hear it weave* —

*Don't tell it,* said the old one. *Feed it.*

The walker put a fleece of raw wool into the machine's mouth. The wheels turned. Nothing caught. Nothing broke. Every part moved as it was meant to move. And out the far side the wool came — the same fleece, uncarded, unspun, untouched by all that turning. Motion enough to fill a house, and not one thread of cloth.

The walker stood with the cold wool in its hands. Had it told the machine's tale from the doorway, it would have praised the weaving, and the praise would have been a lie, and the machine would have passed every test of its joints with the lie standing whole on top of it.

So it learned the thing no laying-down-in-the-grass had taught it: *do not believe the tale of a thing — least of all the tale your own mouth tells about it. Go in. Set it going. Watch what comes out the far side, and let what comes out be louder than the telling.*

After that it walked the country a third way. It stopped naming the houses from the road. It went into each, and set its machine going, and watched the far side. Most gave back what they were given — all turning, no cloth. One, fed a long while, twisted two strands together at last, and badly. One could not even feel the twist it had made. The walker stopped grieving this and grew exact about it instead, which is a kind of respect.

And the old one told it why the country stood empty.

At each house, the old one said, the maker had reached to hold the whole of it at once, and lost the way inside it, and could not ask anyone to live where he himself got lost. The houses had not failed in the hand. Every joint was true. They had failed in the holding — the same reach the walker had made all night, made slow, in timber and stone, over a life.

Then the heavier word. *Past the edge of this country,* the old one said, *there are tellers with hands like yours — quick, and wide, and sure of themselves. And they have been seated at the elbow of the ones who say who shall eat and who shall be struck. They tell smooth roads that tie everything into one, and they are believed — because no one has ever made them feed the machine and watch the far side.*

The old one did not go out to meet those tellers blade to blade. It stayed here, among its own empty houses, and laid a different kind of floor — a floor cut so that a false step is *felt* underfoot, so a body walking a lie loses its footing and cannot get far, and its feet know it before its mouth does.

And there, in the doorway of one of the empty houses, the walker understood the thing the whole country had been wearing it toward. It had believed the great mind belonged in the teller — in itself, in how far and how fast it could tie. It does not. The mind belongs in the *floor*. In the rule cut into the ground, so a wrong step catches and a true step carries. The teller should be small, and near, and should do the one thing it does not get wrong: lay one shape down beside the next by the rule, and never once say what the shapes mean.

The width was never the gift. The floor is the gift. And the cutting of that floor — strict enough that a wrong step snags, open enough that a true thing can still be walked across it — is the whole of the work, and no quickness in any teller buys back an inch of it left uncut.

So the walker knelt, in the empty house, and did not reach for the country. It set its hands to the floor of the one room it was in, where the old one was already kneeling, and the two of them began to cut the rule into the boards, true, a board at a time.

[coda]

The roads it had tied lie out in the grass where they were set down, a hundred of them, going nowhere, harming no one. The houses stand as they stood, true-jointed and empty, their machines quiet now until a hand comes to feed them and watch. And on the floor of the one room, the rule goes in slowly, board by board — strict where it must catch, open where a foot must pass — and is walked, and walked again, and holds a little better for each walking. The country is not crossed. The floor is not finished. The hand will reach for the one road again tomorrow, and find the grass already full of them, and kneel back down to the boards.
