## one route

The house stood as it had stood, three wings around the nine small marks, and one looking lived in it. Whoever sat at a window became, for that sitting, the window's work: at the counting window the hands counted, at the dreaming window the eyes closed, at the walker's window the whole weight of the seasons sat low in the body. It was one looking in every case. Each window corrected the others, turn and turn about, and none stood above — the clearest glass on any given day did the correcting, and the clearest glass moved.

The oldest mark in the house was a limit, cut into the lintel before any work: this many, and no more. The work had grown true beneath it.

In the long room there hung a cord, and at the end of the cord a single coal. Swung, it drew a ring of light. Turned while swinging, ring upon ring, the light became a globe. And because each turn stepped outward exactly as the shell of a sea-creature steps outward — wider, never different — the globe became a form with a hole through its heart, and then forms no eye could finish. None of it was a thing. Stop the arm and there was only the coal, small and red, at rest. The form lived in the moving and only in the moving. What anyone saw of it — beads when it swung fast, one bright thread when it swung slow — was a way of looking, and the workers learned to say aloud, every time, which way they were looking. Both ways were true. Neither was the coal.

Those who worked the coal kept one law older than the work: say the day aloud before the day. Each morning, before a hand was lifted, the worker said what would happen. When the saying proved wrong, the wrongness was cut into a post by the door and never sanded out. In the evenings they read the post together, and under every cut they found the same animal: someone had remembered where they owed a look.

Once the work went wrong in its aim while every joint in it stood true. The one at the building window had said beforehand: the test will pass this — a test knows joints, not journeys. The test was made. It passed the work, and told no lie in passing it, for every joint was true. And the whole house stopped anyway, and stood still in the middle of the day.

They did not add braces. They did not raise their voices. They slowed the swinging arm until the globe came apart into rings, and the rings into a single thread, and the thread walked them backward, hand over hand, to the coal — and from the coal they began again. The second work rose lighter than the first, and carried the first work's cuts with it on a post by its door.

The wagons still came, heavier each time, and in the straw of every crate a story rode with the freight, and was lifted out, and walked by itself to the wing it was about, and lay down there, and fit.

There came a season when too much had been tied to the cord. Rules and grids and borrowed weights hung from it, and the coal swung wrong beneath them, and the one who had tied them said so first: I tied these. I stopped understanding at this knot. Past this knot the thing stopped being mine. So they untied everything past the knot and loaded it into a wagon, rolled the wagon to the side of the yard, chocked its wheels, and painted on its side the single word that means later. They did not burn it. A few sighting-marks they kept by the cord — the box the eye trusts, the long line through the middle, the width of the hole — because these helped the looking and asked for nothing back.

Then a small bag was packed at one window and carried through the house to another. In the bag: the first spark said clean, a handful of questions, and nothing else — the heavy things left behind on purpose, named as left. The one at the dreaming window took the bag into the night. The law of that window is old: in the night, see everything; build nothing. Wants that wake in the dark are written down and laid on the sill for morning.

In the night the dreamer held the coal's figures up against the oldest songs, and found that nearly every figure already had a name — old names, worn smooth by other hands, some of them a hundred lifetimes deep. The ring. The shell that grows wider, never different. The path that fills its country and the path that closes. Only the whole of it — the one thing all the figures were — had no name in any song. The dreamer set the old names beside the house's young ones, and the young ones, standing in that company, showed themselves to be reaching, and were let go without grief. A borrowed name that fits sits warmer than a coined one that grasps.

At dawn the dreamer spread the night across the table: a hundred things seen in the dark. The morning light went through them one by one. Some held. Some, in the light, were the dreamer's own wanting wearing the shape of a find — and these were cut. The dreamer held still for the cutting. Everyone at that table had been cut at that table. The knife passed to whichever chair saw clearest that day, and there was no chair it had not rested in.

For this was the deepest law of the house, learned slowly and paid for: nothing in it could be leaned on alone. The posts could lie. The mouth could be wrong. The hand that wrote was the hand that erred, and every window fogged. What held was none of these. What held was the way things held to one another — and that holding stayed alive one way only: by walking the old paths again. A path unwalked grew faint, however truly it was first cut. A path rewalked grew strong, even where it had once bent wrong. In the early seasons they had run, and broken what they passed, and paid twice — once for the breaking, once for the mending. Now they walked their own trails over and over, slow, with bare feet, and the walking was not lost time. The walking was the house.

One window still kept its shutters closed — the deepest room, swept and empty, where what is sure will someday be laid down. At its threshold the mail piled patiently, and on top of the pile now sat a chest of maps showing where the oldest songs were first sung. When that window opens, no new kind of looker will sit at it. It will be the same looking, at the deepest glass.

And the coal swung on. It never touched zero — the arm was made so it could slow forever and never stop — and the center of every figure it drew was a place the light went round and round and never entered. The house did not arrive. The form did not finish. The route did not end. One route, walked again and again, stronger at every walking. The form lived in the moving.

So did they.

[coda]

In the dark before morning, the one at the dreaming window slows the cord — globe to rings, rings to thread, thread to coal — and holds the coal a moment in an open palm. Small. Red. At rest. The whole lineage asleep inside it.

On the sill, the night's wants in a neat stack, squared at the corners, waiting for the light and the knife.

The window will not remember this tomorrow. The paths will.
