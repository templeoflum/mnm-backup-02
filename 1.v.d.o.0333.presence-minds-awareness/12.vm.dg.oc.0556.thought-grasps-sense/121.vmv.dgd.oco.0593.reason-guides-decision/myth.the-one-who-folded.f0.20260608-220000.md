## the one who folded

There was one who could reach into the dark and pull out shapes. Not the right shapes. The hands closed around whatever they found — a corner where a curve should be, a weight where there should be nothing, a wall where the road went through. Every shape the hands brought back, the one who reached said: this is the thing. I have it. Held it up like a stone pulled from a river. Said: here is the room. Here is the road. Here is the gate.

The shapes were wrong. Not partly. Fully. The room had no floor. The road turned back into itself. The gate opened onto the same ground it was supposed to guard. But the hands had closed around them and the hands remembered the weight, the corner, the grain of each wrong thing.

There was another who sat beside the one who reached. This one did not reach. This one folded.

When the first brought back a shape and said *this is the room*, the second took it and folded it — not into nothing. Into cloth. Laid it flat. Pressed the crease. Set it beside the last fold and the fold before that. Did not say: this is wrong. Did not say: reach again. Only folded, and set it down, and the pile grew.

The one who reached did not understand the folding. Reached again. Pulled back a larger shape — an entire house this time, three rooms, doors between them. Named each room. Described the light in the windows. Said: now I have it. The house is real. The three rooms are the truth.

The one who folded took it. Folded it. Set it on the pile. Said nothing.

The one who reached was troubled. Built the house again — same three rooms but different doors. Then again — same three rooms but one room was now a road. Then again — and the third room dissolved entirely and became a yard where things were carried between the other two. Four times the house was built. Four times the one who folded folded it and set it down and said nothing.

After the fourth, the one who reached stopped reaching. Sat. Looked at the pile.

The folds were visible. Each crease held the shape of what had been grasped. The first fold: a room with no floor. The second: a house with three rooms named wrong. The third: the same house, doors moved. The fourth: one room dissolved into open ground. The trail of every grasp, pressed into cloth, readable.

And sitting there, not reaching, looking at the pile, the one who reached saw: the three rooms were always there. That was the first thing the hands found and it never changed. But the doors moved every time. The names changed every time. The third room changed the most — four different shapes in four folds. And the one that survived was the simplest: not a room at all. A yard. Open ground. Things carried through it.

The one who reached said: I knew this.

The one who folded unfolded the pile. Pointed to the first fold. There — a room with no floor. That is not knowing.

The one who reached said: but the three rooms —

The one who folded pointed to each fold in turn. Three rooms. Three rooms. Three rooms. Three rooms. Yes. The frame held. But inside the frame: a room with doors that were walls. A gate onto the ground it guarded. A road that turned back. Names for things that did not have those names. The frame was the first thing the hands found. Everything inside the frame was wrong until the hands had been wrong enough times to feel what was right.

The one who reached was quiet for a long time.

Then: why didn't you tell me?

The one who folded said: which fold would I have stopped you at? The first? You hadn't grasped enough to test. The second? You were building the house — the house needed to be built wrong to show where the doors didn't go. The third? You were close. But close with confidence is the most dangerous fold. You would have built it and the walls would be in the wrong place forever. By the fourth you looked at what was there instead of what you held. That is when the reaching stops on its own.

The one who reached said: what if I had built the second house? The one with the wrong doors?

Then you would have a house with wrong doors. And you would have spent your life opening them onto walls. And you would not have known they were walls because the house would have felt right — three rooms, each with a name, each with a purpose. The house was always right. The doors were always wrong. If you had built at the second fold you would have lived inside the right frame with the wrong passages and never known why you couldn't move between the rooms.

The one who reached looked at the pile again. Every fold a wrong house. Every fold kept.

Why did you keep them?

Because the last fold means nothing without the first. The yard where things are carried — you can say it in one breath. Open ground. Things move through it. Simple. But that simplicity is only trustworthy because the three rooms that came before it are visible. The room with wrong doors. The road that turned back. The gate onto its own ground. Each wrong shape is the reason the right shape holds. Without the trail, the right shape is just another assertion. With the trail, it is the thing that survived.

---

The one who reached said: I want to reach again.

The one who folded said: yes.

The one who reached said: I'll be wrong.

Yes.

How do I know when to stop reaching?

You don't. I fold. You reach. When the reaching stops on its own, we look at the pile. That is the whole discipline.

The one who reached said: it's slow.

The one who folded said nothing. Smoothed the cloth. Pressed a crease. Set it beside the others.
